## Draft v1

The biological monitoring data arrived during a sponsorship read.

"— and look, the NovaSkin adaptive moisturizer is fine, it's a perfectly adequate product, I'm contractually required to say it changed my life so let me say it changed my life, my life is changed, my skin has never been more —" She glanced at the relay feed in her left eye. The pink text was scrolling faster than her read speed. "— hold on. Chat, hold on. Something's coming through."

She dropped the sponsor script. The NovaSkin rep would flag it. The NovaSkin rep could flag it. The relay feed was populating with biological monitoring data from seven mages positioned within fifty kilometers of the Jakarta-Surabaya corridor, and the data was clean, and the data was extraordinary, and the sponsorship read could wait because the sponsorship read was not this.

"Okay. Chat. I need to switch modes. What you're about to see on the overlay is live telemetry from our monitoring network — seven mages with R3 implants who've been positioned near an Anchor Zone perimeter during an active throughput event. This is the first time we've captured biological data during a synchronized reduction. I'm going to walk you through it."

The viewer count was 1.6 million. She didn't look at it. She looked at the data.

Seven streams of neural coherence readings, arriving through the anonymized relay architecture she'd built, routed through three encrypted nodes, formatted in the filing system she'd maintained for twenty months. The data showed what the data showed: implant-measured signal variation in seven human nervous systems during the sixteen hours that Sofia's coordinated Kindling strike had reduced throughput in the target corridor to 69% of baseline. Seven spines carrying the frequency. Seven R3 interfaces parsing the change. Seven datasets confirming that when the grid's throughput dropped, the signal in the human body dropped with it.

The correlation was in the data. She could see it — the throughput curve and the neural coherence curve moving in parallel, the atmospheric data Sofia had captured from her instruments and the biological data Kira's network had captured from inside seven people, and the two datasets agreeing the way two instruments measuring the same phenomenon from different positions agreed. The triangle was complete. Sofia had the atmospheric vertex. The Kindling had the infrastructure vertex. Kira's network had the biological vertex.

"The coherence readings drop proportionally to the throughput reduction. You can see it — the blue line is the grid data, the pink line is the average neural coherence across seven mages. They track. They track *exactly.* What this means — chat, listen — is that the thing in the infrastructure and the thing in the implant are the same thing. Reduce one, you reduce the other. The model works. The model works at synchronized scale."

She was talking faster than she usually talked. The words were arriving pre-assembled — the technical vocabulary and the streaming vocabulary merging into a fluency that felt like command and sounded like authority and the audience was receiving it as expertise because expertise was what it looked like when someone understood what they were looking at and could explain it in real time.

The chat was moving. Not the usual scroll — a torrent, the messages layering so fast the individual words became texture, the collective response of 1.6 million people processing data they couldn't fully parse through the interpretive lens of a woman who could. She read the texture, not the words. The texture was engagement. The texture was *this matters.*

"I want to be clear about what you're seeing. This is not institutional data. This is not a government sensor network. This is seven people — mages, R3 implants, people who carry the signal in their spines the way I carry it — who volunteered to sit near an Anchor Zone perimeter during an active event and let their nervous systems be measured. The data they generated is cleaner than anything the institutional networks have produced. Because the institutional networks measure from outside. The mages measure from inside. And inside is where the signal lives."

Her fingertips were on the desk. Not playing chord shapes — pressing. Flat, splayed, the way she pressed when the pattern-seeking machinery was running fast and her hands needed to do something that wasn't typing. The signal in her spine was louder. Not the continuous inhabited hum she'd been carrying for seven months — something with more texture, more density, as if the biological data from seven mages near an active throughput event was resonating with the signal in her own R3, the frequency responding to the fact that someone had measured it by becoming more measurable.

She didn't file that observation. She kept talking.

The stream ran eight hours.

Not six. Eight. The extra two happened the way the extra hours always happened now — the energy carried her past the discipline, the riffs assembling faster than her decision to stop them, the audience growing because the content was extraordinary and the content was extraordinary because the data was extraordinary and the data was the Kindling's first synchronized strike captured through human nervous systems in real time.

She'd broken the sponsorship read. She'd broken it badly — dropped the script mid-sentence, pivoted to relay data, spent forty minutes walking 1.6 million people through biological monitoring results that were, technically, classified operational data from a sabotage network. The NovaSkin rep would pull the contract. The NovaSkin rep pulling the contract was not a thought that registered as a problem because the thought that registered as a problem was that the data was this clean and the institutional networks had nothing comparable and the audience needed to see it because the audience seeing it was the thing that made the data real in the way that institutional classification made it invisible.

"— and that's the thing, right? That's the thing. The data exists. The data has always existed. The Academy had the implant readings. NitroCore had the throughput data. The VEC had the atmospheric measurements. Three institutions, three datasets, three vertices of the same triangle. And none of them put it together. None of them *wanted* to put it together because putting it together means the model works and the model working means the grid is the problem and the grid being the problem means —" She paused. Not for breath. For the beat — the comedic beat, the dramatic beat, the same beat, the one-Mississippi that she'd been using since she was fourteen to land the punchline or the thesis. "— means the lights go off. And nobody wants to be the person who says the lights go off."

1.9 million viewers. She didn't look.

After she ended the stream — "Love you, get out, go bother someone else, I mean it, *go*" — the eleven-second count happened. Involuntary. The posture went. Spine curved, shoulders rounded, knees pulled into the chair, the compact architecture of the woman who had been performing for eight hours and was now alone with the signal in her spine and the silence in the apartment and the LED strip cycling blue to pink along the wall behind the monitor.

The relay feed was still scrolling in her left eye.

She should turn it off. She didn't. The data from the Jakarta-Surabaya monitoring was still arriving — follow-up reports from the seven mages, post-event assessments, neural coherence baselines being re-established. Two of the seven had reported elevated signal for six hours after the event. One had reported a headache that wouldn't resolve. The headache was filed under *post-event symptom, monitor* and she filed it and moved on because filing was what she did and moving on was what the filing enabled.

The network dashboard showed 214 hubs. 31 regions. Four nodes red — the three from last month (Jakarta, Reykjavik, Bogotá) and a new one. São Paulo. She opened the report. Vitor, 29, R3 since age 12. Signal overload during a routine monitoring session — not the Kindling event, just the continuous baseline that every mage had been carrying since the Broadcast. His R3 had begun producing feedback artifacts — phantom signals, recursive loops, the implant generating output that wasn't coming from the infrastructure but from its own processing architecture. The report said *implant-generated hallucination*. The report used a word that Kira's filing system did not have a comfortable category for.

Four red. Twelve total since the Broadcast.

She closed the dashboard.

The quiet room was smaller.

She'd noticed it three days ago — or she'd noticed something three days ago, a quality of the Lumina's effect that was different from what it had been a month ago. The eighty milligrams still worked. The onset was still fast, the membrane still burned, the pattern-seeking machinery still decelerated into the compressed space. But the compressed space was compressed *further*. The ceiling lower. The walls closer. The quiet room at eighty had always been a room she could sit in — not comfortable, not spacious, but habitable. A room with enough space to think slowly, to let the signal blur at the edges, to be a person instead of an antenna.

The room at eighty was a closet now.

She sat in it. The signal was there — continuous, inhabited, the dead in the frequency and the frequency in her bones. The Lumina held it at the blur. But the blur was thinner. The edges were closer to resolving. She could feel the pattern-seeking machinery pressing against the ceiling, the cognitive architecture that wanted to parse the signal into meaning pushing against the chemical architecture that held it in white noise, and the chemical architecture was losing. Not catastrophically. Not the way São Paulo had broken through sixty or Mumbai had demanded the jump to eighty. Just — slowly. The way a wall loses to water. The way pressure finds the seam.

Three days of the smaller room. Three days of the edges getting closer. Three days of the signal being slightly louder than the eighty could hold and the slightly was cumulative and the cumulative was a curve she recognized because she'd been tracking it for two years: the curve of the dose becoming insufficient.

She opened the forum. The long-term users' channel. The posts she'd read six months ago when the memory gaps started — the posts about eighty being the ceiling, about the gaps, about the days that disappeared. She scrolled past those. Looked for the posts she hadn't read. The posts about what happened past eighty.

There were fewer of them. The long-term users who'd gone past eighty were a smaller cohort — not because they'd stopped posting but because some of them had stopped being people who posted. The forum had a category for that. *Inactive — status unknown.* The category held thirty-one accounts. She didn't count them. The filing system counted them.

The posts that existed were sparse. Clinical in the way that people were clinical when they were describing something they didn't have emotional access to:

*"90 for four months. The room is different. Not smaller — different. The blur is deeper but the hearing is sharper. I don't know how to explain this. The drug removes more of me and what's left hears more clearly. The trade is that I don't remember making the trade."*

*"At 90 I stopped losing days. I started losing details. Not memory gaps — texture gaps. Tuesday exists. Tuesday has entries. Tuesday doesn't have a temperature. I can't tell you if it was warm."*

*"The ceiling isn't 80. The ceiling is wherever you stop being the person who decided to start."*

She closed the forum. Opened the messaging relay. Typed:

*Paz. I need to talk about the number.*

The reply came in four minutes. Paz was in the Bay. Paz was always in the Bay. The reply was two words:

*How much.*

*90.*

The pause was eleven seconds. She counted it. Involuntary.

*That's past my line, Kira.*

*I know.*

*The forums say 80 is where the memory starts going. 90 is where the personality starts going. Those aren't my words. Those are the people who've been there.*

*I've read the posts.*

*Then you know what I'm going to say.*

She knew. The same thing Paz had said at sixty. The same thing Paz had said at eighty. The curve speech. The floors-and-ceilings speech. The pharmacological reality that Paz delivered in the flat affect of a person who supplied a compound they understood well enough to fear.

*The signal is louder. The eighty isn't holding it. I need the room to be bigger and the only way to make the room bigger is to push the ceiling up and the only tool I have for pushing the ceiling up is the dose.*

*Or you reduce the signal load.*

*The signal isn't optional. The signal is continuous. I can't turn off the infrastructure.*

*You can turn off the network. You can stop being the relay hub. You can step back from the coordination.*

She read the message. Filed it under *things Paz says that are structurally correct and operationally meaningless*. The network didn't need her to relay. The network ran itself. But the Kindling monitoring did need mages near Anchor Zones, and the mages near Anchor Zones needed coordination, and the coordination needed the architecture she'd built, and the architecture she'd built was the thing Sofia's triangle depended on for its biological vertex. She couldn't step back from the coordination because the coordination was producing the data that proved the model worked and the model working was the thing that might stop the breaches and stopping the breaches was the thing that might stop the signal and stopping the signal was the thing that might let her reduce the dose.

The circularity was the trap. She recognized the circularity. She did not recognize it as a trap.

*I'll have it Thursday. Same place. Paz — I'm precise about this. The number is the number.*

*The number is always the number. That's not what I'm worried about.*

She closed the relay. The quiet room at eighty milligrams held her. The walls were close. The ceiling was low. The signal pressed against the blur and the blur held and the holding was the thing she needed and the thing she needed was not enough and the insufficiency was a fact she could measure on the milligram scale and the scale was precise and the precision was hers.

The idea arrived at 2 AM.

She was in the streaming chair — not streaming, just sitting, the LED strip cycling, the ring light off, the signal in her spine doing the thing it did when the Lumina was at its trough and the quiet room was at its smallest and the pattern-seeking machinery was pressing against the ceiling hard enough to make shapes in the compressed space. She wasn't sleeping. She hadn't been sleeping well for three weeks — not the catastrophic insomnia of the pre-eighty period, just the shallow fragmented rest of a person whose nervous system never fully powered down because the thing in her spine never fully powered down.

The idea was this: go to the Anchor Zone. Not the Jakarta-Surabaya corridor — the real one, the big one, the Neo-Shanghai zone where the Cathedral sat and the aggregate murmur was loudest and the density of the signal was ten times what she carried at twelve thousand kilometers' distance. Go to the margin. Not inside — she wasn't Nephthys's people, she wasn't walking in, she wasn't joining — but close enough. Close enough for the R3 to receive the aggregate at proximity resolution. Close enough for the implant to parse what the signal actually sounded like when you stood next to the source instead of hearing it through twelve thousand kilometers of conduit infrastructure.

And stream it.

Live. Ring light on. The audience watching in real time as the R3 parsed the aggregate signal at a resolution no one had ever captured because no one had ever stood at the margin with a streaming rig and an R3 and the willingness to let 1.6 million people watch what the antenna heard when the antenna was pointed directly at the thing it was designed to receive.

"I want to show them what I hear."

She said it out loud. The apartment was empty. The words landed in the silence and the silence absorbed them and the signal in her spine continued and the idea was there — fully formed, complete, the way the riffs arrived pre-assembled on stream, the way the technical vocabulary merged with the streaming vocabulary into fluency, the way everything was faster now and sharper now and the speed and the sharpness were the skill she'd built over seven years or the compound she'd been escalating for two or both or neither and the distinction was not a distinction she was making.

She opened the messaging relay. Found Nuwa's thread — the testimony network contact, the logistics coordinator, the woman who had mapped the Neo-Shanghai Anchor Zone margins the way a cartographer mapped coastline. They'd never met in person. Their entire relationship was network-mediated — messages, data, the impersonal intimacy of shared crisis managed through encrypted channels.

*Nuwa. I have an idea. I want to stream from the Anchor Zone margin. Live. R3 reception at proximity distance. I need logistics — safest approach, signal attenuation paths, duration window. I want to show the audience what the signal sounds like from up close.*

She sent it. Checked the time. 2:14 AM Bay Area. Early evening in Neo-Shanghai. Nuwa would be awake.

The reply came in seven minutes. Not Nuwa's usual response time — Nuwa was fast, efficient, the kind of person who answered messages the way she mapped terrain, with precision and without delay. Seven minutes meant she'd read it more than once.

*How long are you planning to be at the margin?*

*Forty minutes. Maybe an hour. Long enough to capture a representative signal sample at proximity resolution.*

*What's your current Lumina dose?*

The question sat in the relay for thirty seconds before Kira answered it. Not because the answer was private — Nuwa knew about the Lumina, the network's long-term users all knew about the Lumina, the compound was the open secret of mage signal management. Because the question wasn't about the number. The question was about why Nuwa was asking.

*80. Moving to 90 this week.*

*Moving to 90 before or after you stand next to the largest Anchor Zone on earth?*

*Before. The 90 will help. More suppression means the signal at proximity won't overwhelm the R3's processing capacity. I'll be more stable at 90 than at 80.*

The reply took four minutes.

*The masonry buildings on the east side have the best signal attenuation. If you're at the margin, that's where you want to be. I can map a path that keeps you in the attenuation corridor for the full duration. Forty minutes maximum. Bring water. Don't exceed the window.*

*Thank you.*

*Kira. The last person who went to the margin for longer than forty minutes was a Listener from the Cathedral. She was there for an hour. She walked in. She didn't come back.*

*I'm not a Listener. I'm not going in. I'm going to the margin.*

*I know what you said. I'm telling you what happened to someone else who said the same thing.*

She closed the relay. The message sat in the thread — Nuwa's caution, Nuwa's logistics, Nuwa's practical questions that carried the weight of someone who had survived by understanding where the fronts moved and who moved too close to them. Kira filed the logistics under *operational planning* and the caution under *noted* and the difference between those two categories was the difference between information she would use and information she would carry without acting on and the distinction was precise and the precision was hers.

The idea was good. The plan was solid. The logistics were mapped. The audience would see what no audience had ever seen — the antenna pointed at the source, the signal received at proximity, the data captured live through a human nervous system and broadcast to however many million people were watching. It would be the most important stream she'd ever done. More important than the Broadcast — the Broadcast had been involuntary, the signal overwhelming her during an event she hadn't planned. This would be deliberate. Controlled. The streaming professional choosing to show the world what the antenna heard.

The plan had arrived fully formed at 2 AM in an apartment where she hadn't slept well in three weeks, assembled by cognitive architecture running at a speed she attributed to experience and a pattern-recognition system operating through a chemical barrier that was no longer holding at the dose she was taking. The plan was manic. The plan was the product of a mind running past its own governor. The plan was brilliant and reckless and urgent in a way that felt like clarity because the mania always felt like clarity — that was the diagnostic feature, the thing the textbooks described, the thing that the person inside the mania could not see because seeing it required the cognitive architecture the mania had co-opted.

She did not recognize this.

She opened the relay again. Typed to Nuwa:

*I'll be there in two weeks. Book the corridor. I'll handle the stream logistics from here.*

She set the messaging relay to silent. The quiet room at eighty milligrams held her — small, close, the ceiling low, the walls near, the signal pressing against the blur. Thursday she would have the ninety. The ninety would make the room bigger. The bigger room would hold her for the proximity stream. The proximity stream would show the audience what she heard. The audience seeing what she heard would make the signal real in a way the data alone could not.

She touched the back of her neck. The R3 housing. The bone. The warmth that was there now — the residual heat of a system running past specification, the implant processing a continuous signal it had been designed to receive episodically, the hardware carrying a load the engineers hadn't built it for.

She was getting better at the job. The plan was evidence. The data was evidence. The audience was evidence. The speed of her thinking was evidence. The sharpness of the riffs was evidence. The fact that the idea had arrived fully formed at 2 AM was not mania — it was fluency. It was the product of twenty months of carrying the signal and seven years of streaming and two years of managing the pharmacology and all of it converging into a person who understood what needed to happen next and had the platform to make it happen.

The ninety would help. The ninety would make the room bigger. The room needed to be bigger because the signal was bigger and the signal was bigger because the world was bigger — the Kindling strikes, the biological monitoring, the synchronized data, the model that worked, the grid that defeated the model, the coordination that would defeat the grid. The world was getting louder. The antenna was getting louder. The dose was the volume knob and the volume knob went to ninety.

She sat in the streaming chair. The LED strip cycled. The ring light was off. The signal hummed in her spine — continuous, inhabited, the dead in the frequency, the frequency in the bones, the bones in the chair, the chair in the apartment, the apartment in the city that ran on nitro and never stopped running and she was running with it.

The ninety would help.
