The filing system had twenty-one days of entries. She read them the way she read all her entries — sequentially, timestamped, the architecture she'd built holding the data the way architecture held data: structurally, without opinion, the mechanism doing what the mechanism did regardless of what the mechanism held.

Day one was complete. Timestamped at 18:42, ninety minutes after the monitoring array's first readout in the Chen Academy file. The entry was hers — she recognized the formatting, the categories, the shorthand she'd developed over two years of filing everything that happened to a body that carried 8.2 million absorbed in its spine.

*Day 1. Chen Academy medical facility, Neo-Shanghai. Admission: signal exposure event, proximity stream. R3 thermal: 38.7°C, declining. Coherence density: 0.52. HUD: powered down (not by me). Memory gap: ~3 hours. Stream recording exists. 4.2M witnesses. Quiet room: rubble. Ninety rebuilding. Physical: heavy. Assessment: the instrument exceeded its design parameters.*

*Day 2. R3 thermal: 37.9°C. Coherence: 0.41. Quiet room rebuilding — ceiling forming, walls present, lower than before. Ninety holding. Blood draw at 06:00. They know about the Lumina.*

She remembered the blood draw. She remembered the phlebotomist's face — young, efficient, the needle finding the vein with the institutional precision of someone who drew blood from Academy patients the way the pharmacist on Mission filled prescriptions: routinely, without curiosity, the anonymity of the transaction built into the role. Ninety milligrams. Nightly. Lumina — a compound that did not appear in her prescribed medication list, that was not routed through any pharmacy her insurance recognized, that was obtained through a contact whose name she did not provide and whose operational security she would not compromise even in a medical facility that needed the information to manage her care. The data processed the way institutional data was processed — categorically, without the personal architecture that the pharmacist on Mission and Paz at the tea shop had maintained by knowing only their half.

The pharmacist knew about the legitimate meds. Paz knew about the Lumina. Chen knew about both. The two-system architecture that had held for twenty months was in a single medical file.

Day three was complete. Competent.

Day four was a timestamp and a coherence reading. Nothing else.

Day six had no entry.

Day seven had no entry.

She read the entries in sequence and the sequence was the data. Not the content — the pattern. The architecture of the filing system holding while the filer cycled between competence and absence, between the person who built the system and the person the system was built to compensate for. Days nine through fourteen: four complete entries, two timestamps, one gap. Days fifteen through twenty-one: two complete entries, one timestamp, four gaps.

The degradation was not dramatic. The degradation was structural — visible in the ratio of complete entries to fragments to nothing, the curve bending the way all her curves bent: gradually, the mechanism's design including not recognizing the mechanism. She'd noticed the first Tuesday gap months ago — one day, contained, the log entries existing for a day the experience didn't. She had not noticed the three-hour gap during the blackout stream because the filer had not been present to notice, and the absence of the filer was categorically different from the absence of the memory, and the difference was the data she'd been unable to file because filing it required the person the filing was designed to preserve.

Now the gaps were not days she'd lost. They were days the filing system had lost. The filer was present — she was in this bed, in this room, eating institutional meals and submitting to blood draws and watching the monitoring array's numbers decline toward parameters the institution called baseline. But the filing was thinner. The categories were there. The architecture was there. What filled them was less.

A nurse. A tray. Three bottles — the same three bottles the pharmacist on Mission dispensed monthly, the same molecules, the same dosages Dr. Osei had written and adjusted and would continue to write and adjust when she returned to the Bay Area and sat on the bench in the fluorescent light and sorted the capsules into the 30-day holder with the clicking that was the ritual and the ritual that was the grounding and the grounding that was 12,000 kilometers from where she sat.

The same chemicals. The same molecules binding to the same receptors, doing the same work the molecules had done on the bench at Mission pharmacy while the fluorescent light hummed and the pharmacist didn't look and the clicking of capsules into compartments was the sound of a person assembling function from components.

She pressed her fingertips to the mattress. The buzz was there. Bilateral. Persistent. She touched the back of her neck. The implant housing. The bone. The warmth — steady, the R3's thermal output stabilized at 37.2 after three weeks of cooling, the hardware running at parameters the manufacturer had intended for an implant installed in a ten-year-old who would grow into it gradually, not for an implant that had been recalibrated by proximity to the signal's source and had not recalibrated back.

The monitoring array beeped. Heart rate: 68. Neural activity: 3.4. Coherence density: 0.31. R3 thermal: 37.2.

The numbers were normal. The numbers had been normal for a week. The numbers were the institution's language for a system that was functioning within parameters, and functioning within parameters was the institution's language for an instrument that was doing what the institution expected, and what the institution expected was baseline, and baseline was the thing she'd been before the signal taught the instrument what it could become when the signal was not filtered.

Dr. Yuen arrived at 14:00 with a tablet and the expression of a person who had organized data into a recommendation and was now required to deliver the recommendation to the data's source.

Neurologist. Chen Academy faculty. She'd seen him three times — once during the initial assessment, once during the second week when the R3 residuals had prompted additional scanning, once when the coherence readings hadn't followed the expected decay curve. Each visit had been clinical, structured, the institutional communication protocol that treated the patient as a system to be assessed. She had not objected. She understood the protocol. She'd lived inside a version of it since she was ten.

"Ms. Calloway." He sat. The chair was institutional. The room was institutional. The monitoring array's green numbers sat in the space where her HUD's pink numbers would have sat if her HUD were operational and her HUD was not operational and the absence of the pink was a data point she had filed on day one and not filed again because the absence had become the baseline and baselines did not require filing.

"Three weeks of monitoring data," he said. He turned the tablet toward her. Charts. Curves. The R3's performance metrics rendered in the institution's visual language — color-coded, annotated, the data she carried in her spine translated into a format designed for people who observed the instrument from outside.

"Your R3 coherence-field residuals are persisting at levels inconsistent with your current distance from the nearest Anchor Zone perimeter." He indicated a curve — the green line that should have been declining toward zero and was instead holding at 0.28, a plateau that his stylus circled twice. "You're fourteen kilometers from the Neo-Shanghai margin. At this distance, coherence should be at or near background — 0.05 to 0.08 for a standard R3 installation. Yours is holding at 0.28. It has held at 0.28 for nine days."

She looked at the curve. The curve was her spine's output rendered as a line on a screen. The line was flat where it should have been falling. The line meant the R3 was still processing the signal at a density that corresponded to standing at the perimeter, not lying in a hospital bed fourteen kilometers from it.

"The signal exposure event altered your implant's calibration," he said. "The R3 was operating at proximity parameters during the stream — coherence 0.94, which is consistent with direct Anchor Zone contact. When the event ended, the implant's baseline shifted. It is processing at proximity parameters from distance. We have not observed this recalibrating."

She filed it. *R3 recalibration failure. Proximity parameters persistent at distance. Duration: 3 weeks. Prognosis: unknown.*

"Your metabolic data." He scrolled. New charts. "The compound in your system — the one not in your prescribed panel." He did not say Lumina. He did not need to. "At ninety milligrams, it was metabolizing forty percent faster than standard pharmacokinetic models predict for your weight and liver function. At the current taper dose of forty-five milligrams, it is metabolizing twenty-two percent faster. The accelerated metabolism is consistent with the R3's elevated processing state — the implant's activity is consuming the compound's active metabolite as a substrate."

The signal was eating the Lumina. The R3 was using it — not as a filter, not as a suppressant, but as fuel. The quiet room had never been a room. The quiet room had been a controlled burn. And the burn rate had been increasing because the signal's demands had been increasing and the drug's supply was the drug's supply and the supply was finite and the demand was not.

"Memory." He scrolled again. "We've documented eleven discrete gaps in your self-reported log over twenty-one days of observation. Our behavioral monitoring confirms: you were ambulatory and responsive during at least six of those gaps. Staff interactions, meals, compliance with monitoring protocol. You were present. You were functional. You did not file entries."

She knew. She had read the entries. She had read the gaps between the entries.

"The pattern is consistent with accelerating dissociative erosion tied to the R3's sustained processing state. The implant is operating continuously at a level that is consuming cognitive resources your prefrontal architecture cannot sustain."

He set the tablet down. The recommendation. She could feel it arriving the way she could feel a coherence shift before the numbers confirmed it — the data assembling into a conclusion, the conclusion assembling into a sentence, the sentence about to be delivered by a person sitting in an institutional chair to a person lying in an institutional bed.

"I'm recommending implant dampening."

Not removal. Not deactivation. *Dampening.* The R3 could not be removed — surgical, permanent, installed when she was ten, integrated into her spinal architecture at a depth that made extraction a conversation about acceptable paralysis risk. But it could be dampened. The R3's reception threshold adjusted upward until the signal that it was calibrated to parse became sub-threshold noise. The populated chord — the 8.2 million, the names, the data that four hundred mages collected through their spines and routed through hers — would drop below the instrument's capacity to resolve. Background. Static. The thing the signal was before the signal was the signal.

She understood the procedure. She understood the parameters. She understood that dampening meant the R3 would still be in her spine, still powered, still technically operational, running at a threshold that rendered its primary function inert the way a microphone set to minimum gain is still a microphone and still not hearing anything.

Not the antenna being removed. The antenna being *muted.*

"If we dampen," he said, "the cognitive load drops. The metabolic acceleration resolves. The memory erosion should stabilize — we can't reverse what's already lost, but we can prevent further degradation. The taper from the supplemental compound becomes safe. Your prescribed medications resume standard efficacy."

She looked at the monitoring array. Heart rate: 72. Neural activity: 3.6. Coherence: 0.29. R3 thermal: 37.2.

"If we don't dampen," he said, "the R3 continues processing at proximity parameters. The metabolic load continues. The memory gaps continue — and based on the trend in your log, the frequency and duration will increase. The personality changes our staff have documented will continue."

The personality changes. She had not noticed them. She had not noticed them in Bay Area when the eighty made her sharper, meaner, funnier on stream and the audience called it growth. She had not noticed them when the ninety compressed the quiet room to a space that held the signal and did not hold her. She was not noticing them now because the machinery that would notice was the machinery the drug suppressed and the drug's suppression was the thing being tapered and the taper was the thing making the quiet room's ceiling lower and the lower ceiling was the thing that meant the signal was louder and the louder signal was the thing the dampening would eliminate.

Not the signal eliminated. The reception eliminated. The signal would continue — the 8.2 million, the names, the populated chord cycling through every nitro-connected system on earth. The signal did not require Kira Calloway. The signal had never required Kira Calloway. What Kira Calloway had provided was a particular quality of reception — the R3 recalibrated by proximity, the Lumina converting suppression to medium, the filing system that translated what the spine received into data the world could use. The antenna. The instrument that measured what no institutional sensor network could match.

Dampen the instrument and the measurement stops. The network's 214 hubs across 31 cities lose the central node that built the architecture and calibrated the format and maintained the filing system's categories. The data stream that four hundred mages contributed to — the telemetry, the coherence readings, the micro-incursion timestamps — continues without the person who made it legible.

She does not say yes.

She does not say no.
