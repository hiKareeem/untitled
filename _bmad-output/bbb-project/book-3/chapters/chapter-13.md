## Draft v1

The data from the first wave arrived in twelve streams, and by the sixth hour every stream confirmed the same thing: the model worked and the grid defeated it.

Sofia spread the printouts across the desk — the same desk, the same fourteenth-floor safe house in the lower Mid-Levels, three districts south of the VEC tower she would never enter again. Twelve facilities. Three continents. The largest coordinated Kindling action in the campaign's seven-month history, executed within a four-hour window by operatives who understood the grid because they had built the grid, and the atmospheric data from every target sector showed the same pattern the Jakarta-Surabaya proof of concept had shown three weeks earlier: throughput down, coherence down, breach activity down. The correlation holding across every axis at every scale the coordination could produce.

Global throughput had dropped 9%. Not the 31% the model required for zero expansion — the 31% that would exceed the grid's rerouting capacity and produce the sustained reduction the proof of concept had demonstrated at the local level. Nine percent. Enough to register. Enough to measure. Enough to confirm that twelve simultaneous shutdowns across three continents produced a proportional global response in the atmospheric data and a proportional reduction in Anchor Zone activity across every monitored perimeter.

The Anchor Zones had quieted. Not stopped — quieted. The expansion rates had decreased proportionally to the throughput reduction, the same linear correlation operating at global scale that had operated at sector scale in Jakarta. The aggregate murmur — the structured signal that the mage network monitored through biological telemetry and that Sofia's field kit measured through atmospheric instruments — had dropped in amplitude across all thirty-one monitored regions. For six hours, the world's void infrastructure had operated at 91% of baseline, and the void had responded exactly as the model predicted.

Then the grid recovered. Emergency protocols overriding the distributed load-balancing systems. Throughput climbing back to baseline across every sector within six hours. The Anchor Zones resuming. The expansion rates returning to their pre-strike trajectories. The aggregate murmur rising to its previous amplitude.

Six hours. The Jakarta proof of concept had produced sixteen hours at the local level. Twelve facilities across three continents had produced six hours at the global level. The grid's emergency protocols were faster than its automated redistribution — designed for cascading failure scenarios, not coordinated action, but effective against both.

She marked it on the printout. Red marker: *12 facilities. 3 continents. 9% global reduction. 6-hour window. Grid recovery via emergency protocols. The emergency systems are faster than the standard redistribution.*

And underneath, blue: *To exceed emergency protocol capacity, the coordination must be larger. Faster. More simultaneous. The grid's emergency tolerance is higher than its redistribution tolerance. The 18% band applies to redistribution. Emergency protocols can handle more.*

She set the marker down. Looked at the field kit on the edge of the desk. Green indicator. The 0.7 Hz signal through the floor — steady, the Neo-Shanghai baseline, the hum that had not changed in the six hours the rest of the world's signal had dropped because Neo-Shanghai's conduit infrastructure had not been targeted in the first wave. The local frequency, unchanged, while the global frequency shifted around it.

The field kit did not know the difference between a successful strike and a failed one. The field kit measured. The numbers resolved. The model was correct and the grid had defeated it and the field kit pulsed green in the dark of the safe house and the numbers were the numbers.

---

The encrypted relay connected at 04:00, fourteen hours after the grid's recovery.

Karaboué first. His feed routed through the Mombasa-Singapore-Seychelles chain, the twelve-hour lag compressed to nine through the secondary node. His voice carried the same compression artifacts it always carried — the relay chain's acoustic signature, the sound of distance processed through infrastructure.

"The thermal data confirms," he said. "Every target sector showed the same conduit temperature response. Four to six degrees drop within the first hour of shutdown. The throughput-heat correlation held across all twelve sites. The recovery thermal signature matches Jakarta — same ramp profile, same timeline."

"Six hours," Sofia said. "Not sixteen."

"Emergency protocols. The grid's cascading-failure response is faster than the load-balancing redistribution. Different system. Different threshold."

"Different threshold," she repeated. She was writing while he spoke — red marker on the thermal data printout, the numbers from his feed correlating with the numbers from her atmospheric instruments. The correlation was the correlation. It held because physics held. "What's the emergency threshold?"

"Higher than 18%. My estimate from the thermal data — the emergency systems engaged at approximately 12% global reduction and had the grid stabilized within six hours. The redistribution systems in Jakarta engaged at the local equivalent of 8% and took thirty-six hours. The emergency system is faster because it's designed for faster."

She wrote: *Emergency protocol engagement: ~12% global. Recovery: 6 hours. Redistribution engagement: ~8% local equivalent. Recovery: 36 hours. To defeat emergency protocols, coordinated reduction must exceed emergency capacity — estimated 25-30% global simultaneous.*

"The number keeps going up," she said.

Karaboué's silence on the relay was the silence of a conduit thermodynamics specialist who had run the same calculation. "The number was always going to go up. The grid is designed to survive. That is what grids do."

---

The working group convened on the relay at 06:00.

Fourteen nodes connected across six time zones. The Erasure List — forty-one researchers, fourteen countries, the suppressed and marginalized and reassigned scientists who had been removed from their institutions for producing data the institutions could not process — and the Kindling cell commanders who translated scientific data into operational plans. Sofia could see the relay indicators on the display: green dots distributed across a schematic of the global grid, each dot representing a node, each node representing a person or a cell, each cell representing people who had agreed to shut down the infrastructure their communities depended on because the alternative was the void consuming everything.

She presented the first-wave analysis. The data. The numbers. The correlation confirmed at global scale. The six-hour window. The grid recovery. The emergency protocol threshold. She presented it the way she presented all data — without qualification, without interpretation beyond what the numbers supported, without the institutional framing that would have required her to acknowledge uncertainty she did not feel. The numbers were the numbers. The model was the model. The grid was the grid.

"The first wave confirmed the model at global scale," she said. "Nine percent reduction. Proportional breach response. The correlation holds. The emergency protocols recovered the grid in six hours. To produce a sustained reduction — the kind that stops expansion, not the kind that slows it — we need to exceed the emergency protocol capacity."

She paused. The relay hummed. Fourteen nodes listening.

"Karaboué's thermal analysis estimates the emergency threshold at approximately 25 to 30 percent global simultaneous reduction. The redistribution threshold is 18 percent. The emergency threshold is higher because the system is designed for cascading failure — it assumes failures are random, not coordinated. But it compensates faster."

"How many facilities?" The voice was a cell commander — South American corridor, the relay routing through São Paulo. Sofia did not know his name. She knew his operational code and his grid sector and the throughput data from his region.

"The first wave was twelve facilities across three continents. Nine percent. To reach 25 to 30 percent, we need approximately thirty-five to forty facilities in a coordinated window of two hours or less. The window has to be tighter than the first wave because the emergency protocols engage faster than redistribution."

Silence on the relay. Not dropped connections. Processing.

"Forty facilities," someone said. A different node — the relay indicator showed Southeast Asian corridor, the same grid infrastructure where the Jakarta proof of concept had operated. "We have the operational capacity for forty. The Kindling network has grown since the first wave. The proof of concept recruited. The first wave recruited more."

"Operational capacity is not the constraint," Sofia said. "The constraint is the dependency mapping."

---

She brought up the maps.

Not grid schematics — those were the Kindling's operational documents, the infrastructure diagrams that showed processing facilities and conduit routes and load-balancing nodes. The maps Sofia brought up were different. She had built them over the past three weeks from public infrastructure data, humanitarian databases, and the ground-truth reporting that the mage network provided from thirty-one regions. They were dependency maps. They showed what happened to the populations served by each grid node when that node went offline.

The Gulf corridor first. Three desalination plants powered by the nitro grid — the plants that converted seawater to freshwater for 2.1 million people in the coastal settlements that had grown around the post-BLACKWEIR reconstruction zones. The desalination plants ran on the same conduit infrastructure that carried the void's structured pulse. The same throughput. The same grid. Shut down the processing facilities that fed the Gulf corridor's conduit network and the desalination plants lost power.

She did not say *2.1 million people lose access to freshwater.* She said: "Gulf corridor. Three desalination plants. Grid-dependent. No backup generation capacity — the plants were built post-BLACKWEIR on the assumption of continuous nitro supply. Shutdown duration of six hours produces manageable water reserves depletion. Shutdown duration exceeding twenty-four hours produces critical shortfall."

The relay was quiet.

"South American hospital network." She moved to the second map. "Fourteen hospitals in the São Paulo-Rio corridor that draw primary power from the nitro grid. Backup generators present at eleven of fourteen — rated for seventy-two hours. Three hospitals lack backup generation entirely. Grid shutdown in the São Paulo corridor produces immediate power loss at three facilities and backup-dependent operation at eleven."

"Which three?" The São Paulo cell commander.

"Santa Casa de Misericórdia regional annex. Hospital Municipal de Guarulhos. Centro Médico de Baixada Fluminense." She read the names from the dependency map. She had looked them up. She had mapped their grid connections and their backup capacity and their patient census data from the public health databases that still published because the public health infrastructure still functioned because the grid still functioned. "Combined patient census: approximately four thousand. ICU beds: two hundred and twelve. Ventilator-dependent patients: thirty-one."

She heard the number land on the relay. Thirty-one ventilator-dependent patients in three hospitals without backup generators in a grid sector she was planning to shut down.

"Southeast Asian manufacturing corridor." The third map. "The Jakarta-Surabaya-Semarang zone — same infrastructure as the proof of concept. Manufacturing zones serving approximately 340,000 workers. Residential power for 1.2 million. The proof of concept demonstrated eleven hours of manufacturing shutdown and hospital backup activation in the adjacent sector from a three-facility strike. A targeted strike in this corridor for the second wave would affect a larger zone."

She stopped. The maps were on the display. The dependency data was in the relay. The numbers were the numbers — the same phrase she used for atmospheric data and throughput correlations and breach measurements, applied now to populations and hospitals and desalination plants and ventilator-dependent patients.

The numbers were the numbers. The numbers were people.

---

"The targeting question," Sofia said, "is which nodes produce the maximum throughput reduction with the minimum dependency impact."

She had framed it that way deliberately. *Minimum dependency impact.* The language of optimization. The language of a scientist selecting variables in a controlled experiment — except the variables were grid sectors and the experiment was a coordinated infrastructure shutdown and the controlled conditions included populations that depended on the infrastructure being shut down.

"The Gulf corridor produces high throughput reduction per facility — the processing plants are large-scale, high-output. But the dependency is acute. Desalination. Freshwater. The backup capacity is zero."

"Southeast Asian corridor has the best operational infrastructure," the Southeast Asian node said. "We know the grid. We've operated here. The proof of concept was here. But the dependency impact is diffuse — manufacturing, residential, hospital networks all affected."

"South America has the backup generation." The São Paulo commander. "Eleven of fourteen hospitals have generators. The manufacturing zones have rolling-blackout protocols from the pre-nitro era. The corridor is more resilient."

Sofia listened to the analysis. Infrastructure workers analyzing their own infrastructure. People who had built the grid discussing which parts of the grid they were willing to shut down and which populations would bear the cost of the shutdown. The discussion was clinical — not because the participants lacked empathy but because clinical language was the mechanism that permitted the decision. If they named the thirty-one ventilator-dependent patients as thirty-one people with families and histories and names they could look up in the public health databases, the discussion would stop. The discussion could not stop. The void was consuming territory at measurable rates across every Anchor Zone on the planet and the model worked and the model required throughput reduction and throughput reduction required shutting down the grid and the grid was the thing that kept the lights on and the water flowing and the ventilators running.

Clinical language was how you selected which populations lost power and which kept it.

"Prioritize South American corridor for the highest-density strikes," Sofia said. "The backup infrastructure provides a buffer. Gulf corridor — limited strikes, targeting the highest-throughput facilities with the shortest projected shutdown duration. Southeast Asian corridor — operational discretion to the cell commanders. You know the infrastructure. Target the nodes that produce maximum throughput reduction with minimum residential impact."

She was choosing who suffered. Not who died — the grid's structure determined that, the backup generators determined that, the infrastructure resilience of each corridor determined that. But who suffered first. Who suffered longest. Who suffered most visibly. The Gulf corridor's 2.1 million people would lose freshwater access at a rate determined by their reservoir capacity. The São Paulo corridor's thirty-one ventilator-dependent patients would lose power immediately at three hospitals without backup. The Southeast Asian corridor's 1.2 million residents would lose residential power according to the grid's load-shedding hierarchy — industrial before residential, the residential blocks going dark first.

She was an atmospheric physicist. She measured. She modeled. She published. She coordinated the deliberate shutdown of the infrastructure that 2.1 million people depended on for freshwater because the alternative was the void expanding until the freshwater infrastructure was inside the absorption field and the 2.1 million people were part of the aggregate signal.

The model did not distinguish between the suffering it prevented and the suffering it caused. The model described a correlation. The correlation operated at every scale the coordination could produce. The suffering operated at every scale the coordination could produce.

---

At 09:00, three hours into the coordination session, the relay carried a question from a node Sofia had not heard before.

"The three from Jakarta." A woman's voice. Southeast Asian corridor — the relay indicator showed a node in the Surabaya sub-network. "K-JAK-017. K-JAK-023. K-JAK-041. They've been in detention for twenty-three days. Do we have extraction capacity?"

Sofia looked at the arrest report on the right side of the desk. The same report from three weeks ago. The three designations. The three operational codes representing three people who had executed the proof of concept and not made it out.

"No," she said. "Jakarta Regional Security is holding them under infrastructure sabotage statutes. The legal architecture does not provide for extraction. Black Babel's legal contacts have filed representation motions but the statutes carry mandatory detention periods."

"How long?"

"Minimum ninety days. Maximum two years."

The relay was quiet.

"Their sectors," the woman said. "Their families. Are they in the dependency maps?"

Sofia looked at the maps on the display. The Jakarta-Surabaya corridor. The residential blocks adjacent to the three facilities the operatives had shut down. The 1.2 million people who depended on the grid infrastructure in that corridor. Three of those people were in a detention facility. Their families were in the residential blocks. The families of the people who had shut down the grid were living in the grid's service area, depending on the grid their family members had sabotaged, and Sofia's second-wave plan included the corridor where they lived.

"Yes," she said.

The relay held the silence.

"The coordination continues," Sofia said. It was not a dismissal. It was the only answer the data supported. Three operatives in detention. Their families in the dependency zone. The model working. The void expanding. The coordination continuing because the alternative to the coordination was the void consuming the dependency zone and the detention facility and the families and the operatives and every other population within the Anchor Zone's expansion trajectory.

She did not know their names. She knew their node assignments. She knew the throughput data from their sectors. She knew the model worked. She knew what the model cost.

---

At 11:00, she ate a protein bar. Standing. The same compound taste. The mechanical process of feeding the body while the mind continued assembling the dependency maps into targeting priorities and the targeting priorities into operational timelines and the operational timelines into the coordination plan that would send people into processing facilities across three continents to shut down the infrastructure their communities depended on.

She finished the bar and dropped the wrapper in the bin and went back to the desk.

The relay had been running for five hours. The working group had dispersed to their regional coordination tasks — the Gulf cells calibrating strike durations to minimize desalination disruption, the South American cells mapping backup generator capacity at every hospital in the target corridors, the Southeast Asian cells revising their operational plans based on the proof of concept's lessons. Sofia remained on the open channel, monitoring the feeds, available for data requests.

The field kit sat at the edge of the desk. Green indicator. The 0.7 Hz signal through the floor. The hum that was the hum — the structured pulse she had spent thirteen years measuring and three years trying to reduce and seven months coordinating people to reduce by shutting down the thing that carried it.

She looked at the dependency maps on the display. The Gulf corridor with its 2.1 million freshwater-dependent residents. The São Paulo corridor with its thirty-one ventilator-dependent patients. The Southeast Asian corridor with its 1.2 million residential power consumers. The numbers arranged on the maps the way numbers arranged themselves on every display she had ever read — clean, precise, the instruments not knowing the difference between measuring a population and measuring a throughput fluctuation.

She picked up the red marker. Wrote on the margin of the dependency map printout: *The model works at every scale. The cost scales with the model. 9% global = 6-hour window. To reach 31% = sustained zero expansion. The dependency cost at 31% is the dependency cost at 9% multiplied by 3.4, non-linear — the emergency protocols that recovered in 6 hours cannot recover from 31%. The shutdown is permanent until manual restoration. Manual restoration requires the operatives who shut it down or replacement crews. The replacement crews are the same workforce the Kindling recruits from.*

She set the marker down. Looked at the note. The red ink on the printout. The data. The cost. The same document.

---

At 14:00, Karaboué's feed carried a secondary analysis.

"The biological data from the mage network," he said. "The first-wave monitoring. Neural coherence across the thirty-one monitored regions dropped proportionally during the six-hour window. The mages reported — and I'm quoting the aggregate from their relay — 'the murmur softened.' The biological confirmation is clean. The triangle held at global scale."

"The triangle held," Sofia said. Atmospheric, infrastructure, biological. Three datasets. Three receiver types. One phenomenon. The same triangle she had described in her first publication and assembled for the first time at synchronized scale in the Jakarta proof of concept and confirmed at global scale in the first wave. The science was complete. The model was validated at every scale. The data was reproducible and the methodology was sound and the correlation was operating and the math held and the science was the science.

The science was the science. The science said shut down the grid. The grid was the thing 2.1 million people needed for freshwater. The science did not have a column for freshwater.

"There is one additional data point," Karaboué said. "From the Gulf monitoring station. During the six-hour window, the desalination Plant 2 — the one closest to the Al-Jubayl Anchor Zone perimeter — experienced a power fluctuation from the first wave. Not a full shutdown. A fluctuation. The plant's output dropped 7% for approximately two hours."

"And?"

"The plant's reservoir buffer absorbed the reduction. No service interruption. But the plant's operational team filed an incident report with the regional grid authority. The report references 'coordinated infrastructure action' as a potential cause."

She processed this. The Gulf desalination infrastructure was already being monitored for Kindling activity. The first wave's 9% global reduction had produced a measurable effect on a desalination plant that Sofia's second-wave targeting had identified as a critical dependency node. The operational teams knew. The grid authorities would be watching.

"The second wave will be harder," she said.

"The second wave will always be harder. Each wave teaches the grid what to defend."

---

She worked through the afternoon. Alone at the desk. The relay carrying intermittent traffic — regional cells reporting operational assessments, the Erasure List researchers updating their monitoring protocols, the mage network's biological data streaming in the background. She correlated. She mapped. She calculated the throughput reduction each target facility would produce and the dependency cost each shutdown would impose and the operational window each strike required and the extraction protocols each cell had prepared.

She built the second-wave plan the way she had built every model in her career — from the data up. The numbers first. The correlation. The prediction. The methodology. The variables controlled and the variables uncontrolled and the confidence intervals narrow enough to publish and the error bars small enough to defend.

Except the error bars were populations. The confidence intervals were measured in hours of freshwater access and ventilator-dependent patients and residential power consumers. The methodology was a coordinated infrastructure shutdown across three continents. The variables she could not control were the backup generators and the grid's emergency protocols and the operational teams who would be watching for exactly the kind of action she was planning.

She looked at the arrest report. K-JAK-017. K-JAK-023. K-JAK-041. Twenty-three days in detention. She did not know their names. She knew the throughput data from their sectors. She knew the model worked. She knew what the model cost. She knew the cost was going to increase.

She picked up the blue marker. Wrote on the margin of the second-wave plan:

*The human cost is the model's cost. The model does not distinguish between the signal and the service. The throughput that carries the void is the throughput that carries the power. To reduce the signal is to reduce the service. The correlation does not have a humanitarian exemption.*

She set the marker down. The ink dried on the paper. The hum continued through the floor.

The coordination continued.
