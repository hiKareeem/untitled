She counted before she opened her eyes.

Creds: sixty-four. Room: paid through Thursday — nine per day, thirty-six committed, leaving twenty-eight for food and the adhesive tube on day nine of twelve. Rice: two days in the cabinet. The route: confirmed. Three days of walking it. The masonry junction on the east side of the Anchor margin district, where two load-bearing walls met at a corner that pre-dated the breach and pre-dated the conduit infrastructure and pre-dated the system that had routed nitro through the substrate beneath it. The junction's stone was dense. Pre-breach construction — poured aggregate and rebar, the old method, the method that attenuated the signal by twelve to fifteen percent depending on wall thickness. She had measured this with the portable RCI monitor Yara's documentation group had lent her — not the tourist model, the industrial one, the kind the conduit maintenance crews used before the maintenance crews were decommissioned and the monitors became consumer goods and the consumer goods became lanyards around the necks of people paying two hundred creds to stand where eight point two million people had been absorbed.

The junction read 12.8 at baseline. The perimeter fencing was fourteen meters east. The observation platforms were sixty meters north — far enough that the tourist foot traffic would not interfere. The masonry walls at the junction rose four meters and provided a sheltered corridor with sight-lines to the Anchor Zone's outer edge. From the junction, the amber conduit glow was visible through the gaps in the perimeter fencing — the orange light that had been the Sump's permanent sky, leaking into the district that had formed around the boundary of the thing that had consumed her neighborhood.

She had mapped this the way she had mapped the buffer zones for Shuang's family. The way she had mapped the relocation corridors for the families in Sector 12. The topology of safety — which walls carried the signal and which walls blocked it, which paths ran through junction-dense corridors and which paths hugged the masonry, which positions allowed a person to stand at the edge of the Anchor Zone and the standing would not destroy them if they stayed less than forty minutes.

Forty minutes. She had calculated this from the advisory threshold data in the public directory, cross-referenced with Fuxi's conduit density logs for the eastern perimeter, adjusted for the twelve-percent attenuation at the masonry junction. Forty minutes at 12.8 RCI with the attenuation buffer. After forty minutes, cumulative exposure climbed past the threshold where the advisory classification shifted from *elevated* to *intervention recommended* and the shift was not gradual — the shift was a step function, the body's tolerance operating like a load-bearing wall that held and held and held and then didn't.

She opened her eyes. The ceiling. The concrete. The hum through the masonry walls — steady, residential-grade. Nineteen months of this hum.

The frozen notification.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

Beside it, three green notifications. She would read them after the rice.

She stood. Brushed her teeth. Boiled water. Made rice. She ate standing at the sink.

Fen did not follow the mage network or the streaming platforms or the wider architecture of information that existed above and around the margin district's daily operations. The observation platforms had doubled — six now, extending past the perimeter fencing at intervals along the transit way, each one occupied, each one generating the foot traffic and the commerce and the ambient noise of a district that metabolized proximity into economy.

She stood at the junction's southern edge, where the transit way met the corridor that led to the masonry district on the east side. The corridor was quieter. The tourists stayed on the transit way — the platforms, the stalls, the vendor corridor on Level 2 where the polymer fragments were sold and the shimmer tours departed. The masonry district was residential. The masonry district did not sell anything.

She waited.

She heard it — the transit system's arrival tone, three ascending notes, the sound that meant the city was still moving people through corridors designed for the movement of people. The transit junction's foot traffic shifted as the doors opened and the passengers emerged and the passengers were tourists and residents and workers and one person whose hair caught the mixed light of the corridor — amber from the Anchor Zone bleed, white from the institutional illumination — and became something that did not belong to either system.

Blue on the left. Pink on the right. Neon. Shimmering. The synthetic augmentation that caught light the way a signal caught a receiver — designed for it, calibrated for it, impossible to miss.

She was shorter than Nuwa had expected.

The network had not conveyed height. The testimony exchanges, the topology data, the warning message she had sent after watching the stream where the plan was announced — none of it had included the datum that Kira Calloway was five-foot-four and that the five-foot-four was visible only now, in the transit junction, among the tourists and the vendors and the corridor's four-meter ceiling that made everyone small and Kira smaller.

Nuwa raised a hand. Not a wave — a location marker. The hand said *here*, the way her hands said everything: practically, efficiently, without performance.

Kira saw her. Crossed the junction. The walk was fast — the trained movement of a person whose body had been choreographed since childhood, the posture straight, shoulders open, chin level. The performer's carriage. Kira moved through the transit junction the way the amber light moved through the perimeter fencing — filling the space, claiming it, making the space orient toward her without effort and without choice.

"Nuwa."

The voice was different in person. On the network, Kira's messages had been text — precise, formatted, the filing system's architecture applied to communication. In person, the voice was the stream-voice. Warm. Fast. The energy of someone accustomed to narrating their experience as it happened.

"You're — I've been looking at your topology maps for months and I just realized I never actually pictured — you're real. Obviously. But you're *here*. And I'm here. And we've been exchanging data for —"

"Seven months. Right." Kira's eyes moved. Both of them — the natural blue right and the pink ocular implant left, the HUD's faint projection visible on the surface of the left eye as scrolling text, the interface processing something Nuwa couldn't read at this distance. The eyes didn't settle. They scanned — the corridor, the junction, the tourists, Nuwa's face, the masonry wall behind her, the amber light from the south, the observation platforms to the north. The scanning was comprehensive. The scanning was fast.

The scanning was too fast.

Nuwa knew this speed. Not from the network. Not from Kira's stream, which she had watched twice — once before sending the message, once after. She knew this speed from the Sump.

The maintenance crews. Deep-shift workers who serviced the conduit infrastructure in the high-RCI corridors where the throughput ran hot and the shifts ran twelve hours and the body required chemical assistance to sustain the focus and the reaction time and the fine-motor precision that the work demanded. The stacks — stimulant compounds, sourced through the Sump's pharmaceutical economy, dosed by weight and adjusted upward as tolerance built. The crews on stacks moved like this. Hands steady but restless — the fingers looking for something to do, the motor cortex running hotter than the task required. The crews on stacks deteriorated. No adverse effect noted.*

Kira's eyes moved too fast. Kira's voice was too quick. Kira's posture was too electric for a woman who had traveled on civilian rail for nine hours to stand at the edge of the thing that had consumed eight point two million people.

Nuwa did not say this.

"This way," she said.

"RCI monitor. Industrial model. I walked the corridor with it for three days."

Kira looked at her. The eyes — both of them, the scanning paused for a moment, the focus settling on Nuwa's face with the attention of someone who was accustomed to reading audience reaction and was now reading a person who was not an audience.

"I mapped this for whoever was going to stand at that junction. You're the one who decided it would be you."

They reached the masonry junction. "You sound like my mother."

"I sound like someone who survived the last time someone didn't listen."

Kira's expression shifted. The stream-voice energy dimmed for a half-second — the recognition of something landing, the weight of a sentence that arrived through the body rather than through the processing speed. Then the energy returned. The number, whatever the number was — Nuwa did not know the compound, did not know the dose, knew only the pattern — restoring the speed that had absorbed the weight before the weight could settle.

"Forty minutes," Nuwa said.

She placed the water bottle against the masonry wall beside Kira's position. She checked the dampening field on Kira's chest — the portable unit, military surplus, generating the counter-frequency whine that Nuwa could hear through the cracked housing. Active.

"Keep the dampening on."

"It reduces signal resolution. The stream needs —"

"Keep the dampening on."

Kira looked at her. The eyes moving — the scan, the assessment, the processing. Then a nod. Small. The nod of a person who had spent months receiving topology data from a woman she'd never met and who was now standing in the topology the data described and the data was correct and the woman was correct and the correctness was the thing that the filing system could categorize as *trust*.

"Forty minutes," Kira said.

Not quickly. The pace of a woman crossing thirty meters of masonry corridor because the crossing was the next item in the sequence. "Bring water."

The age of a person who had been modified since ten and medicated since sixteen and performing since thirteen and who was now standing in a transit junction in a city she had visited three times and understood from orbit.
