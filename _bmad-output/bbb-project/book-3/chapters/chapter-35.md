> *"Sustained presence is not a medical intervention. It is a logistical one. She counted before she opened her eyes.

Creds: fourteen. Room: paid through tomorrow — nine committed, leaving five for food. Rice: gone. Oil: gone. Greens: Fen had left a bundle of morning glory outside her door last night, the damp cloth, the cloth she used for greens and nothing else. Adhesive: day eight of twelve. Transit pass: valid through 18:00 today, round-trip, purchased yesterday with the forty creds the documentation group had transferred for the eastern perimeter topology update.

The transit pass was the first item in the inventory that had not existed in any previous inventory. She had never purchased a transit pass. She had walked everywhere in the margins — the masonry corridors, the residential grid, the market on Level 3, the Anchor margin district — because walking was free and transit was not and the arithmetic of creds and days did not include the variable of going somewhere the walking could not reach.

The documentation group had paid her. Not for tours. Not for guided proximity experiences. For the topology data — the junction coordinates, the conduit densities, the path-poor classifications that the Lagos integration used and the Mumbai node used and the fourteen-city database used because the data was corridor-level and the corridor-level data required a person who had walked the corridors and survived the walking. Yara had sent the transfer with a one-line message: *This is compensation, not charity. Your data has institutional value. The institution should pay for it.*

Forty creds. Enough for a round-trip transit pass and a meal she would not eat because the five remaining creds were for tomorrow's rice and the meal would cost three and the three subtracted from five left two and two was not enough for the adhesive tube on day twelve.

She opened her eyes. The ceiling. The concrete. The hum through the masonry walls — steady, residential-grade. Twenty-three months.

The frozen notification.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

Beside it, eleven green notifications. She would read them on the transit. The green had been accumulating faster since the blackout stream — faster still since the network learned that Kira was hospitalized and the hospitalization meant the hub was offline and the hub being offline meant four hundred mages across thirty-one cities were routing their queries through the next available node and the next available node was whoever answered and Nuwa had been answering.

She stood. Brushed her teeth. Boiled water. Ate the morning glory — steamed, no rice, no oil, the greens from Fen's cloth the only food in the room. She ate standing at the sink.

---

The adhesive tube was on the shelf. Day eight of twelve. She unsealed the cap and ran the line along the hairline crack. Thumbnail into the split. Counted to thirty. The chip was in the port — Fuxi's thirteen years, the corroded contacts, the junction maps that were now in a database in fourteen cities and that had earned forty creds and the forty creds had become a transit pass and the transit pass was taking her to a hospital where a woman she had positioned at a masonry junction was lying in a bed because the positioning had been correct and the signal had not followed the positioning.

She pulled on her jacket. The mechanical latch clicked behind her.

She stopped at Fen's door. Knocked once.

"Early," Fen said through the door.

"I'm going to Chén Academy. The medical facility."

A pause. Then the door opened. Fen looked at her — the brief assessment, the reading of posture and intent.

"Kira?"

"She's been there five weeks."

"You haven't been."

"I couldn't afford the transit."

Fen did not say what the assessment was calculating — that the transit cost less than the media consulting rate Nuwa had refused, that the eight hundred creds would have paid for forty round trips, that the arithmetic of refusal had a cost the refusal did not include. Fen did not say this because Fen had said *good* when Nuwa refused and the *good* was still the correct word and the cost was still the correct cost.

"Back by eighteen hundred," Nuwa said. "The pass expires."

"Eighteen hundred," Fen said. "Chén Academy."

The protocol. Tell someone where you are going and when you expect to return. The protocol of women who had learned that the system did not track them and that the not-tracking meant nobody would look.

---

The transit was fast.

Forty minutes — the same duration she had calculated for Kira's proximity window at the masonry junction. The coincidence was not meaningful. The coincidence was arithmetic. But the body registered it anyway, the way the body registered all patterns — without interpretation, without significance, as data filed in the inventory that did not explain itself.

She read the green notifications on the transit. Tilted her head to compensate for the dead zone.

Three from the network's coordination channel — mages in Jakarta and São Paulo and Lagos requesting telemetry routing updates. Kira's network had been self-sustaining for months. Two hundred and fourteen hubs across thirty-one cities. The architecture held. The architecture had been designed to hold without Kira — the filing system, the categories, the format that four hundred mages used to standardize their signal observations. What the architecture had not been designed to hold without was the person who answered when the architecture produced a question the architecture could not resolve.

The mages were not panicking. The mages were professional — trained, experienced, the four hundred people who carried the signal in their spines and documented the carrying with the institutional precision of people who had learned that documentation was the work. But the questions were accumulating. Anomalous readings since the Proposition. Throughput shifts. Coherence patterns that did not match the pre-Proposition baseline. The network needed someone to say *file it under the new category* or *cross-reference with the Mumbai node's data* or *this is consistent with the São Paulo readings from last week*. The network needed a hub.

Nuwa was not a mage. She did not have an R3. She did not carry the signal in her spine. She had a cracked civilian implant with a dead man's data chip in the port and a frozen notification and a transit pass that expired at 18:00.

She answered the queries. Jakarta's anomalous reading: *Forward to Ren in Singapore, he has the spectral analysis tools. File under post-Proposition baseline shift.* São Paulo's throughput correlation: *Cross-reference with Lagos node — Adaeze reported the same pattern three days ago. Consolidate.* The third query was logistical — a hub coordinator in Manila requesting the network's current routing table because the routing table had been in Kira's filing system and Kira's filing system was in a hospital and the hospital was not routing anything.

Nuwa had the routing table. She had downloaded it from the network archive six days ago — the day she started answering queries, the day she realized the accumulation was not going to stop and the queries were not going to route themselves and the network that Kira had built from a streaming chair was going to require someone who did not carry the signal but who knew how to carry information through corridors when the corridors' usual architecture was offline.

She sent the routing table to Manila. She answered two more queries — Nairobi and Mumbai. She closed the overlay.

The transit was arriving. Chén Academy. The station's display showed the institutional seal — the Academy's emblem, the stylized R3 implant symbol that she had seen on Kira's streaming overlay and on the documentation group's medical references and that she was now seeing on a transit platform because the transit had brought her to the institution that trained mages and she was not a mage and the institution did not know her name.

---

The hospital's visitor protocol required a credential scan.

She stood at the reception desk. The desk was institutional — white composite, the surface smooth, the kind of surface that subderm fingertip contacts interfaced with seamlessly and that Nuwa's chemical-stained hands rested on without interfacing with anything. The receptionist looked at her the way institutional staff looked at people whose presence the institution had not anticipated — the brief assessment, the scanning of clothing and augmentation and posture that located a person in the institution's taxonomy before the person spoke.

"I'm here to see Kira Calloway," Nuwa said.

"Relationship?"

The word the form required. The category that the institution used to determine whether a person standing at a desk had sufficient connection to a person lying in a bed to justify the administrative processing of a visitor pass. The categories: family, partner, legal representative, medical professional, institutional affiliate.

"Friend," Nuwa said.

The word was not in the categories. The word was in the gap between the categories — the space the form did not have a field for because the form was designed for relationships the institution recognized and the institution recognized relationships that had institutional architecture and friendship did not have institutional architecture.

The receptionist processed the gap the way institutional staff processed all gaps — by finding the nearest available category. "Non-family visitor. I'll need identification."

Nuwa placed her hand on the scanner. The cracked implant transmitted what the cracked implant transmitted — the Basic credential, the frozen notification's data, the serial number of a salvage-grade external-mount unit that the scanner read with a half-second delay because the scanner was calibrated for subderm models and the external housing required the scanner to search for the signal at a different depth.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Visitor pass: temporary, 4 hours.*

Four hours. The institution's allocation for non-family visitors. Enough time. She had calculated enough time the way she calculated everything — by the inventory of what was needed, not the inventory of what was permitted.

---

The corridor was white. The lighting was institutional. The hum was absent.

She noticed the absence the way she noticed all absences — as a datum, as a negative reading in the inventory that the body maintained without instruction. The Chén Academy medical facility was shielded. The conduit infrastructure that carried the hum through every wall and floor and ceiling in the Mid-Levels was blocked here — dampening architecture built into the facility's construction, the counter-frequencies embedded in the structural materials, the institutional investment in creating a space where the signal did not reach and the bodies inside could be assessed without the signal's interference.

The silence was loud. The silence was the loudest thing in the corridor. Twenty-three months of the hum — residential-grade, steady, the frequency that meant the system continued — and now the frequency was gone and the body noticed the way a body notices when the thing it has been carrying is removed and the removal weighs more than the carrying.

Room 4-East. The door was open. The monitoring array beeped. The numbers — green, institutional — sat on a display beside the bed.

Kira was sitting up. Not the performer's posture — the spine that straightened and the shoulders that opened and the frame that became larger when the overlay activated and the audience arrived. The posture was the other one. The one Nuwa had seen once, at the transit junction after the first proximity stream — the spine curved, the shoulders forward, the five-foot-four frame that was five-foot-four without the electricity the compound had provided.

The neon hair was growing out. Roots visible — dark, the natural color emerging from beneath the blue and the pink, the augmentation fading the way all augmentation faded when the maintenance stopped. The left eye was dark. No pink scroll. No HUD overlay. The right eye was looking at her.

"You came," Kira said.

The voice was different. Slower. The stream-voice's energy — the warm, fast narration, the associations pre-assembled, the pauses structural — was gone. What remained was quieter. The voice of a person who was not broadcasting and who was not performing and who was not processing at the speed the compound had provided. The voice of a person in a hospital bed.

"I brought water," Nuwa said.

She set the bottle on the bedside table. She had filled it at the transit station — the refill point, free, the city's infrastructure providing what the city's infrastructure provided. The bottle was the same one she carried to the masonry junction. The same one she had handed to Kira after the first proximity stream when the hands were shaking and the tremor was not cold.

Kira looked at the bottle. The right eye — the natural blue, no longer scanning too fast, no longer jumping between focal points with the precision that biology did not produce. The eye was still. The eye was the speed of a person whose processing was the person's processing and not the compound's.

"How did you get here?" Kira asked. "The transit —"

"The documentation group paid me for the topology data. Forty creds."

"Yara's group?"

"The junction coordinates have institutional value. They paid for the value."

Kira almost smiled. The almost was different from the first almost-smile at the transit junction — that one had been the compound's energy dimming for a half-second, the person visible through the pharmaceutical filter. This one was the person without the filter. The almost was slower. The almost was tired.

"You've been answering the network queries," Kira said.

"Someone had to."

"Ren said you rerouted the entire Jakarta hub's telemetry through Singapore in four messages."

"Ren has the spectral tools. Jakarta doesn't. The routing was arithmetic."

"It wasn't arithmetic. It was — you kept the network running."

Nuwa did not answer this. The answer was in the bottle on the bedside table and the queries she had routed and the transit pass in her pocket and the forty creds that were now fourteen creds and the fourteen creds were tomorrow's problem and tomorrow's problem was tomorrow's inventory.

She sat in the chair beside the bed. The chair was institutional — hard, not designed for four hours, designed for the visits the institution allocated for non-family visitors. She sat the way she sat everywhere — compact, shoulders forward, occupying as little space as the chair allowed.

---

The monitoring array beeped. The numbers held.

Nuwa looked at them — heart rate, neural activity, coherence density, R3 thermal. She did not know what the numbers meant in the clinical vocabulary the institution used. She knew what numbers meant. She knew what a system looked like when the numbers were the system's language for describing the state of the thing the system monitored.

"The drugs," she said.

Kira looked at her. The look was the look of a person who had been in a hospital bed for five weeks being assessed by instruments and staff and neurologists and who was now being assessed by a woman whose assessment operated in a different vocabulary.

"Fifteen milligrams," Kira said. "Down from ninety. They're tapering to zero over the next week."

Fifteen. From ninety. The arithmetic was simple — the reduction rate, the duration, the curve that Nuwa had seen in the maintenance crews when the stacks were withdrawn and the withdrawal followed the same trajectory the stacking had followed, reversed. The speed went first. Then the focus. Then the thing the speed and the focus had been compensating for became visible, and the visible thing was the person, and the person was what had been underneath the compound the entire time.

"The eyes," Nuwa said.

"What?"

"Your eyes. When I met you at the transit junction. They were too fast. Scanning. The speed the maintenance crews had when they were on stacks."

Kira was quiet. The quiet was not the performer's pause — the structural beat that created space for the audience. The quiet was a person hearing something she had not heard before, or had heard and not filed, or had filed in a category the filing system had not been able to process because the filing system had been running on the compound the filing was about.

"You knew," Kira said.

"I saw the pattern. I sent you a message."

"Four seconds. I filed it in four seconds."

"I know."

The silence was the silence of the shielded room — no hum, no frequency, the absence that weighed more than the presence. The monitoring array beeped. The numbers held.

"They want me to go to Mumbai," Kira said. "The Academy. For the dampening."

Nuwa waited.

"Chén doesn't have the equipment. The R3 was installed at Mumbai's campus — they have the original calibration data, the surgical records, the implant specifications from when I was ten. They're the only ones who can dampen it without risking —" She stopped. The sentence unfinished. The sentences had been unfinished more often since the compound's reduction — the processing speed that had kept the sentences complete now operating at the speed the biology provided, and the biology did not always finish.

"Risking," Nuwa said. Not a question. A completion the silence needed.

"Paralysis. The R3 is integrated into the spinal architecture. Dampening means adjusting the reception threshold — pharmacological, not surgical. But the calibration has to match the original installation parameters or the adjustment destabilizes the implant's interface with the neural tissue." She was reciting. The filing system's architecture applied to medical information she had been given by people whose institutional vocabulary she had learned the way she had learned every institutional vocabulary — completely, precisely, the categories absorbed.

"So you go back to the institution that installed it," Nuwa said.

"The institution that installed it when I was ten. Trained me. Expelled me. And is now the only one that can fix me."

"Fix."

"Dampen. The word they use is dampen."

Nuwa knew the word. She did not know it from the medical vocabulary or the mage vocabulary or the institutional vocabulary that the Academy used to describe what it did to the people it processed. She knew it from the Sump.

The dampening fields the tourists wore at the Anchor margin — the chest-mounted units that generated a counter-frequency to reduce the hum's amplitude within a one-meter radius. The dampening that the system sold as a product to people who wanted to stand near the thing that had consumed eight point two million people without the thing reaching them. Dampening was the word the system used for the technology of proximity without cost. Dampening was how you stood at the boundary and did not pay.

Kira was going to pay. The dampening was the cost. The cost was the signal — the populated chord, the 8.2 million, the names, the data that four hundred mages collected through their spines and routed through hers. The dampening would take it. Not the signal — the signal would continue. The reception. The thing that made the signal legible. The thing that made Kira the hub.

Nuwa had watched the Sump's conduit infrastructure get dampened. After BLACKWEIR — the decommissioning crews, the counter-frequency generators installed at every junction, the systematic reduction of the signal through the infrastructure that carried it. The dampening had not eliminated the hum. The dampening had made the hum quiet enough that the system could build observation platforms on top of it.

She did not say this.

"When?" she said.

"Next week. The transfer paperwork is processing."

"Do you need someone to coordinate the network while you're there?"

Kira looked at her. The right eye — still, the natural blue, the speed of a person without a compound.

"You've already been coordinating it."

"I've been answering queries. The coordination requires someone who understands the signal architecture. Ren can do it from Singapore. I can handle the logistics — the routing, the scheduling, the hub communications. The topology."

"You're offering to run my network."

"I'm offering to keep the corridors clear while you're in the other room."

The sentence arrived the way all of Nuwa's sentences arrived — practically, without metaphor, the logistics of care compressed into operational vocabulary. But the sentence carried the weight of the masonry junction and the thirty meters and the first proximity stream and the watching-as-work and the emergency call from a cracked implant and the five weeks of not visiting because the transit was too expensive and the five weeks of visiting now because the documentation group had paid forty creds for data that had kept people alive.

Kira's right eye was wet. Not the signal — the signal was dampened in this room, the shielding blocking whatever the R3 would have received. The wet was the other kind. The kind that the compound's speed had absorbed and the compound's absence permitted.

Nuwa reached for the water bottle. Poured. Handed the cup to Kira. The paper cup — institutional, disposable, the kind that held a single dose of the chemistry the hospital provided without the architecture the pharmacist on Mission had provided. The cup held water. The water was the thing Nuwa had.

Kira drank.

---

She spent three hours in the corridor.

The chair beside Kira's bed had been sufficient for the conversation. The corridor was sufficient for the work. She sat on the floor — back against the wall, the institutional composite cool through her jacket the way masonry was cool through her jacket, the body finding the position the body found in every corridor: compact, efficient, the posture of a woman whose spatial economics had been learned in rooms with low ceilings.

The overlay was open. Tilted head. Dead zone compensation.

She answered seventeen queries in three hours. The network's post-Proposition traffic — mages reporting coherence shifts, throughput anomalies, the aggregate's changed behavior since the forty-seven-minute event that the network was calling the Proposition and that Nuwa was calling what it was: a status report from the thing that had consumed her neighborhood.

She did not use that vocabulary in the messages. She used the network's vocabulary — the clinical terminology, the filing categories, the standardized format that Kira had designed and that four hundred mages used and that Nuwa had learned the way she learned everything, by doing it until the doing was the knowing.

*Jakarta: forward anomalous coherence readings to Singapore hub. Ren will run spectral analysis. File under post-Proposition baseline — Category 7, Sub-category: new.*

*Nairobi: the throughput correlation you're reporting is consistent with Lagos, São Paulo, and Manila readings from the past week. Consolidate with Adaeze's dataset. The pattern is emerging across southern-hemisphere nodes.*

*Mumbai: your hub coordinator is requesting Kira's original calibration format for the telemetry logs. I have a backup of the format template from the network archive. Sending.*

The work was the work. The work did not require an R3 or a signal or a populated chord or the pharmaceutical architecture that had made Kira the fastest processor in the network. The work required someone who could read a query and route it to the person who could answer it and file the routing in a system that held the routing the way all systems held their data — structurally, without opinion, the mechanism doing what the mechanism did.

A nurse passed. Looked at her — the woman on the floor, the cracked implant, the Basic credential on the visitor pass clipped to her jacket. The nurse's assessment was the institution's assessment: a non-family visitor who had exceeded the chair's comfort parameters and relocated to the floor and who was performing a function the institution did not classify as visiting.

The nurse did not ask her to move. The nurse continued down the corridor.

Nuwa answered two more queries. Closed the overlay. The four-hour visitor pass showed ninety minutes remaining.

She went back into the room. Kira was asleep. The monitoring array beeped. Heart rate: 62. Neural activity: 2.8. Coherence density: 0.19. R3 thermal: 37.0.

The numbers were the institution's vocabulary for a system that was running quieter. The drug taper. The dampening preparation. The instrument being returned to factory calibration, the field calibrations — the adjustments accumulated during operation in non-standard conditions — classified as drift.

Nuwa refilled the water. Set it on the bedside table. The bottle beside the paper cup beside the institutional tablet beside the monitoring array. The inventory of objects around a person in a hospital bed — the same inventory every hospital bed held, the same objects arranged in the same institutional geometry, the architecture of care that the institution provided and that did not include the architecture the person needed.

She thought of her mother. Mei. The last year — mostly in the apartment, coughing, watching the Feed, telling Nuwa to save her money and get out. The institutional bed had been the same. The objects had been the same. The monitoring had been the same. The system that had consumed her mother through chronic nitro-exposure had provided the bed and the monitoring and the institutional geometry and the institutional geometry had held the body while the body was consumed and the consumption had been called *care* and the care had been administratively sufficient and the sufficiency was the system's vocabulary for the distance between what was provided and what was needed.

The distance was a bottle of water and a woman sitting on a corridor floor answering queries on a cracked implant.

She did not wake Kira. She adjusted the water bottle's position — closer to the bed's edge, within reach. She checked the monitoring array. The numbers held.

She left the room.

---

The transit home was forty minutes. She read the remaining green notifications. Two topology requests. One from the Dharavi correspondent — the one who counted nineteen, the one who had written *it is not the same verb.* The message was brief. *Mumbai Academy is preparing for a high-profile patient. The network is speculating. Is it her?*

She did not reply. She would reply tomorrow. The reply would be data — not confirmation, not speculation. The logistics of what the network needed to know and when the network needed to know it and the boundary between the person's medical decisions and the network's operational requirements.

The frozen notification sat at the edge of her vision. Grey. Permanent.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

The green notifications sat beside it. Accumulating.

She cooked in the evening. The morning glory stems from this morning — re-steamed, the greens reduced to their second use, the nutrition diminished but present. No rice. No oil. She would buy rice tomorrow with the five creds and the rice would last two days and the two days would carry her to the next topology payment and the next topology payment would carry her to the next transit pass and the next transit pass would carry her back to the hospital.

She ate at the table. The folding surface with the cardboard shim.
