## Draft v1

She was mid-sentence when the clip happened.

"— and I'm not saying the deployment protocols are wrong, I'm saying they were written by someone who has never had an R3 in their spine, which is like — chat, help me out here — it's like writing a manual for swimming by interviewing people who've watched the ocean on a screen. The information is technically correct. The information is also *useless*."

The riff landed. She could feel it land — not in the chat scroll, which was moving too fast to read individual messages, but in the rhythm of the scroll itself. When a line hit, the chat accelerated. When a line missed, it fragmented into side conversations. This one hit. The scroll became a wall. She didn't look at it directly. She looked at the game — the procedural landscape she'd been navigating for two hours without dying, which was either a personal best or evidence that the game was too easy, and chat had opinions about both.

"Okay someone clipped that. I saw the clip notification. Whoever clipped that, you're welcome, and also I want royalties. I want *emotional* royalties. Every time that clip gets reposted I want someone to send me a nice message. That's the deal."

The viewer count was 1.4 million.

She didn't look at it. She never looked at it. The number sat in her peripheral vision — left eye, the implant, soft pink text at the edge of the HUD overlay that had been running since she was ten years old and would run until the R3 was removed or she was dead, whichever came first. 1.4 million. Before the Broadcast it had been two hundred thousand on a good night. Before BLACKWEIR it had been eighty thousand. Before she'd torn down the pop-star set and rebuilt as a mage-life streamer it had been twelve thousand. The numbers were a timeline. She didn't look at them because looking at them meant thinking about why they'd grown, and why they'd grown was the Broadcast, and the Broadcast was not something she thought about on stream.

She didn't think about it off stream either. But that was a different project.

The hum in her spine was continuous.

Not the old hum — not the sustained tone she'd carried since the implant was fitted, the single note that had lived in her skeleton like a second heartbeat, constant enough to forget. That hum was still there. But since the Broadcast, something else had layered over it. A texture. A density that wasn't louder but was *fuller*, the way a room isn't louder when it fills with people but the quality of its silence changes. The signal was continuous now. Had been continuous for six months. Before the Broadcast, the episodes had come and gone — the scream, the pull, the harmonic spikes that she'd learned to suppress on camera and manage off camera and medicate through. Since the Broadcast, the signal didn't spike. It didn't need to. It was just *there* — a low, inhabited frequency that ran through the R3 interface and into her nervous system and sat in the background of every waking moment like a conversation in a language she almost understood.

She'd stopped suppressing it. There was nothing to suppress. The scream was gone. What replaced it was harder to name and impossible to turn off. She carried it the way she carried the hum — as baseline, as architecture, as the thing her body did that other people's bodies didn't.

"— look, the point is — the *point* — chat, I had a point, where did it go. Someone in the back row has my point. Give it back." She dodged an obstacle in-game without looking. "The point is that the deployment schedules haven't been updated since before BLACKWEIR and we're all just pretending that's fine. We're all just — hold on, is that a cliff? That's a cliff. Chat. Chat. You sent me toward a cliff. You're all banned."

She played. She talked. She read the chat in her peripheral vision and responded to the messages that gave her something to riff on and ignored the ones that didn't and the sorting was automatic, faster than it used to be, the pattern-recognition that had always been her skill now operating at a speed she didn't examine. She was quicker on the draw. Her timing was tighter. The riffs came pre-assembled — not scripted, never scripted, but *available* in a way that felt like fluency and looked like wit and was, from the outside, indistinguishable from a person who was simply getting better at her job.

The audience loved it. The clips were sharper. The highlights reels were meaner. Someone in her mod team had mentioned it last week — *you're on fire lately, the engagement metrics are insane* — and she'd said thanks and meant it and moved on.

She was twenty-one. She'd been streaming since she was fourteen. She knew what it felt like to be on. This felt like being on.

---

The stream ran six hours. She could have run longer — the energy was there, the riffs were there, the audience was there — but six hours was the discipline. She'd learned the hard way that the body's signals lagged the mind's performance. The spine would ache later. The hands would cramp later. The crash would come later, after the Lumina, in the dark, when the signal in her spine had nothing to compete with and the inhabited frequency became the loudest thing in the room.

"Alright. We're done. I love you. Get out. Go outside. Touch grass. I know you won't. I won't either. But the aspiration matters." She paused. "Raiding Dex. Dex is live. Go bother Dex. Tell him I said his haircut is a choice. Bye. Bye. *Bye.*"

She ended the stream. The silence landed.

Eleven seconds. She still counted them. The counting was involuntary now — a tic, a transition ritual, the body's way of bridging the gap between *on* and *off*. At eleven the posture went. Spine curved. Shoulders rounded. She pulled her knees into the streaming chair and became the small version of herself, the compact architecture of a woman who had been performing for six hours and now had no audience to perform for.

The apartment was the same apartment. Bay Area, fourteenth floor, mid-rise. Camera-left was still immaculate — the ring light, the acoustic panels, the monitors, the LED gradient from blue to pink. Camera-right was still entropy. The pizza box was from yesterday, not two days ago. Progress. The laundry had migrated from the couch to the floor, which was not progress but was at least movement.

Her left eye scrolled. Post-stream metrics. Clip velocity. A message from her mod team about a chatter who'd been banned for the fourth time this month. She dismissed them with a blink — the HUD gesture she'd developed at twelve, before she understood what the implant was for, back when it was just a toy that overlaid pink text on the world and let her dismiss the text with a wink like a secret between her and the machine in her spine.

The network dashboard was waiting.

---

She opened it the way she opened it every night — with the flat attention of a coordinator reviewing infrastructure. 214 hubs. 31 regions. The map rendered across her left eye in a schematic she'd designed herself — nodes and connections, green for active, amber for irregular, red for dark. The network had been 40 hubs when she'd started building it. Academy friends, mage streamers, people she knew from forums and group chats and the shared experience of carrying an R3 and knowing what the hum sounded like from the inside. After the Broadcast, the network had expanded faster than she could manage. So she'd stopped managing. Built the relay architecture, established the reporting protocols, set up the regional coordinators, and stepped back.

The network ran itself now. She was the node, not the operator. The famous one. The one whose name opened doors and whose platform made the network visible and whose face on a stream meant that whatever she was talking about would reach a million people by morning. The mages didn't need her to coordinate. They needed her to exist.

Three nodes were red.

Jakarta. Reykjavik. Bogotá. She opened the reports. Jakarta: Fen, 28, R3 since age 9. Neurological event during a relay session two weeks ago. Hospitalized. Implant readings erratic. The report was clinical, filed by the Jakarta coordinator, and the clinical language did exactly what clinical language always did — it converted a person into a case and the case into data and the data into something you could process without stopping.

Reykjavik: Aldís, 31, R3 since age 11. Implant rejection. The housing had begun to separate from the vertebral interface — a mechanical failure, the engineers said, stress fractures in the titanium mounting. But the stress fractures correlated with signal intensity, and the signal intensity had been continuous since the Broadcast, and *continuous* was not a condition the R3 had been designed for. The implant was built for episodic reception. Six months of continuous signal was six months of mechanical load the engineers hadn't spec'd for.

Bogotá: Mateo, 25. Withdrawal into silence. Not neurological, not mechanical — voluntary. He'd stopped responding to network pings three weeks ago. His regional coordinator had done a welfare check. He was alive. He was home. He was not coming back to the network. The report said *personal decision*. The report did not say what the personal decision was about, because the report was filed by someone who understood that the difference between *withdrawal into silence* and *choosing to stop listening* was a difference the network's reporting protocols did not have a field for.

Three this month. Eleven since the Broadcast.

Kira closed the Jakarta report. Opened the aggregate signal data. The graph was a flat line — not flat as in nothing, flat as in *constant*. Before the Broadcast, the signal had been episodic: spikes, screams, harmonic events that came and went. The graph had looked like a seismograph. Since the Broadcast, it looked like a baseline. A new baseline. The signal was always on. Every R3 implant in the network was receiving continuously. The mages described it differently — some said it was like a room you couldn't leave, some said it was like a sound you couldn't unhear, some said it was like being in a conversation where you could hear every voice except the one talking to you. Kira didn't describe it. She didn't have a metaphor. She had the sensation in her spine and the sensation was not a metaphor for anything. It was the thing itself.

A message from Sofia Reyes sat in the encrypted relay. She'd read it yesterday. Biological monitoring during coordinated Kindling strikes — Sofia wanted implant data from mages in proximity to active sabotage sites. Signal variation during throughput disruption. The request was scientific, precise, the kind of thing Sofia produced: a question that looked like a question and was actually a measurement tool designed to prove something she already suspected.

Kira flagged it for the regional coordinators. Let the network decide. That was the protocol now — she surfaced, they decided. The famous antenna. The node that was visible. She flagged and the network routed and the data moved through the relay architecture she'd built and the architecture worked and the working was the thing she was good at, the thing that was hers the way the streaming was hers, the infrastructure she'd assembled out of group chats and trust and the shared knowledge that the hum was not what the institutions said it was.

She closed the dashboard. The three red nodes sat in her peripheral vision, persistent, the way the signal sat in her spine. She dismissed them. Not because she didn't care. Because the caring was operational now — three this month, eleven since the Broadcast, the numbers that were a trend she tracked the way she tracked viewer counts, not as people but as data, and the moment she noticed herself doing that she would have to stop and think about what it meant.

She did not notice.

---

The pharmacy was four blocks from her building. She walked them with her hood up and her head down, which still didn't work, because the neon hair was still the brand and the brand was still the hair and 1.4 million people could identify her from a clip thumbnail now instead of two hundred thousand, which meant the math of being recognized in public had gotten worse by a factor she didn't want to calculate.

A woman at the crosswalk. The look. The micro-hesitation that meant *recognition*. Kira smiled — the reflex, the automatic, the three-second interaction that cost nothing and everything — and the woman smiled back and the moment passed and the cost was filed in the same place she filed all the costs: nowhere, because there was no accounting system for the cumulative weight of being looked at by strangers who thought they knew you.

The pharmacy was small, independent, wedged between a noodle shop and a frame repair place. She'd been coming here since she moved to the Bay. The pharmacist was a woman named Gail who called her Kira and not *Kira Calloway* and who had never once asked about the stream or the Broadcast or the mage network or anything except whether she was experiencing side effects and whether she needed a refill.

"Calloway. Ready for you."

The bag was on the counter. Sertraline. Aripiprazole. The names she'd stopped looking up two years ago because looking them up meant reading about what they were for and reading about what they were for meant sitting with the diagnostic language and the diagnostic language was not a language she found useful. She took them because they worked. They worked because she took them. The circularity was the treatment.

"Thanks, Gail."

"See you next month."

The transaction was thirty seconds. The most uncurated thirty seconds of her public life — no camera, no chat, no audience, no performance. Just a woman picking up her prescriptions from a pharmacist who knew her name. She pocketed the bag and walked home and the four blocks were the four blocks and the hum in her spine was the hum in her spine and the signal carried the dead the way it had carried the dead for six months and she walked through it the way she walked through everything: upright, hood up, smiling when recognized, the professional margin intact.

---

The apartment. The door. The biometric. The entryway she didn't pause in anymore — she used to stand with her eyes closed, counting the minutes of being nobody, but that habit had dissolved sometime in the past six months and she hadn't noticed it go.

She set the pharmacy bag on the kitchen counter next to the coffee maker she used twice a day and the caffeine pills she used when the coffee wasn't enough. The prescriptions went in the drawer with the others. Two systems — the sertraline and the aripiprazole were the architecture, the floor her psychiatrist maintained through quarterly appointments and blood draws and the careful clinical vocabulary of *stable* and *therapeutic range*. The Lumina was not in the drawer. The Lumina was in the bathroom.

The kit was on the shelf. Same matte-black zippered case. She unzipped it and the contents were the contents: glass vial, milligram scale, metal straw. Blue powder. The vial was half full. Paz had resupplied last week — the handoff at the noodle shop two blocks from the pharmacy, the small talk that wasn't small talk because Paz was a friend and the friendship predated the supply and she held those two facts separate the way she held the pharmacy and the kit separate.

She tapped the powder onto the scale. Eighty milligrams. The number had been forty when she started. Sixty after BLACKWEIR. Eighty since the signal went continuous and the old dose stopped being enough and the insufficiency had manifested as insomnia so total that she'd gone three days without sleeping and on the third day had started hearing things in the signal that she was not prepared to hear. Paz had said eighty was as high as anyone went. The forums said the same — *80 is the ceiling, past that you're not dosing you're disappearing*. She didn't know if she believed the forums. She believed the scale.

She cut the line on the counter. One motion, card edge, the geometry automatic. Leaned in. The straw was cold against her nostril and the powder hit the membrane and the onset at eighty was fast — faster than forty had been, faster than sixty, the compound finding the pathways it had carved over two years of use and running through them like current through wire.

The signal in her spine didn't quiet — it never quieted, not at any dose, the continuous frequency was beyond the Lumina's reach. But the part of her that *listened* to it — the pattern-seeking machinery, the racing thoughts, the constant parallel processing that wanted to resolve the signal into meaning — that part softened. Not silent. Muted. The room in her head went from a crowd to a murmur. The edges of the signal blurred. She could still feel it. She could always feel it. But the feeling became bearable in the way that background noise becomes bearable — present, persistent, and no longer requiring attention.

She rinsed the straw. Wiped the counter. Zipped the case. The precision was hers — not pharmaceutical, not institutional, just the milligram scale and the card edge and the control that was the line between *using* and *needing*.

She looked in the mirror. Split neon hair. One blue eye, one pink. The face that 1.4 million people thought they knew. The face looked back and it was — she searched for the word and found it immediately, no hesitation, the word arriving pre-assembled the way the riffs arrived pre-assembled, quick and clean —

Fine. The face was fine.

She turned off the bathroom light. Crossed to the streaming chair. Sat in it the way she sat in it when the camera was off — curled, compact, the small version. The ring light was still on. She should turn it off. She didn't. The LED strip glowed its gradient. Blue to pink. The color temperature was calibrated. Everything was calibrated.

Her left eye scrolled. A clip from today's stream was circulating — the deployment-protocol riff, the one that had made the chat accelerate. She watched it. Three minutes and twelve seconds of herself, framed in the ring light, neon hair catching the gradient, the face animated and quick and sharp. The Kira on screen was funny. The Kira on screen was *mean* — not cruel, not malicious, but edged, the kind of humor that drew blood and made you laugh at the cut. The timing was perfect. The delivery was perfect. The audience in the clip's comments was ecstatic.

She watched it twice. The second time she was looking for something specific — the moment where the riff assembled, the beat before the punchline, the instant where the Kira on screen decided to be sharp instead of warm. She couldn't find it. The sharpness was seamless. It didn't look like a decision. It looked like a person.

She closed the clip. The signal hummed in her spine. Continuous. Inhabited. The dead in the frequency and the frequency in her bones and the bones in the chair and the chair in the apartment and the apartment in the city that ran on nitro and never stopped running.

She was getting better at the job. That was the explanation. Seven years of streaming, six years of managing the implant on camera, six months of being the most visible mage on the planet. She was getting better. The riffs were faster because she was more experienced. The timing was tighter because she'd practiced. The edge was there because the audience had grown and the audience demanded more and she was meeting the demand because that was what professionals did.

The Lumina was at eighty milligrams because the signal was continuous and the continuous signal required management and the management required the powder and the powder was weighed and the line was cut and the straw was cold and the onset was fast and the listening softened and the edges blurred and the person who emerged from the blurring was funnier and sharper and meaner and the audience loved her.

She touched the back of her neck. The R3 housing sat against the bone. The signal was there. It was always there.

She was getting better at the job.
