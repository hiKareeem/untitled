She counted before she opened her eyes.

Creds: thirty-one. Room: paid through tomorrow — nine committed, leaving twenty-two for food and the adhesive tube on day three of twelve. Rice: today. Oil: gone. Greens: none. The Level 3 market vendor who accepted unregistered cred had raised prices twice since the district licensing went through. Supply lines unchanged. Demand relocated — the tourists ate, the margin residents ate, and the corridor between eating and affording had narrowed the way all corridors in the margins narrowed, incrementally, until the narrowing was the architecture.

She opened her eyes. The ceiling. The concrete. The hum through the masonry walls — steady, residential-grade. Twenty-one months.

The frozen notification.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

Beside it, seven green notifications. Network responses. She would read them after the rice. The green had been accumulating faster since the blackout stream — the network processing what had happened to Kira at the masonry junction where Nuwa had positioned her, the signal spike she had not predicted, the eleven minutes of silence on a live feed watched by four point two million people while Nuwa stood thirty meters away and called emergency services on a cracked implant that could barely hold a connection.

She had positioned Kira at the junction. She had calculated the forty minutes. She had mapped the attenuation and checked the RCI and said *this way* and *bring water* and *keep the dampening on*. The calculation had been correct. The signal had not followed the calculation.

She stood. Brushed her teeth. Boiled water. Made rice. Ate standing at the sink.

The rice was plain. The oil was gone. She ate the way she always ate when the inventory was low — efficiently, without pausing, the calories entering the body as supply rather than meal. She had cooked in the Sump because cooking was the one domain where she chose what entered her body. She cooked here for the same reason. The reason did not require oil.

---

The adhesive tube was on the shelf. Day three of twelve. She unsealed the cap and ran the line along the hairline crack. Thumbnail into the split. Counted to thirty. The chip was in the port — Fuxi's thirteen years, the corroded contacts, the junction maps that had become the foundation of every topology she had built and the topology that had failed to account for a signal spike that did not travel through infrastructure but through the aggregate itself, through the air, through the masonry that was supposed to attenuate it.

She pulled on her jacket. The mechanical latch clicked behind her.

---

The district had a name now.

The Feed called it the Anchor Margin Commercial Zone. The municipal licensing framework called it Sector 7M-South, Proximity Experience District. The vendors called it the Edge. The tourists called it the Edge. The margin residents who had lived here before the tourists arrived called it nothing — they did not name districts the way the system named districts, by function and classification. They named corridors. They named stalls. They named the woman at the far end of the Level 3 market who accepted unregistered cred and the man on Level 2 who repaired implant housings for twelve creds and did not scan serial numbers.

Nuwa walked south. The masonry corridor. The route she had walked for twenty-one months, her hand against the stone, the body confirming what the body knew. The stone was cool. The hum was present. Four blocks to the district the system had built on the boundary of the thing that had consumed her neighborhood.

The first block was residential. Unchanged. The second block was residential with modifications — a food stall at the corridor junction, a vendor selling portable RCI monitors from a folding table, a woman offering overlay calibration from a recessed doorway. The third block was the transition — the corridor widening where the residential infrastructure met the commercial licensing zone, the municipal fencing visible at the corridor's southern terminus, the amber light bleeding upward from the Anchor Zone and mixing with the institutional white to produce the copper wash that the district's marketing materials called *atmospheric.*

The fourth block was the district.

She stopped at the corridor's edge. She did not enter yet. She stood where the residential masonry met the licensed commercial zone and she looked at what the perimeter had become since the last time she had walked this route, three weeks ago, the day after Kira's blackout stream, the day she had walked home from the medical transport junction and not stopped walking until she reached her room and sat on the mattress and stared at the topology map on the wall and the map had not changed and the map had not been enough.

Three weeks. The district had grown.

The observation platforms had multiplied. She counted nine — six along the perimeter fencing, cantilevered over the municipal barrier on reclaimed infrastructure composite. Three more on the upper level, built into the corridor's structural beams, enclosed in transparent polymer that she recognized as the same material the Sump's residential windows had been made of. The enclosed platforms charged more. The polymer walls provided dampening. The dampening was the product — not the view, not the proximity, but the assurance that the proximity would not cost you what it had cost the people who had lived here.

The platforms were full.

The tourists were visible in the way that tourists were always visible — by the quality of their augmentation. Mid-Level standard and above. Current-generation implants with subderm housing, the sleek lines invisible beneath the skin, no external disc, no visible seams, no itch. Frame overlays rendered at resolutions Nuwa's cracked housing could not produce — she could see the projection edges on the tourists' eyes, the faint violet shimmer of a high-bandwidth feed processing the Anchor Zone's data in real-time, converting the hum and the amber light and the RCI readings into an experience that the overlay packaged and the tourist consumed and the consumption was the product and the product was two hundred creds for ninety minutes.

The bodies were the other thing she noticed.

Not the faces. The bodies. The tourists moved through the district in bodies that were more modification than original — the accumulation of twenty-two years of consumer augmentation since the first commercial implant lines had reached the Mid-Level market. Subderm overlays that traced the jawline in faint luminescent thread, active only when the feed was processing, pulsing with the data rate. Spinal reinforcement visible as a ridge of polymer housing beneath the fabric of lightweight jackets — the kind of structural modification that the Sump's workers had needed and never received and that the Mid-Level's consumers had purchased as posture correction and lifestyle enhancement. Hands with dermal conductors embedded in the fingertips — silver contact points that turned every surface into an interface, every wall into a data source, every railing into a haptic connection between the body and the system that monitored the body.

The tourists touched the perimeter fencing and their fingertips read the RCI. Their spinal housings processed the hum as data. Their overlay threads rendered the amber light as information — wavelength, intensity, spectral composition, the color of eight point two million absorbed rendered as a gradient map on the inner surface of an eyelid.

Nuwa's hands were at her sides. The chemical staining on her fingertips. The iridescent residue that marked her as someone who had touched infrastructure with bare skin and the infrastructure had marked her back. Her hands were not augmented. Her spine was not reinforced. Her implant was a salvage-grade external-mount unit with a cracked housing and a dead man's data chip lodged in the port and a frozen notification that had not updated in twenty-one months because the system that had generated it had been decommissioned when the district it monitored had been consumed.

She was the only person in the corridor whose body was entirely original.

---

The vendor corridor had doubled in length.

She walked through it the way she walked through everything — efficiently, compact, shoulders forward, taking up as little space as the corridor allowed. The vendors had organized into zones. RCI monitors at the entrance — the consumer models on display racks, the enhanced versions with false-color gradient rendering and haptic alerts, the professional models that Yara's documentation group used and that cost more than Nuwa's annual rent. Overlay accessories in the second zone — augmented dampening filters, signal-amplification patches for tourists who wanted to *feel more*, sensory enhancement drugs in sealed dispensers that the municipal licensing framework classified as *proximity experience supplements* and that Nuwa classified as the same stimulant stacks the Sump's maintenance crews had used to work double shifts in high-RCI junctions.

The supplements were new. Three weeks new. Small dispensers mounted on vendor walls — biometric payment, subderm scan, the silver contact points on augmented fingertips pressing against the dispenser's interface and the dispenser releasing a capsule that the tourist swallowed and the capsule opened the nervous system's reception bandwidth and the bandwidth was the product. Feel more. Receive more. Experience the proximity at a depth that the unaugmented body could not achieve.

She thought of Kira.

The supplements in the vendor dispensers were not what Kira was on. They were civilian-grade — lower potency, shorter duration, calibrated for the advisory RCI range rather than the Anchor Zone's interior frequencies. But the architecture was the same. Open the bandwidth. Receive more. Pay for the receiving. The system that had consumed the Sump's population was now selling the sensation of proximity to the consumption.

She passed the artifact vendors without stopping. The polymer fragments were still there — mounted on stands, priced by mark. Unmarked: forty. Marked: one-twenty. Verified marked: three hundred. The prices had not changed. The inventory had expanded. New categories. Conduit housing fragments — sections of the trunk-line casings that had carried nitro through the Sump's infrastructure, cut and polished, the amber residue visible in the metal's grain. Textile samples — fabric strips from the reclamation plant where Nuwa had worked second shift for eight years, the synthetic fibers still carrying the solvent staining that marked them as Sump-origin. Masonry chips — small pieces of the annex walls, the poured concrete that had saved twelve percent of the survivors, sold as talismans for eighty creds each.

Masonry chips. The wall she had pressed her back against while the wet cloth cooled on her face and the hum detonated through the building and the eleven seconds consumed everyone who was not behind walls like the one she was touching. That wall was a product now. Eighty creds. The vendor had a basket of them.

---

She reached the masonry junction.

The junction where she had positioned Kira. The load-bearing walls that met at the corner, the pre-breach construction, the poured aggregate and rebar that attenuated the signal by twelve to fifteen percent. The junction was occupied. Three streamers — she could see the overlay activation rings in their eyes, the pink and green and amber of three different streaming platforms, the data flowing outward from their positions as they narrated the proximity for audiences she could not see and could not count and whose counting was someone else's inventory.

They were not Kira. They were the copies that had emerged after the blackout stream — streamers who had watched Kira's feed and seen four point two million concurrent viewers and calculated the arithmetic of attention and decided that proximity was content and the Anchor Zone was a set and the masonry junction was a location and the location was available because Kira was in a hospital bed and the hospital bed was what happened when the content consumed the performer.

One of the streamers was standing at the exact position Nuwa had mapped for Kira. Back against the eastern wall. Sight-line to the perimeter. The amber light across the face. The position Nuwa had calculated for optimal signal attenuation and minimum cumulative exposure and maximum safety within the forty-minute window.

The streamer was wearing a dampening field on a lanyard. Consumer model. The kind that the licensed proximity experience vendors provided as standard equipment and that reduced the RCI by approximately four percent and that was not the twelve to fifteen percent the masonry provided and was not the calculation Nuwa had performed and was not enough.

She did not say anything. The streamer was not her responsibility. The streamer had not asked for her maps or her topology or her forty-minute calculation. The streamer was a person in a licensed commercial zone using a position that Nuwa had mapped and that the licensing framework had made public and that the public had made into a product.

She turned away from the junction. Walked north along the masonry wall. The stone was cool against her hand. The hum was louder here — the Anchor Zone's proximity, the gradient climbing. The cracked housing registered it as warmth behind her ear. The itch.

"Excuse me."

She stopped. The voice was behind her — professional register, the modulated tone of a person who had been trained to approach strangers in public spaces. She turned.

A man. Mid-thirties. The augmentation was corporate — spinal housing visible at the collar, subderm overlay with the faint luminescent trace along the left temple, the kind of modification that indicated institutional employment rather than consumer choice. His hands had the silver contact points. He held a portable display — larger than Yara's, the professional model with the institutional seal of a media outlet she recognized from the Feed's news rotation.

"Are you Nuwa Lin?"

The second time. The first had been Yara in the Level 3 transit corridor — *You're Nuwa Lin, from the testimony network.* That had been recognition. This was identification. The difference was in the preparation — Yara had recognized her by coincidence, by proximity, by the convergence of a person and the data the person had produced. This man had come looking.

"I'm with the Meridian Network," he said. "We're developing a documentary series on Anchor Zone communities — the people who live in the margin districts, the survival knowledge, the ground-level experience that the institutional coverage doesn't capture. Your name came up in our research. The topology data, the testimony contributions, the work you did with the relocation networks."

The work she did. The sentence that converted what she had done — knocked on doors, shown maps, said *this way* and *bring water* and *the masonry corridors are the safe routes* — into a category the media outlet could process. *Work.* She had not called it work. She had called it the thing that happened when you had a map and the map showed where people would die and the people did not have the map.

"We'd like to offer you a consulting role," the man said. "Guided proximity tours for our production crew. Your knowledge of the safe routes, the infrastructure topology, the path-poor structures. Paid. Standard media consulting rate — two hundred creds per session, four sessions minimum."

Two hundred creds. The number that was the relocation stipend from the containment buffer zone notice. The number that was the price of a ninety-minute proximity experience on the licensed platforms. The number that the system assigned to things it was willing to purchase and the purchasing was the system's way of saying *this is what your knowledge is worth in the architecture that consumed the people the knowledge is about.*

Eight hundred creds minimum. More than she would earn in three months of unregistered-cred subsistence. Enough to pay rent through the end of the year. Enough for a new adhesive supply, a market run without calculating the cost of greens, a month where the inventory did not begin with subtraction.

"No," she said.

The man paused. The professional register adjusted — she could see the recalibration, the shift from pitch to accommodation, the trained response to a declined offer.

"The compensation is negotiable," he said. "And we're committed to ethical representation. The series —"

"The series is a proximity experience," she said. "With better production value."

She did not say the rest. She did not say that the safe routes she had mapped were mapped for the people who lived in the corridors the routes ran through, not for a production crew whose spinal housings would process the hum as content. She did not say that the topology data was in the network for the documentation group and the Lagos integration and the fourteen-city database that counted what the system did not count, not for a media outlet that would convert the counting into a narrative that the counting had never been. She did not say that the forty-seven names were names and the names were in the infrastructure and the infrastructure was the thing the documentary series was going to point cameras at.

She said no. The no was sufficient.

The man nodded. Professional. He produced a card — physical, printed, the corporate seal embossed. He extended it. She did not take it. He set it on the masonry wall's ledge — the stone surface that attenuated the signal and that had saved her life and that now held a media consultant's business card at the boundary between the zone where people had been absorbed and the zone where the absorption was being sold.

He left. She did not watch him leave.

The card sat on the masonry ledge. White against grey stone. The corporate seal catching the amber light from the perimeter. She picked it up. Put it in her pocket. Not because she would use it. Because the ledge was the junction and the junction was the topology and the topology was not a surface for the system's business cards.

---

She walked home along the masonry wall. The route. The hand against the stone. The body confirming what the body knew.

The district continued behind her. The platforms. The vendors. The streamers at the junction where she had positioned Kira and where the signal had spiked and where four point two million people had watched a woman stop speaking and stand still for eleven minutes while Nuwa called emergency services from thirty meters away and the emergency services had come and the woman had been taken to a hospital she had not yet visited because visiting required transit she could not afford and the not-visiting was in the inventory alongside the creds and the rice and the adhesive tube and the seven green notifications she had not yet read.

The corridor narrowed as it moved north. The commercial zone's licensed infrastructure gave way to the residential corridors of Levels 4 and 5. The amber light faded. The institutional white resumed. The hum dropped to residential baseline — the note that meant the system continued.

She climbed the stairs to the ninth floor. The stairwell was dark above the second floor. The masonry walls carried the hum muffled and reduced.

She stopped at Fen's door. Knocked once.

"Back already?" Fen said through the door.

"They offered me a job."

The door opened. Fen looked at her — the brief assessment.

"Who?"

"Media. Guided tours. The safe routes."

Fen was quiet. The quiet held the shape of the assessment — Fen measuring what the offer meant and what the refusal meant and whether the refusal was the right refusal or the refusal of a woman whose arithmetic did not include the variable of eight hundred creds because the variable was denominated in a currency that cost more than it paid.

"How much?"

"Two hundred per session. Four minimum."

"That's rent through the year."

"I know."

"And you said no."

"I said no."

Fen leaned against the frame. Arms crossed. The sorting hands still — the stillness of a woman who was not sorting but holding.

"Good," Fen said.

She did not say more. The word was sufficient the way Nuwa's *good* to Yara had been sufficient — a single word that contained the assessment and the conclusion and the shared understanding that the safe routes were not a product and the topology was not content and the knowledge of which walls carried death was not a consulting service.

"Greens?" Fen said.

"No. Tomorrow."

"I have cabbage. More than I need."

Nuwa's hands were at her sides. The reflex — the arithmetic that converted every offer into a transaction. She could feel it. The reflex was older than the margins, older than the Sump, old enough that it preceded the naming of it.

"Thanks," she said.

She took the cabbage. She carried it up the stairs to her room and put it on the counter beside the rice cooker and the adhesive tube and the empty oil bottle and the business card she had pulled from her pocket and set beside them because the card was now part of the room's inventory even though the card's offer had been refused and the refusal was the thing that made the card bearable as an object.

She cooked. Rice. The cabbage — sliced thin, dry-fried in the pan without oil, the edges browning in the heat, the smell filling the room. The smell of a choice made without oil and without two hundred creds and without a consulting contract and with the same hands that had mapped the routes the contract wanted to purchase.

She ate at the table. The folding surface with the cardboard shim.

The overlay pulsed. She opened it. Tilted her head.

Seven green notifications. She read them the way she read everything — once for inventory, once for structure. The Lagos coordinator requesting updated junction data for the Neo-Shanghai eastern perimeter. Yara's documentation group forwarding a pattern analysis from the Mumbai node. Three topology queries from margin residents in Sectors 8 and 9 — people she had never met, directed to her handle by the informal network that connected the buildings where people without credentials lived and where the walls were masonry and where the knowledge of which walls mattered traveled through the corridors the way the hum traveled through the conduits.

The sixth notification was from the Dharavi correspondent — the one who had counted nineteen, the one who had written *it is not the same verb.* The message was brief. *The absorption-adjacent artifact market has reached Mumbai. They are selling brick fragments from the textile workshop. I am writing to you because you will understand what it means when the wall that saved you becomes a product and the product is eighty creds and the eighty creds is the price the system assigns to the difference between absorbed and alive.*

She understood.

The seventh notification was from a handle she did not recognize. The message was one line.

*Is it true you turned down Meridian?*

The district talked. The district was a corridor and corridors carried signals and the signal of her refusal had traveled from the masonry junction to the network in the time it took to walk home.

She did not reply. She would reply tomorrow. Or the day after. The reply would be the work — junction coordinates, conduit densities, the topology of the sector's latest infrastructure changes. The reply would not be personal. The reply would be what she had instead of two hundred creds per session and a production crew with spinal housings and silver fingertips pointing cameras at the boundary of mass death and calling the pointing *documentary.*

The frozen notification sat at the edge of her vision. Grey. Permanent.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

The green notifications sat beside it. Accumulating.

She closed the overlay. Washed the bowl. One bowl. One pair of chopsticks. One mug. The count had not changed. Everything she owned could still be packed in four minutes.

The business card was on the counter. White against grey. She did not throw it away. She did not file it. She left it where it was — beside the adhesive tube and the empty oil bottle and the rice cooker and the cabbage stems she would compost in the corridor bin tomorrow. The card was an object in a room that did not accumulate objects, and the object was the shape of a refusal, and the refusal was hers.

The inventory was open. She was not done.

### Cyberpunk + 100 Years Visual Motif

Per author direction, this chapter pushes the cyberpunk visual register through:

1. **Bodies as commodity landscape:** The augmentation passage (lines 126-133) — subderm overlays tracing jawlines in luminescent thread, spinal reinforcement as polymer housing ridges, dermal conductors with silver contact points in fingertips. Bodies are more modification than original. The augmentation is consumer-grade, lifestyle-oriented, and creates a visual gulf between Nuwa (entirely original, external-mount implant, chemical staining) and the tourists (subderm, sleek, invisible seams).

2. **Technology as consumption interface:** Silver fingertips reading RCI from fencing, spinal housings processing hum as data, overlay threads rendering amber light as gradient maps on eyelid surfaces. The tourists' bodies are configured to *consume* the Anchor Zone's output — the same output that consumed the Sump's population.

3. **Sensory enhancement as product:** The proximity experience supplements in vendor dispensers — biometric payment via subderm scan, capsules that open reception bandwidth. The architecture mirrors Kira's Lumina escalation at consumer scale.

4. **The district as cyberpunk market:** Licensed observation platforms in reclaimed infrastructure composite, artifact authentication industry, overlay accessories, vendor zones organized by product category. The district has the density and layered economy of a cyberpunk bazaar built on the boundary of mass death.

5. **Nuwa as visual counterpoint:** Her body — unaugmented, stained, the cracked external-mount implant with its dead man's chip — is the only original body in the corridor. The contrast makes visible what "cyberpunk + 100 years" means: the augmentation is so normalized that its absence is the anomaly.

### Thematic Compliance

**Commodification of Suffering (Dominant):** The chapter is structured as an escalating inventory of commodification — from observation platforms to RCI monitors to dampening fields to proximity supplements to artifact vendors to masonry chips to copycat streamers to the media offer. Each item adds to the list. The list is the horror.

**Irrelevance of Individual Being (Strong):** Nuwa's refusal of the media offer is an assertion of individual relevance against a system that would convert her survival knowledge into content. The refusal does not stop the commodification. The district continues. The streamers occupy the junction. The masonry chips sell.
