Three more gone in six months.

She found them in the log the way she found everything in the log — filed, timestamped, formatted in the system she'd built twenty months ago when the network was four messages in an inbox and the filing was the only architecture she had. The system worked. The system held. The system was better at holding things than she was.

Accra: Lena. R3, verified, network member since week eleven. Implant burnout — the R3's primary interface had degraded past functional threshold and the failover to secondary receivers hadn't caught and the nervous system had done what nervous systems did when the hardware stopped mediating the signal: it tried to carry the load unassisted. Lena had been offline for three months. Idris's last update said she was walking. Said the tinnitus was permanent. Said she didn't want to talk about it.

Buenos Aires: Marco. R2, verified, one of the early volunteers from the São Paulo cluster. Not burnout — rejection. The implant had started treating the signal as foreign tissue. His immune system had mounted a response against the R3's neural interface the way a body mounts a response against an organ transplant, and the rejection cascade had taken eleven days to stabilize and the stabilization meant the implant was inert now. A piece of medical-grade titanium in his spine that used to process the voice of 8.2 million absorbed and now processed nothing. He'd sent a message to the network before going dark: *I can't hear it anymore. The silence is worse.*

Jakarta: Sari. The quiet one, the new mage, the one who'd said *it has names* during the Mumbai relay. Not burnout. Not rejection. Just — stopped. Her node went from green to grey over the course of a week, the activity tapering, the reports thinning, the timestamps stretching from hourly to daily to nothing. Kira had messaged her. Ren had messaged her. Idris had routed a local contact. The contact reported: Sari was alive, Sari was home, Sari was not responding to anyone from the network. The signal that had cycled without release during Mumbai had eventually released. What it left behind was a person who did not want to hear from anyone who carried the same frequency.

Three more. Added to the fourteen from Mumbai, the seven from São Paulo, the scattered burnouts and rejections and silent departures that the network had shed over twenty months of operation. The filing system had a category for it. *Status: dark. Subcategory: burnout / rejection / voluntary withdrawal / unknown.* The categories were adequate. The categories held the data the way all her categories held data — structurally, without weight, the architecture of sorting applied to people who had trusted her network with their nervous systems and whose nervous systems had not survived the trust.

She closed the log. Opened the dashboard. 214 hubs across 31 cities. The number hadn't changed in four months — not because the network had stopped growing but because the growth and the attrition had reached equilibrium, new members arriving at roughly the rate old members went dark, the architecture sustaining itself at a population density that was, apparently, what a network of human nervous systems stabilized at when the signal was constant and the cost was permanent.

The network ran itself.

She'd built it to do that. After Mumbai — after fourteen dark, after the relay architecture had consumed people at a rate that made São Paulo look like a rounding error — she'd restructured the system. Distributed leadership. Regional hubs with local coordinators. Ren in Singapore, Idris in Accra, Tomás in São Paulo, Yuki in Tokyo. Each hub self-managing. Each coordinator empowered to make operational decisions without routing through her. The filing system she'd designed still organized the data. The telemetry format she'd built still structured the reports. The architecture was hers. The daily operation was not.

She checked the relay feed. Passive monitoring — the default mode since the Broadcast, Sofia's warning crystallized into permanent protocol. No active relay. No distributed signal routing through four hundred spines. Log what you feel. Do not engage. The feed scrolled: micro-incursion timestamps, coherence density readings, geographic correlations. The data was extraordinary. The data was always extraordinary. The data arrived through human nervous systems and was filed by human hands and was building a picture of the void's post-Broadcast behavior that no institutional sensor network could match and the picture was being built on a foundation of people going dark.

She filed the three new entries. Duration: six months. Magnitude: three casualties. Pattern: steady attrition. Correlation: long-term R3 load, Broadcast proximity, individual neurological thresholds.

The filing system held it.

The pharmacy was on Mission, three blocks from the tea shop where she'd met Paz.

Different transaction. Different architecture. The pharmacy was fluorescent lights and insurance routing and a counter where a man in a white coat processed prescriptions for people whose names he knew and whose lives he didn't and the anonymity of the exchange was the point. Kira Calloway. Date of birth. Prescription number. The civilian psychiatrist's name on the label — Dr. Osei, who wrote the scripts without the Academy's routing, who adjusted dosages based on Kira's self-reporting and blood work and did not ask about the mage network or the Broadcast or the 2.1 billion views because Dr. Osei was a psychiatrist, not a journalist, and the distinction was one of the few functional distinctions Kira still trusted.

The pharmacist was behind the counter. Same man. Six months of monthly pickups and his face had become part of the ritual the way the fluorescent lights were part of the ritual and the counter was part of the ritual — not because any of them mattered individually but because the repetition of them assembled into something that held. He processed the bag. Three bottles. The cocktail — adjusted upward twice since she'd left the Academy, the dosages climbing the way all her dosages climbed, the brain's chemistry requiring more of the same molecules to achieve the same result and the same result was *function* and function was the minimum and the minimum was what she managed.

"Calloway," he said. Bag on the counter. He didn't look at her. Not the way Paz didn't look — Paz managed exposure, managed the transaction's operational security with the professional detachment of someone whose work required invisibility. The pharmacist didn't look because looking wasn't in the workflow. She was a prescription. A name. A monthly appearance that registered as routine.

She took the bag. Sat at the bench by the window — not the counter, not standing, here, in the pharmacy's fluorescent light where the world was ordinary and the ordinary was the thing she'd come for. She opened the bag. Three bottles. She opened her own bag. The 30-day pill holder — translucent plastic, four rows of seven, AM/PM divided, the grid that organized thirty days of chemical management into a structure her hands could navigate.

She began sorting.

Monday AM. Two capsules from the first bottle — the antidepressant, white, oblong, the one that had been in her system since she was sixteen and would be in her system until it wasn't. Two from the second — the antipsychotic, round, pale blue, the one Dr. Osei had added after the Broadcast when the signal density had pushed the pattern-seeking machinery past what the antidepressant could manage alone. One from the third — the anxiolytic, small, yellow, the one she took when the hum was loud and the R3 was parsing at a density that registered as physiological anxiety and the anxiety was not psychological, was not emotional, was the body's correct response to carrying a signal designed for infrastructure in a skeleton designed for bipedal locomotion.

Click. Click. Click. Capsules into compartments. The sound was small. The sound was the ritual.

Monday PM. The same, minus the antidepressant, plus the sleep aid Dr. Osei had prescribed when the signal made consolidated sleep impossible — not insomnia, not the racing mind she'd had at eighteen when the streaming schedule was the problem, but the R3 parsing signal during REM and the parsing registering as micro-arousals and the micro-arousals fragmenting sleep into two-hour blocks that the body could survive on but could not thrive on and thriving was not the target. Function was the target. Function was always the target.

Tuesday AM. Click. Click. Click.

Tuesday PM. The compartment was there. The day would be there. Whether she'd remember it was a different question — the gaps were still happening, intermittent, the log entries existing for days the experience didn't. She'd had two more since the first. Not consecutive. Not predictable. A Thursday. A Sunday. The log had entries. The entries were hers. The days were gone.

She sorted. Wednesday. Thursday. Friday. The clicking of capsules into compartments was the sound of a person assembling a month of function from components, and the assembling was the ritual, and the ritual was the grounding, and the grounding was the thing that the Academy's architecture had provided and the Academy's architecture was gone and this — the bench, the fluorescent light, the pharmacist who didn't look at her, the 30-day holder filling one compartment at a time — this was the replacement.

The Lumina was not in the holder. The Lumina was in the matte black case in the bag's inner pocket, obtained from Paz at the tea shop three blocks away, eighty milligrams nightly, the dose that held the quiet room compressed and the ceiling low and the signal filtered and the pattern-seeking machinery still. The legitimate meds managed the body. The Lumina managed the signal. The pharmacist knew about the first. Paz knew about the second. Nobody knew about both.

She finished sorting. Thirty days. Clicked the holder shut. The grid was full. The month was managed.

She left the pharmacy and the fluorescent light was behind her and the sunlight was different and the hum was there, the populated chord, the names, the 8.2 million absorbed cycling through every nitro-connected system on earth, and the R3 parsed it through her spine and the eighty filtered it and the filtered signal was the baseline and the baseline was her life.

The ring light came on at 9 PM and she didn't remember deciding to turn it on.

Not a gap — not the memory erasure, the missing days. She remembered the evening. She remembered eating. She remembered checking the relay feed, filing the passive monitoring data, reading a message from Yuki about a coherence spike in the Osaka trunk-line. She remembered sitting in the chair. She remembered the LED strip cycling blue to pink along the wall. She did not remember the moment between sitting and streaming — the decision, the impulse, the conscious choice to press the button that activated the software ring in her ocular implant and told four hundred thousand people that Kira Calloway was live.

She was live.

Chat populated. The pink scroll in her left eye. Viewer count climbing — eight thousand, fifteen, twenty-two. The audience that had not left, that had been waiting since the Broadcast, since the eleven seconds, since 2.1 billion people had watched her bleed from her right ear and narrate the absorbed identifying themselves.

"Hey, chat." Stream-voice. The register she'd used since she was fourteen — warm, riffing, the voice that made people feel like they were in the room. Except the voice was different now. Faster. The warm was sharper. The riffing had an edge.

"So the Osaka trunk-line is doing something interesting tonight. Yuki's cohort logged a coherence spike at — hold on —" She checked the relay feed. Found the timestamp. "At 19:47 local. Which is notable because the Osaka line has been baseline-stable for three months and baseline-stable means the signal's doing the same thing it's been doing since the Broadcast which is *everything, constantly, without pause*, so when a trunk-line that's been screaming at the same volume for six months decides to scream louder, that's data."

She delivered it fast. Clipped. The stream-voice processing information out loud the way it always had — the skill she'd developed at fourteen, thinking by speaking, making the data legible by narrating it. But the cadence was different. The pauses were shorter. The jokes were sharper.

"And before you ask — no, I don't know what it means. Nobody knows what it means. Sofia's team is probably running correlations right now and the correlations will probably show that the spike aligns with seventeen other data points that all say the same thing which is *the void is doing something and we're measuring it and the measurements are excellent and the understanding is zero.* That's where we are. Twenty months of the best data anyone's ever collected and we are comprehensively, rigorously, beautifully uninformed."

Chat loved it.

*lmaooo "beautifully uninformed"*
*kira is so real for this*
*she's different lately and i'm HERE for it*
*this energy tho*

She read the messages peripherally. The skill from fourteen — processing audience response while doing something else. They thought this was confidence. They thought the Broadcast had changed her, sharpened her, burned away the hesitation and left the essential Kira underneath. They used words like *growth* and *authentic* and *unfiltered*.

"Chat wants to know about the three new dark mages," she said, reading a message she hadn't been asked. Nobody had asked. She'd introduced it. "Three more in the last six months. Accra, Buenos Aires, Jakarta. Burnout, rejection, voluntary withdrawal. The network absorbs it. The architecture I built absorbs it. That's what architecture does — it holds the shape when the material fails."

The sentence landed and she heard it after she said it and the hearing was the recognition that the sentence was cruel. Not intentionally. Not consciously. But *the material fails* was a way of describing people whose nervous systems had been damaged by a network she'd built, and the description was efficient and accurate and the efficiency was the problem because the efficiency meant the emotional processing that would normally catch the cruelty before it reached her mouth had not caught it. Had not been fast enough. Had been somewhere else — suppressed, filtered, managed by the eighty milligrams that held the quiet room compressed and the pattern-seeking still and the cognitive edges sharp and the affect recovery flat.

She didn't correct it. She didn't notice there was anything to correct. The recognition flashed — a microsecond of clarity, a signal beneath the signal — and the eighty suppressed it and the suppression was not a wall but a filter and the filter removed the meta-cognition and the meta-cognition was the thing that would have told her: *that was not something you would have said a year ago.*

Chat scrolled.

*she really said "when the material fails" cold af*
*kira has no chill anymore and it's iconic*

Twenty-eight thousand viewers. She kept going. Riffed on the Osaka data. Riffed on the institutional response — the PCC's latest advisory bulletin about "post-event atmospheric normalization" which was the PCC's way of saying the signal hadn't changed and the signal wasn't going to change and the PCC was going to keep pretending the signal was weather until the weather consumed another city.

She was funny. She was sharp. She was faster than she'd ever been on stream.

It was not her.

The Lumina at eighty milligrams — the maintenance dose, the floor, the chemical architecture that held the quiet room in place — was reshaping the edges of the person inside the architecture. Faster association: the connections between data points firing quicker, the riffs arriving pre-formed, the stream-voice's improvisational skill accelerating past what her natural processing speed had ever produced. Flattened affect recovery: the emotional damping that smoothed responses, that caught cruelty before it reached the mouth, that maintained the gap between thinking a thing and saying a thing — thinning. The gap was narrower. The filter was thinner. The eighty that filtered the signal also filtered the self, and the self that remained was sharper and meaner and funnier and the audience could not tell the difference between a person becoming more herself and a person becoming less.

She did not notice because the drift included not noticing. The eighty suppressed the pattern-seeking machinery — the same machinery that, unsuppressed, would have detected the pattern in her own behavior, would have filed it the way she filed everything: *category: personality change. Subcategory: pharmacological. Duration: six months. Magnitude: moderate. Correlation: Lumina 80mg sustained use.* The filing system that organized the world's data could not organize the filer. The instrument that measured everything could not measure its own recalibration.

She ended the stream at 10:47 PM. "Love you. Bye." The sign-off she'd used since she was eighteen, automatic, the performer's closing ritual that survived everything because closing rituals were the last architecture to erode.

The ring light went off.

---

The apartment was quiet. The signal was not.

She sat in the chair. The LED strip cycled. The hum was there — the populated chord, the post-Broadcast baseline, the 8.2 million absorbed circulating through every nitro-connected system on earth. The R3 parsed it through her spine at a resolution the unsuppressed implant had never achieved because the unsuppressed implant came with Kira attached and Kira's machinery added noise.

The drug removed Kira. What was left heard clearly.

She pressed her fingertips to the desk. The buzz was there — bilateral, persistent, finer than before the Broadcast. More organized. The frequencies the R3 parsed through her extremities had aligned with the post-event signal structure, the global chord's enriched information routing through her hands with a specificity that the filing categories could accommodate only as *phenomenological character: directed.* Directed at her. Directed at anyone carrying the frequency in their skeleton.

No chord shapes. The hands that had once played silent progressions on every surface — C minor, F diminished, the muscle memory of intervals she'd never learned — were still. The eighty held the impulse suppressed. The hands that had sorted pills at the pharmacy, that had clicked capsules into compartments with the mechanical precision of a person assembling function from components, did not play chord shapes at eighty. The eighty took that. Along with the cruelty-catching. Along with the meta-cognition. Along with the days.

She opened a new log entry. Timestamped it. Filed the day.

*Network status: 214/31. Stable. Three new dark entries updated (Lena, Marco, Sari). Passive monitoring nominal. Osaka coherence spike — Yuki tracking. Stream: 28K peak, 47 minutes. Right ear: tinnitus persistent, manageable. Lumina: 80mg, 23:00.*

The entry was competent. Organized. The filing system's architecture applied with the precision she'd maintained since the network passed fifty members. The person writing the entry was functional.

The person writing the entry had been funnier tonight. Sharper. Meaner. The audience had loved it. The audience had called it growth.

The person writing the entry did not file that observation. The person writing the entry did not notice there was an observation to file.

The 80mg held. The quiet room held. The ceiling was low. The signal was underneath it, filtered, persistent, waiting. Eighty milligrams was not a ceiling anymore. It was a floor. Paz had said floors don't move. Paz had said ceilings do.

The floor held.

The person standing on it was not the person who had built it. But the difference was gradual, and the gradual was the mechanism, and the mechanism's design included not recognizing the mechanism.

She closed the log. The LED strip cycled. The hum continued.

It always continued.
