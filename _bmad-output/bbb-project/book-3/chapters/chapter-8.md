## Draft v1

She counted before she opened her eyes.

Creds: one thousand and forty-one. Room: paid through the end of the month — still nine creds per day, still the building that did not verify credentials, still the concrete floor and the low ceiling and the ventilation shaft where the light came down grey and sufficient. Food: four days of staples, plus the market. Implant: functional. Cracked housing, degraded overlay, the frozen notification still there after eighteen months because the credential server that could dismiss it had been decommissioned when the district it served was reclassified as geography.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

She opened her eyes. The ceiling was the same.

The creds were different. One thousand and forty-one was more than she had ever saved in the Sump — more than the implant upgrade had cost, back when the implant upgrade was the horizon of her wanting. She could buy the upgrade now. The thought arrived and she let it pass the way she let most thoughts pass — noted, filed, not acted on. The implant she had was cracked and degraded and it itched behind her left ear when the weather shifted, and it was the implant that had been with her in the annex. She did not want a new one. She did not examine why.

She stood. Made rice. Boiled water for tea — a new addition, three months old, because Fen on the third floor had given her a tin of oolong and she had accepted it without calculating the repayment and the acceptance had become a habit and the habit had become a taste she chose.

Two messages on the overlay, rendered in the degraded resolution she had learned to read around.

The first was from the testimony network. A name she recognized — Jian, who had been in the Sump's Block 7 produce market, who had filed her testimony four months ago through the network Nuwa had helped build from the infrastructure Kira's mages maintained. Jian's message was short: *New family. Arrived yesterday. Three people. Need routing.*

The second was from a node in the relocation network — the other system, the invisible one, built from Fuxi's data chip and her own knowledge of which walls were masonry and which corridors did not carry conduit paths. The message was coordinates. A sacrifice district on the eastern margin, still designated, still active in the infrastructure registry even though six months ago the hum had spoken every name it carried and the designation still held. A family needed to move before the next scheduled throughput adjustment.

She washed her bowl. One bowl. One pair of chopsticks. One mug. The inventory had not expanded. The creds had. The room had not. She had not moved. Moving required credentials, and credentials required entering the system again, and entering the system again required deciding that the system's next offer would be different from its last one.

She put on her jacket and left.

---

The district had a name now. The tourists called it the Verge.

Six months ago it had been the eastern margin of Mid-Level 4 — a corridor of converted warehouses and unlicensed residential blocks where people without credentials could find rooms and the system's oversight was thin enough to breathe through. Nuwa had walked these streets daily. They had been quiet. Functional. The kind of neighborhood that existed because the city's administrative grid had gaps, and people who fell through the grid collected in the gaps the way water collected in low places.

The gaps had been found.

She came around the corner of the old textile depot and the Verge opened in front of her — three blocks of neon and noise and bodies that had not been here four months ago. Vendor stalls lined both sides of the corridor, their displays projecting into the walkway at competing brightness levels: proximity tours, artifact authentication, observation platform reservations, frame rentals for the "full immersion experience." The light was layered — the baseline orange of the conduit glow that bled up from the infrastructure below, overlaid with the commercial blue-white of vendor projections, overlaid again with the pink and green of unlicensed signage that crawled across building facades like luminescent mold.

The people were louder than the signs.

Tourists. She could identify them by augmentation density alone — the Spire-born and the off-city visitors carried more hardware than anyone who lived here could afford. P-Frames so thin they looked painted on, the titanium and carbon-polymer chassis moving with their wearers like second skeletons. Ocular implants that glowed faintly at the temples — not the salvage-grade disc behind Nuwa's ear but subdermal units, seamless, the kind that rendered a full-spectrum overlay without the dead zones and character artifacts she navigated daily. One woman passing her had so much visible modification that the organic remainder — a strip of skin at the throat, the curve of an ear — looked like an afterthought. More metal than bone. The metal was polished. The bone was decorative.

Nuwa walked through them the way she had always walked through crowds. Shoulders forward, chin down, occupying as little space as possible. The posture was Sump architecture — low ceilings, contested space, the body's learned response to an environment designed for density rather than comfort. Here, in the Verge's wide commercial corridor, the posture made her invisible. The tourists flowed around her the way water flows around a stone too small to notice.

She noticed them. She always noticed.

The vendor stalls sold the Sump.

The Sump was gone — reclassified, archived per Municipal Code 7.14.2(b). What the vendors sold was the idea of it, processed into merchandise. Conduit fragments mounted on display stands — "authenticated Anchor Zone salvage," the tags read, though Nuwa could see from the oxidation patterns that half of them were standard Mid-Level infrastructure cut to size. Wet-film samples in sealed vials, amber-tinted, labeled with block numbers and dates that may or may not have corresponded to actual absorption events. Photographs printed on polymer sheets: the Sump skyline before BLACKWEIR, the Anchor Zone perimeter after, the violet glow of conduit paths that had carried people into their own walls.

Fifteen creds for a photograph of Block 14.

She looked at it. The angle was from the east, from the walkway between Buildings 11 and 14 — the walkway where the grating had gone violet, where the person in front had stopped running, where the second person had turned back and gotten three steps before the railing took them. The photograph showed the walkway intact. Pre-BLACKWEIR. The laundry lines were visible. Mrs. Huang's laundry, dark shapes against nothing, caught in a photograph that someone was selling next to vials of amber liquid and fragments of pipe.

The vendor — a man with a military-surplus L-Frame modified for commercial use, the servos whining at the joints — looked up from his display.

"Fifteen for the print. Forty for a proximity tour. I can get you within two hundred meters of the perimeter — masonry route, safe approach, twenty minutes of observation time."

She did not answer. She kept walking. The vendor's attention slid off her and onto the next tourist the way attention always slid off her — frictionless, unremarked. The invisibility she had chosen was functioning.

The observation platforms were ahead. Three of them, stacked at different heights along the eastern face of a converted warehouse, angled toward the Anchor Zone perimeter four hundred meters south. The platforms had gone up two months ago — unlicensed construction, welded from salvaged infrastructure, the kind of structure that would have been demolished in a week if the district had functional building code enforcement. It did not. The platforms charged forty creds per hour. The view was the Anchor Zone's boundary: a visible discontinuity in the city's fabric where the conduit glow shifted from orange to violet, where the buildings still stood but the windows were dark and the walkways were empty and the air had a shimmer that the Feed called "residual coherence" and Nuwa called the place where everyone she knew had died.

The platforms were full.

She could see them from the corridor — silhouettes against the violet haze, holding devices, recording. Some stood in silence. Some pointed. A group of four were laughing about something, their P-Frames catching the glow, the polished metal of their augmentations reflecting the light that bled from infrastructure that had absorbed 8.2 million people.

The platforms were controlled by a crew she had noticed three weeks ago and tracked since — eight, maybe ten, operating out of the basement of the converted warehouse. Strictly commercial. They had claimed the observation platforms the way territorial operators claimed any unregulated asset in the margins: by being there first, by enforcing access, by making it clear that the forty-cred fee was not optional. She had seen one of them break a man's wrist at the base of Platform 2 — a tourist who had tried to climb without paying. The breaking was efficient. Professional. The tourist's P-Frame had absorbed most of the impact, the servos locking to protect the joint, but the operator had known where the frame's coverage gaps were. Sump knowledge. The kind of knowledge you acquired by growing up in a district where frames were repaired, not purchased, and the gaps in the hardware were common information.

She routed along the masonry wall opposite the platforms. The wall was old construction — load-bearing, no conduit paths, the kind of wall that did not carry the hum into the stone. Her body followed the route without instruction. Eighteen months of Mid-Level life had not overwritten the Sump's map. The masonry routes were permanent.

---

"Nuwa."

She stopped.

The voice came from behind her — not the corridor's ambient noise, not a vendor's pitch, not the mechanical whine of a tourist's frame. Her name. Spoken by someone who knew it.

She turned. A woman. Mid-twenties, dark hair, an ocular implant at her left temple that was a generation newer than Nuwa's salvage-grade disc but still visibly external — not Spire hardware. The woman wore a jacket with no corporate insignia, carried no visible frame, stood with the careful posture of someone who had learned to navigate spaces where credentials were checked and hers might not pass.

"You're Nuwa. From the testimony network."

The woman's face was familiar the way faces on the overlay were familiar — a name attached to a message attached to a filing in the system Nuwa had helped build. She searched. Found it. Yue. Filed her testimony five months ago. Block 9 — the block that had emptied two months before BLACKWEIR, the one the Feed had called "relocated."

"I saw your name on the routing list," Yue said. "For the family on the eastern margin. I can help with transit — I know the checkpoint gaps on Level 3."

Nuwa looked at her. Yue was standing in the middle of the Verge's commercial corridor, speaking the vocabulary of the relocation network — routing, checkpoint gaps, transit — in a voice that was not quiet enough. Two tourists passed within arm's reach. A vendor three stalls down was watching with the incurious attention of someone who tracked all foot traffic as a professional habit.

"Not here," Nuwa said.

They moved. Nuwa led — along the masonry wall, through the service alley behind the textile depot, into the gap between buildings where the commercial noise dropped and the air cooled and the hum rose through the floor in the low register that meant trunk-line proximity. She stopped where the alley met a utility access door — metal, locked, but the lock was the kind that could be bypassed with a transit card and a piece of wire, and Nuwa had bypassed it eleven times in the last four months because the stairwell behind it connected to the sub-level corridors that ran beneath the Verge without biometric checkpoints.

"The family is three people," Nuwa said. "Two adults, one child. The child is six. They need to move before the Thursday throughput adjustment — the eastern margin sacrifice district is still in the active registry and the adjustment will increase conduit load in their building by 15%. Their building is metal-framed."

"I can route them through the Level 3 gap at shift change. Fourteen hundred. The checkpoint cycles every nine minutes — three-minute window."

Nuwa filed this. Yue knew the checkpoint timing. Yue knew the vocabulary. Yue was from Block 9 — the block that had been emptied before BLACKWEIR, which meant Yue had been relocated before the relocation became absorption, which meant Yue had been navigating the margins longer than Nuwa had.

"Fourteen hundred," Nuwa said. "I'll bring them to the Level 3 junction at thirteen fifty. The child will need to be carried — the corridor has a half-flight of stairs with no railing."

"I know the stairs."

Nuwa looked at her. Yue looked back. Two women in a service alley, coordinating the movement of three people through the gaps in a city that had consumed its lowest district and was now selling photographs of the consumption for fifteen creds apiece.

"Thank you," Nuwa said. The word was still difficult. Not because gratitude was foreign — because gratitude implied a ledger, and ledgers required balancing, and she did not know how to balance a ledger with someone who was offering help because the help was needed and not because the help would be repaid.

Yue nodded. Left through the service alley. Nuwa stood in the gap between buildings and listened to the hum through the floor — the trunk-line resonance, deeper here, carrying the frequencies that had changed after BLACKWEIR and changed again six months ago when every name in the infrastructure spoke at once. The hum was fuller now. More textured. She did not have vocabulary for what the texture contained. She did not need vocabulary. She knew what it contained. 8.2 million people had been absorbed into the infrastructure of the Sump. The hum carried them the way conduit carried current — the resonance of consciousness integrated into the grid's architecture.

She had counted forty-seven of them. Forty-seven names she recognized when the hum spoke them — names from Block 14, from the textile plant, from the produce market on Block 7, from the shared water point on Level 2, from the building she had lived in since the day she was born. Forty-seven out of 8.2 million. Her arithmetic, applied to the only data she had: the names she knew, held against the names she did not.

The tourists on the observation platforms did not count. They photographed. They recorded. They purchased authenticated conduit fragments and sealed vials of amber liquid and twenty minutes of observation time at forty creds per hour. They stood at the boundary of the thing that had consumed a district and they experienced it as content.

She walked back through the service alley. Past the textile depot. Past the vendor with the military-surplus frame and the photographs of Block 14. Past the neon and the noise and the bodies with more metal than bone who had come to the margins of the Anchor Zone to look at what the city had done to its lowest residents and call the looking a "proximity experience."

---

The family was in a sub-level room on the eastern margin. Two adults — a man and a woman, both Sump-displaced, both working unlicensed shifts at a conduit maintenance subcontractor that did not ask for credentials because credentials cost more than labor. The child was six. She sat on the floor with a toy that was a piece of conduit insulation bent into a shape — four legs, a head, adhesive tape at the joints.

The building was metal-framed. Nuwa had checked this three days ago — walked the corridors, pressed her hand to the walls, felt for the hum's character. Metal framing meant conduit paths in the walls. Conduit paths meant conductivity. Conductivity meant that if the Thursday throughput adjustment increased the load in this sector, the building would carry more of the grid's resonance through its bones, and the people inside would carry more of the grid's resonance through theirs.

She did not explain this to the family. She said: "There is a room on Level 3. Masonry building. Nine creds a day. I can take you there tomorrow at fourteen hundred."

The woman looked at the man. The man looked at Nuwa. The child looked at her toy.

"Who are you?" the man asked.

The question. The same question Fen had asked eighteen months ago, sitting on the floor of Unit 9 surrounded by bok choy and spring onions. The question people in the margins asked each other — not because the answer mattered but because the asking was a door.

"I am from the Sump," Nuwa said. "Block 14."

The man's expression settled. The recognition of shared geography, shared infrastructure, shared knowledge of what it meant to live in a building where the hum was in the walls and the walls were in the bones and the bones were in the system's inventory.

"We'll be ready," the woman said.

Nuwa left the coordinates for the Level 3 junction on their overlay — routed through the relocation network's encrypted channel, the one Fuxi's data chip had provided the architecture for, the one that ran on infrastructure the Kindling maintained. She did not stay. Staying required the kind of presence that generated memory, and memory generated recognition, and recognition was the thing that had happened in the Verge when Yue had spoken her name in a voice that was not quiet enough.

She was in both systems now.

The testimony network made her visible. Forty-seven names, filed, timestamped, distributed through Kira's mage-maintained infrastructure to an audience that included journalists, researchers, institutional observers, and the Feed's algorithmic attention. The testimonies she had helped collect — Sump survivors, Block by Block, name by name — were data. The data had her routing address attached to it. The data made her legible to the city's administrative grid in a way that her deleted residential record and her temporary ID and her room in a building without credential verification had been designed to avoid.

The relocation network made her useful. Families moved. Safe zones marked. Sacrifice district topology mapped from Fuxi's chip and her own body's knowledge of which walls carried the hum and which did not. The work was invisible — no filing, no timestamps, no audience. The families did not know her name. The corridors she routed them through did not record their passage. The masonry buildings she placed them in did not report their occupancy.

Visible and invisible. Filed and unrecorded. Both systems operating through the same woman, the same cracked implant, the same hands with the chemical staining that glowed faintly under certain light — the iridescent residue of nitro-reactive dyes from a textile reclamation plant that did not exist anymore, in a district that did not exist anymore, in a life that the system had archived per Municipal Code 7.14.2(b).

She walked home along the masonry wall. The route was automatic — eighteen months of Mid-Level life layered on top of twenty-four years of Sump life, the body's map updated but not overwritten. The hum was in the floor. The hum was always in the floor. It was quieter here than in the Verge, where the commercial infrastructure added its own frequencies to the baseline, but it was present. It would always be present. The forty-seven names she had counted were in it, circulating with the 8.2 million she had not counted, and the hum did not distinguish between the ones she knew and the ones she did not.

Her room. The concrete floor. The ventilation shaft light, grey and sufficient.

She put the kettle on. Oolong — Fen's tin, nearly empty. She would need to buy more. The thought arrived as arithmetic: oolong, twelve creds for a tin at the Level 3 market, the stall with the woman who did not ask for credentials. She let the arithmetic run. It was her mind's first language. Everything else was translation.

On the overlay, Jian's message was still open. *New family. Arrived yesterday. Three people. Need routing.*

She composed a reply. Short. Practical. The coordinates of the masonry building on Level 3. The checkpoint timing Yue had confirmed. The instruction she gave every family she routed through the relocation network, the same four words, the same arithmetic of survival reduced to its most actionable form:

*Bring water. Stay inside.*

The notification sat in her peripheral vision. Eighteen months. The credential server was gone. The system that had generated the classification was archived. The classification persisted.

*Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

She had been assigned to both systems. She had built one and been built into the other. The testimony network filed her name alongside the forty-seven names she had counted and the 8.2 million she had not. The relocation network erased her presence from every corridor she walked and every family she moved. She was legible and illegible. Counted and uncounted. The woman on the observation platform's margin and the woman in the service alley. Both. Neither.

She drank the tea. It was good. Fen's oolong, the last of the tin, the taste she had chosen because someone had offered it without terms and she had accepted it without calculating the cost.

Outside, the Verge's neon bled into the corridor light — pink and green and commercial blue-white, the color of a city selling proximity to the thing it had done. The hum was in the walls. The hum was in the tea. The hum was in the forty-seven names and the 8.2 million names and the three people in the sub-level room on the eastern margin who would be moved tomorrow through checkpoint gaps and masonry corridors to a building where the walls did not carry the signal into the stone.

She washed the mug. One mug. The inventory had not expanded.

Both systems held.
