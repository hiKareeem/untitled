## Draft v1

The fingertips came and went.

Not numb exactly — *absent*. As if the nerve endings had decided, independently and without consulting the rest of her, to stop reporting. It happened mid-stream now, three or four times a session, a quiet dropout in sensation that lasted two or three seconds and then returned as if nothing had happened. Her left hand would go flat on the desk — not a flinch, not a tell, just a hand resting on a surface the way anyone's hand might rest on a surface — and then the feeling would come back and she'd pick up the controller and keep talking and chat wouldn't notice because chat never noticed and that was the whole point.

She noticed.

She noticed the way she noticed everything her body did wrong — a beat behind the event, after the performance had already covered it, the delayed report arriving like a memo from a department she'd stopped funding. *That happened again. Third time this stream. It's getting more frequent.* She filed it. She filed everything. The filing was the coping mechanism — categorize, shelve, move on. If the body's complaints were just data points in a log she wasn't going to read, they couldn't become a pattern she'd have to think about.

The stream ended at its usual hour. She raided Mako again — Mako was always live, Mako was a machine — and said the words she always said, the warm ones, the genuine ones, the ones that cost her something she couldn't name. Love you. Bye. Go. Get out.

Eleven seconds.

The performer left. The posture collapsed. She pulled her knees up and became small in the chair and the ring light was still on and the LED strip was still glowing and she sat in the silence that landed after every broadcast like a note released after being held too long.

Tuesday. The forty was every night — had been for fourteen months, since the titration settled. After stream. Before bed. She didn't think about it the way she didn't think about locking the door. Part of the shutdown. Part of the routine. You didn't count how often you brushed your teeth.

The bump was newer. Fifteen milligrams, on the nights the quiet room wasn't quiet enough. It had been a once-a-week thing — a bad stream, a rough episode, the kind of night where the hum leaked through and the forty wasn't enough to hold the walls. Now it was twice a week. Maybe three times last week, but two of those were back-to-back stream days and the episodes had been worse so that didn't count. She was in control. She was always in control.

She went to the bathroom. Kit behind the mirror. Scale, vial, straw. Forty milligrams. The cold burn. The subtraction.

The quiet room.

She cleaned the straw, zipped the case, put it back. Crossed to the couch — not the streaming chair, the couch, the one buried under laundry that she'd cleared a Kira-sized space in three days ago and hadn't re-cleared since. She pulled the blanket over her legs and opened her left eye's feed — not chat, not the post-stream analytics, but the private message queue that had been pulsing at the edge of her HUD for the last four hours.

She'd been ignoring it. She'd been ignoring it the way she'd been ignoring Priya's message for weeks — not deleting, not opening, just letting the notification sit in her peripheral vision like a piece of furniture she'd learned to walk around. The group chat had moved on. Dex posted memes. Tomás complained about São Paulo's transit system. Suki sent terse one-line updates from deployment that read like poetry if you squinted. Normal. The texture of people who'd known each other since they were children.

Priya hadn't brought it up again. That was worse than if she had.

---

The private messages weren't from the group chat.

Four of them. Staggered across the last ten days, from mages she knew by name but not by habit — Academy contacts, people she'd met at conferences or through the streamer circuit, the kind of connections that existed in the space between strangers and friends. Not her people. Not the group chat. These were mages who'd found her through the network the way signals find antennas — because she was visible, because she was famous, because two hundred thousand followers meant that when a mage needed someone who might *understand*, Kira Calloway was the name that came up.

She opened the first one. Ren Matsuda — Kyoto program, graduated two years ahead of her, field-certified but working research now. She'd met him once at a Varma guest lecture. Quiet. Serious. The kind of mage who documented everything.

*Kira — sorry for the cold message. I don't know who else to ask. During the Neo-Shanghai incursion three weeks ago (the Mid-Levels one, containment unit deployed), I was monitoring remotely from Kyoto. Standard observation protocol — implant tuned to atmospheric resonance, passive mode. At 11:28 UTC my R3 registered a signal I've never encountered. Not the usual ambient noise. Not the hum. Something with structure. It repeated four times at what felt like regular intervals before the incursion was contained. I ran a self-diagnostic afterward — implant nominal. I filed it as anomalous and moved on. Then I heard from two others who were monitoring the same event. They felt it too. Same timestamp. Same structure. I don't know what to do with this. I thought you might.*

She read it twice. The Lumina was working — the quiet room held, the pattern-seeking machinery was still, the part of her brain that wanted to reach for the data and *pull* was resting. She could read this and not react. She could read this and file it.

She opened the second message. Lena Okafor — Lagos program, active deployment, someone Kira had interviewed on stream two years ago about field conditions. Direct. No preamble.

*K — weird one. You know how the hum changes during incursions? Mine did something different last month. During containment ops in the Accra District corridor, my implant picked up what I can only describe as a pattern. Not the usual spike-and-fade. A repeating sequence. Like something was trying to — I don't know. Transmit? That sounds insane. Forget I said that. But the sequence repeated. Three times. Same intervals. My squad lead logged it as resonance feedback. I don't think that's what it was.*

The third was shorter. Yuki Tanaka — Osaka, field mage, someone Kira had never spoken to directly but who followed her stream.

*Calloway-san — I experienced something during the Neo-Shanghai event that I cannot reconcile with my training. My implant registered structured input during the incursion window. Not random. Structured. I have been reluctant to report it through official channels because the Academy's position on structured auditory phenomena is clear. I am contacting you because your stream discussion of deployment schedules suggested you might be open to unofficial conversation.*

The fourth was a voice message — audio only, thirty-one seconds. A man she didn't recognize, identifier stripped, routing through an anonymized relay. His voice was steady in the way that voices are steady when the person behind them is trying very hard not to sound afraid.

*"I don't know if you'll get this. I'm — I was monitoring during the Mid-Levels event. The one three weeks ago. My implant — look, I've been doing this for nine years and I've never felt anything like it. It wasn't noise. It was organized. I timed it — four repetitions, approximately ninety seconds apart. The intervals were consistent. I don't know what that means. The official line is resonance feedback. It's not resonance feedback. I know what resonance feedback feels like. This was something else. Please don't share this. I just needed someone to know."*

She played it twice. Then a third time, because her left eye had auto-transcribed it and the transcription was clean and the words on the screen were the same words she'd just heard and reading them was different from hearing them — reading them made them data, made them text, made them something she could scroll past. Hearing the voice made them a person.

She closed the message queue.

---

The apartment was dark. The ring light was still on — she should turn it off, she never turned it off — and the LED strip cast its blue-to-pink gradient across the ceiling in a soft wash that made the room look like it was underwater. She pulled the blanket tighter. The Lumina was holding. The quiet room was quiet. She didn't have to think about this.

Her left eye pulsed. The group chat.

Dex: *anyone else get that Academy health bulletin? "Structured auditory phenomena are artifacts of implant resonance feedback, not external communication." Very reassuring. Very normal thing to send unprompted to the entire mage registry.*

Priya: *I saw it. Interesting timing.*

Tomás: *what's "structured auditory phenomena"?*

Dex: *it's when your spine plays music and the Academy says it's your fault*

Tomás: *💀💀*

Priya: *It's when multiple mages report the same signal during the same event and the institution responds with a press release instead of an investigation.*

Dex: *damn Priya woke up and chose violence*

Suki: *The bulletin is standard. They issue them after any incursion with widespread implant activation. It's protocol.*

Priya: *Suki. Respectfully. When have they ever used the word "structured" in a health bulletin before?*

Suki didn't respond to that.

Kira read the exchange twice. The Lumina was holding — the room was quiet, the machinery was still — but the quiet room had a window now and through the window she could see the shape of something she didn't want to look at. Not clearly. Not the whole thing. Just the edge. The Academy had sent a health bulletin specifically addressing structured auditory phenomena. Which meant enough mages had reported it that the institution felt the need to respond. Which meant the four messages in her private queue weren't anomalies. They were a sample.

She touched the back of her neck. The hum was there. It was always there. But the thickness that had started weeks ago — the splitting, the harmonics she couldn't resolve — was different now. Not louder. Not thicker. *Tighter*. As if the chord had been retuned and the overtones were no longer bleeding into each other but aligning. Organizing. She didn't have a word for what the difference was. She had a feeling — a sensation in the bone where the implant sat, a quality of vibration that she'd been carrying for weeks now without examining because examining it meant —

She pulled the blanket over her shoulders. Drew her knees up. Became small.

The group chat had moved on. Dex was posting a clip of someone's cat walking across a keyboard during a stream. Tomás was rating the cat's content strategy. Normal. Warm. The texture of people being people.

Priya sent a private message. Not to the group. To Kira.

*I know you saw my message three weeks ago. I know you felt it too. You don't have to say anything. But I need you to know: two other mages contacted me after that night. All Academy. All describing the same thing. Structured. Repeating. During incursions. The Academy just sent a bulletin telling us it's resonance feedback. It's not resonance feedback, Kira. You know it's not.*

---

She sat with it.

The Lumina was holding — the quiet room was still quiet, the edges were still pushed back, the forty milligrams was doing what forty milligrams did. But the data was assembling itself anyway. Not because the pattern-seeking machinery had broken through the chemical barrier. Because the pattern was simple enough that she didn't need the machinery.

Priya's pull. 11:30 UTC. Her scream. Same timestamp.

Four private messages. Different mages, different locations, same event. Structured signal. Repeating intervals. Approximately ninety seconds.

The Academy health bulletin. "Structured auditory phenomena are artifacts of implant resonance feedback, not external communication." Sent to the entire mage registry. Unprompted. After enough mages reported something that the institution felt the need to define what it wasn't.

She didn't need to be a pattern-seeker to see this. She just needed to be a person who could count.

Her fingers were doing the thing — the silent chord shapes on the blanket, pressing into the fabric, finding progressions she hadn't consciously chosen. D minor. A diminished. G suspended. The fingers knew something her mind hadn't assembled yet. The fingers always knew first. That was the implant's gift — or its curse, depending on whether you believed the interface was translating sensation into music or music into sensation or whether the distinction mattered at all when your spine had been wired to a frequency you couldn't turn off since you were ten.

She opened Ren's message again. Read it a third time. *Something with structure. It repeated four times at what felt like regular intervals.*

Lena's: *A repeating sequence. Like something was trying to — I don't know. Transmit?*

The anonymous voice: *Four repetitions, approximately ninety seconds apart. The intervals were consistent.*

She didn't know what it meant. She wasn't going to pretend she did. She didn't have training for this — the Academy had taught her to suppress the scream, not to analyze it. She was a twenty-year-old streamer with numb fingertips and a nightly forty milligrams and a hum in her spine that was getting tighter, and she didn't know what *tighter* meant except that it wasn't what it used to be and the people who were supposed to explain it were sending health bulletins instead of answers.

But she could count.

Four messages in her inbox. Two more who'd contacted Priya. Priya herself. Her.

However she counted it, it wasn't an anomaly.

She stared at the ceiling. The blue-to-pink gradient shifted slowly — the LED strip's default cycle, sixty seconds per transition, the same ambient loop she'd installed three years ago and never changed. The light moved across the ceiling the way the hum moved through her spine — constant, cyclical, so familiar it had become invisible until something changed its quality and the familiarity cracked.

The Academy said it was resonance feedback. The Academy had trained her to suppress the scream at fourteen — *control is silence, if they can't hear it, it isn't happening*. The Academy had fitted the implant at ten while everyone smiled. The Academy had opinions about everything and the opinions were always the same opinion: that whatever the mages felt was the mages' problem, generated internally, managed individually, and definitely, certainly, absolutely not coming from somewhere else.

She didn't know if it was coming from somewhere else. She didn't know enough to know that. But she knew what resonance feedback felt like — she'd been living with it for a decade — and the thing in her spine wasn't that. The tightening. The alignment. The sense that the chord was resolving toward something she couldn't hear yet but could feel in the bone.

Not resonance feedback. Something else.

She wasn't going to figure out what. Not tonight. Not with the forty holding the edges back and the quiet room intact and the part of her that wanted to *reach* for the data resting. She was going to sleep. She was going to stream tomorrow. She was going to maintain the schedule and maintain the performance and maintain the line between using it and needing it that she'd been standing on since the titration settled, and she was still on the right side, she was certain, she was *almost* certain, because the forty was just the forty and the fifteen was only twice a week and she was fine.

She was fine.

She opened Ren's message. Typed:

*I hear you. I don't know what it means yet either. But you're not the only one. Can I share what you told me — anonymized — with the others who reached out?*

Sent.

Opened Lena's message. Typed the same thing, adjusted for Lena's directness:

*Got your message. Others have reported the same thing. Same structure, same intervals, same event. I'm trying to figure out if this is bigger than anecdotal. Can I connect your account with the others? No names unless you say so.*

Sent.

She copied the essential elements into a note to Yuki. More formal, matching the register:

*Tanaka-san — thank you for reaching out. I can confirm that your experience is not isolated. Multiple mages have reported structured input during the same incursion window. I am attempting to gather accounts. Your identity will remain confidential unless you authorize otherwise.*

Sent.

She didn't respond to the anonymous voice message. There was no return address. But she saved it. Moved it from the message queue to her personal archive — the same archive she used for stream assets and contract drafts and the folder labeled MISC that contained nothing miscellaneous and everything she didn't want to file honestly.

Then she opened Priya's private message. Read it again.

*I know you saw my message three weeks ago. I know you felt it too.*

She started typing. Stopped. Her fingers hovered over nothing — the interface was neural, the typing was a metaphor, a phantom habit from before the ocular implant made physical keyboards obsolete for anything except streaming aesthetics and personal taste. But the fingers still moved when she was thinking. Still found the shapes. Still pressed silent chords into whatever surface was nearest.

She typed:

*Yeah. I felt it.*

Three words. Not four. She'd edited one out — the *too* felt like too much, felt like confirmation of something she wasn't ready to confirm, felt like joining rather than acknowledging. *I felt it* was a statement. *I felt it too* was solidarity. She wasn't ready for solidarity. She was ready for three words and a period.

She sent it.

The quiet room held. The Lumina held. The hum held its tighter note in her spine and she sat in the streaming chair she'd migrated to without noticing — the couch was for collapsing, the chair was for doing things, and sending four messages was doing something even if the something was small and insufficient and late.

She should turn off the ring light. She didn't.

She pulled up the Academy health bulletin on her HUD. Read it in full. "Structured auditory phenomena are artifacts of implant resonance feedback, not external communication. Mages experiencing persistent or unusual auditory events should consult their assigned Academy health liaison. Self-diagnosis is not recommended. Peer discussion of unverified phenomena may contribute to anticipatory anxiety and is discouraged under Section 14.3 of the Academy Communications Policy."

*Peer discussion of unverified phenomena is discouraged.*

She read that line three times. Then she closed the bulletin and opened the four messages again — Ren, Lena, Yuki, the anonymous voice — and read them side by side and the tightness in her spine matched the tightness in her chest and neither of them was resonance feedback and neither of them was something she could name.

She didn't know what it was. She wasn't going to pretend she did. But mages across multiple programs had felt the same structure during the same event and the Academy's response was a health bulletin that said *stop talking to each other* and that was an answer to a question nobody had asked yet, which meant someone, somewhere, had already asked it and been told to stop.

She touched the back of her neck. The hum. The tightness. The chord that was resolving toward something she couldn't hear.

She wasn't going to figure it out tonight.

She saved the messages. All of them. Moved them to the archive folder — the real one, not MISC, the one she'd started the night Priya's timestamp matched her scream and she'd closed the chat and taken the bump instead of thinking about it. It had been empty for three weeks. It wasn't empty now.

She turned off the ring light.

The apartment went dark except for the pink glow of her left eye — the implant, the HUD, the interface that never turned off. She sat in the chair and the hum sat in her spine and outside the window the Bay was dark and the conduits beneath the streets hummed at frequencies she could feel through the building, through the floor, through the architecture of her skeleton into the place where the R3 sat against the bone and carried a signal that was not resonance feedback and was not random and was not something she could ignore anymore even though she was going to try.

She was going to try.

The quiet room was still quiet. The forty milligrams was holding. She was in control. She was almost certain she was in control.

She closed her eyes. The hum held. Tighter now. Waiting.
