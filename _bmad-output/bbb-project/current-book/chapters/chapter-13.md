# The Pattern

She checked the dead-hand timer before she checked anything else. Forty-one hours. The address had pinged clean yesterday, the seventy-two-hour window reset, and somewhere in Black Babel's automated architecture a sealed data package continued to exist independent of her — holding the weight of sixteen names and everything she'd compiled around them. Two missed windows and the data would move on its own, delivered to three addresses she'd set and Talia had never seen.

She set the comm on the desk beside the terminal and sat with the number. Forty-one. Enough time to descend to the Resonance District, make the exchange, and return. Enough time to read whatever Talia had sourced and add it to the deposit before the next ping. Enough time, if she was careful. Not enough if she wasn't.

The transit box was six weeks old — rented under a credential she'd bought from a document broker in the Hum Market, lower Mid-Levels, a room whose principal advantage was that no one who mattered knew it existed. Narrow cot. An isolated terminal built from salvage components she'd assembled from three separate vendors, none of whom could reconstruct what she'd built from the parts they'd sold. A shelf of dead comms: four of the original nine, each one battery-powered, mesh-isolated, bought from wetters who accepted physical currency and remembered nothing. The walls vibrated. Two floors down, a conduit branch fed the residential strata above, and the sound came up through the concrete — through the desk surface, through the cot frame when she lay still. A low, constant pressure that was not loud enough to be a problem and not quiet enough to forget.

Her right ear was ringing. Five weeks now. The onset had been gradual enough that she'd logged it as occupational — chronic Sump exposure, the body's predictable response to low-frequency saturation. But three weeks had become five, and the tone had not adapted. It sat beneath the ambient hum like a second signal, thinner, higher, with a persistence she couldn't explain and a structure she wasn't ready to name. She pressed her thumb against the tragus. The gesture had never helped. She did it anyway.

She dressed. Boots — the RCI sensor still under the left insole, industrial surplus, borrowed seven months ago and never returned. She'd measured eighteen sites now. Eleven above baseline for their stratum. The two newest were Sector 8, both within two hundred metres of trunk-line junctions, both gone in the same six-day window. The Feed had said *relocated* both times. The Feed's vocabulary for disappearance was indistinguishable from its vocabulary for administrative transfer.

*Dix-huit.* The count had been sixteen when she'd registered the dead-hand. Two more names. Two more chalk marks she couldn't verify because the junctions were restricted-corporate now and the walls were behind a clearance level she couldn't borrow.

A Wire dispatch had come through São Paulo at 03:40 — encrypted, relayed through a node she'd used twice before. Three Sump-beat journalists terminated this quarter. Two sources gone silent. She'd read it, killed the comm, set the dead unit on the shelf. The dispatch was not news. It was arithmetic. The denominator was shrinking.

She pocketed a fresh comm and took the stairs.

---

The descent was shorter from the lower Mid-Levels than it had been from Sector 3. Eight minutes through service corridors — steep, maintenance-grade stairwells designed for crews who serviced the conduit branches, not for foot traffic. Nobody used them by choice. That was the point.

The light changed at the brightline. Not gradually — the managed spectrum of the Mid-Levels ended and the conduit glow began, hard-edged, the amber-orange of pipes that had been carrying refined nitro for a hundred and fifty years. Below the line, you didn't hear the hum so much as wear it. A compression in the jaw. A heaviness behind the sternum. The deeper she went, the more the sound became a thing her skeleton conducted rather than a thing her ears reported.

She took a route she hadn't used before. West through a commercial alley where a noodle vendor's cart sat above a trunk-line joint, the broth trembling in the pot from vibration transmitted through the pavement. Past a row of frame repair kiosks — more of them than a month ago, more cracked casings stacked in crates outside, the district's small economy adjusting to hardware that failed faster near high-throughput infrastructure. The air smelled of solder and something sweet and chemical — frame lubricant, probably, or the aerosol residue of counter-surveillance coatings being applied in a back room nearby.

The Resonance District didn't announce itself with signage. It announced itself with absence. Feed-screens gone. Surveillance mounts stripped or scorched — she could see the empty brackets where mesh relays had been, the housings charred by the kind of counter-surveillance hardware sold openly two blocks east. Narrower streets. Fewer faces that looked up. The chronic RCI — twelve to eighteen, elevated for stratum — acted as its own border. Corporate security never stayed long enough for the headache to settle in.

Talia's node was east of the market. She'd learned the turnings well enough that her feet made the decisions — the body's map running ahead of conscious navigation, muscle memory doing the work that tradecraft had originally required.

---

The counter had a different face behind it. Third visit, third woman. The rotation was protocol — personnel cycled to prevent pattern recognition by anyone cataloguing the shop's traffic. Mirelle gave the passphrase. Six words. The door opened.

The back room was warmer than she remembered. Not by much — two or three degrees — but enough to register against the memory of the cooled, insulated space she'd occupied last time. The climate system was working harder. More hardware drawing more heat: new relay stations along the east wall, a second tier of storage racks bolted above the originals, cable runs she didn't recognise feeding equipment she hadn't seen. Talia's operation was expanding. Whatever Mirelle was buying, other clients were buying more of it.

Talia was standing. That was different — last time she'd been seated at the workstation, back to the door, the posture of someone who controlled the space by declining to acknowledge it. This time she was at the east wall, hands inside an open relay chassis, the luminescent ring behind her irises pulsing at the faster rate that meant her optical hardware was processing close-range technical detail. The gray coverall had cable dust on the sleeves. Her mug of tea was on the floor beside her boot, the surface filmed.

She didn't look up.

"Thirty seconds," she said.

Mirelle waited. She counted the new equipment while Talia's hands moved inside the chassis — four relay stations that hadn't been here before, two additional storage units, the three familiar displays still running but flanked now by two more she couldn't identify, their scroll rates faster than her eye could track without the implant's assist.

Talia closed the chassis. Wiped her hands on the coverall. Turned.

"You brought it."

Mirelle held out the data chip. "Eighteen months of Sump population movement. Sector by sector. Cross-referenced with Feed reclassification notices."

Talia took the chip and walked to the workstation. She didn't sit — she leaned, one hand on the surface, and slotted the chip into the interface with the other. Data populated the displays. File trees. Geographic overlays. Encrypted partitions unpacking with the mechanical efficiency of hardware built for intake.

"Your count," Talia said, watching the data resolve.

"Eighteen."

"Sector 8."

"Both. Junction-adjacent. Six days apart."

Talia scrolled. Paused on the geographic overlay — eighteen points across five sectors, plotted against the conduit infrastructure. The clustering was tighter than it had been at sixteen. The new points pulled the centre of gravity east, toward the trunk-line convergence beneath the Resonance District itself.

"You're building a target on your chest," Talia said. She said it the way she said everything — measured, flat, the register of a woman who priced risk the way other people priced vegetables. "The demographics are NitroCore's proprietary dataset. Someone cross-referencing their population numbers against their infrastructure maps is someone they'll want to find."

"Someone already found three journalists this quarter."

"And you're making it easy to be the fourth." Talia straightened from the workstation. "Rules."

"I know them."

"I say them." The voice went procedural — recitation, not conversation, the words worn to smoothness by repetition. Mirelle had heard them before and the cadence was the same and the content was the same and that was the point. Ritual. The rules existed outside the transaction the way conduit pipes existed outside the buildings they ran through. "Black Babel does not edit data. What you deposit is stored as received. What I provide is delivered as sourced. If the data is wrong, it arrived wrong."

Mirelle sat in the chair opposite the workstation.

"Compartmentalization," Talia continued. "I don't know who accesses your deposits. You don't know who sources my inventory. The network is partitioned. If this node burns, the network survives."

"Understood."

"Transactional trust. No loyalty, no alliance, no rescue. You're buying a service."

"And you're selling one."

"And I'm selling one." Talia reached beneath the workstation and set a second chip on the surface — same form factor, same physical medium, the same deliberate refusal of anything mesh-connected. "The Arctic-7 fragment. Pages twelve through thirty-one of a forty-eight-page internal assessment. The rest is classified or destroyed — nobody in the network has it. These pages survived an eleven-day window before the document was sealed. Someone extracted them. They've been in the network since."

"Who compiled the assessment?"

"The station's atmospheric research lead. No name — attribution stripped before the pages reached us. Compartmentalization protects everyone, including sources who didn't know they were sources." Talia picked up the mug from the floor. Held it without drinking. "The assessment is infrastructure data. Maps, monitoring overlays, physical layout. Not a narrative document. If you're looking for an argument, you won't find one."

"I'm looking for data."

"Then you'll find what you're looking for." Talia drank the cold tea. Set the mug on the workstation beside the chip. "The three journalists in that dispatch were looking for data too. They found it. The finding didn't protect them."

"Your network doesn't protect me either."

"My network stores your dead-hand deposit and delivers it on schedule. That's the service. What happens to you between check-ins is not my concern." She turned back to the relay chassis. "The data is the data."

Mirelle took the chip. Thumbnail-sized. Twenty pages of a classified document from a station that had been sealed eleven months ago, carried through a network built to outlast the institutions that had tried to bury it.

She left the way she'd come in. The woman at the counter was rearranging inventory on the shelf. She didn't look up.

---

Different route back. Uphill — the city's vertical architecture designed for the downward movement of labour, ascent an afterthought carved from the structure's indifference. She climbed through service corridors, past the brightline, into the managed white of the lower Mid-Levels. The chip sat in her jacket pocket. Her thumb found its edge and stayed — the tactile confirmation that the thing she carried was real, physical, destructible.

In the transit box she sealed the door. Powered the terminal — salvage components, no firmware, no mesh connection, a machine built to answer to nothing. She slotted the chip.

Pages twelve through thirty-one. No attribution. No executive summary. No institutional framing — what had survived the eleven-day window was the analytical core, stripped of everything that would have identified who had compiled it and why.

Maps.

Infrastructure density maps of Arctic-7's internal monitoring grid, rendered as geographic overlays — measurement data plotted against the station's physical architecture. The notation was technical, beyond her training. But maps were a language she'd spent eighteen months learning to read. Architecture made visible. Geography as argument.

Base layer. Nitro throughput — volume of refined nitro flowing through the station's conduit network, measured at each junction, colour-coded by density. Highest concentration around core infrastructure: primary processing hub, trunk-line convergences, relay stations feeding the containment perimeter. Standard facility layout. The architecture of a building designed around the material it handled.

Second layer. Void breach events — every recorded breach within the monitoring period, plotted by location and timestamp. The breaches were not scattered. They followed the conduit network, clustering at junctions, thinning in the corridors between nodes. Geographic. Infrastructure-dependent.

She overlaid the two.

The maps aligned. Not loosely — precisely. The highest-throughput junctions were the highest-breach junctions. The same geography. The same origin points. Two phenomena occupying the same architectural space, as if the conduit infrastructure carrying the nitro was also carrying the conditions for the thing the containment system was supposed to prevent.

She paged forward. Temporal overlays — the same data sliced by period. Early monitoring: breaches distributed, no focal pattern. Later: concentrated. The geography contracting toward the highest-density nodes. The station was not developing random failures. It was developing a pattern, and the pattern had a centre, and the centre was the infrastructure.

The final pages carried handwritten annotations — notes added in the margins, outside the digital record, by someone who understood what the institutional systems would do with this document and chose to write in a medium the systems couldn't edit. One annotation, beside a temporal overlay of the station's primary junction:

*Co-origin. Not proximity. Not correlation. The throughput and the response share a source.*

Mirelle read the annotation three times.

Then she closed the Arctic-7 file and opened her own data.

Eighteen disappearances. Five sectors. Eleven sites above RCI baseline. Seven municipal junctions in Sectors 7 through 9 reclassified to restricted-corporate maintenance — removed from public oversight, all transferred in the same quarter. Population declining around failing junctions with no corresponding increase in adjacent sectors. People not moving. People gone.

She pulled up the Sump map on the terminal. Conduit routes. Junction locations. Sector boundaries. Overlaid her disappearance data — eighteen points clustered around trunk-line junctions. Added the reclassification data — seven junctions, same sectors, same quarter. Added the RCI readings from the sensor in her boot — eleven sites above baseline, the gradient steepest near the junctions, declining with distance.

The same pattern. Arctic-7 at station scale. Neo-Shanghai at city scale. Different geography, different population, identical architecture. Nitro throughput density and void breach frequency sharing the same origin points, the same infrastructure, the same nodes. The junctions where the conduits converged were the junctions where the breaches concentrated were the junctions where the people who lived closest disappeared first.

She could read the reclassifications now. NitroCore wasn't transferring those junctions to restricted maintenance because the infrastructure needed it. They were transferring them because municipal maintenance meant municipal inspectors, municipal records, municipal accountability. Restricted-corporate meant NitroCore documentation. NitroCore personnel. NitroCore silence.

They were reclassifying the evidence.

Not negligence. Not the slow drift of institutional will that let infrastructure decay and populations thin and data disappear into the Feed's vocabulary for things that hadn't happened.

*Architecture.*

The system had not failed. The system was performing as built. The disappearances were not anomalies — they were returns on an infrastructure that concentrated the highest throughput in the lowest strata, housed expendable populations beside its most dangerous junctions, deferred maintenance where the political cost was lowest, and reclassified the record when the pattern became visible. At Arctic-7 a research lead had seen it in a contained facility and written *co-origin* in the margin before the institution consumed the document in eleven days. In Neo-Shanghai the same architecture operated at the scale of thirteen million lives, and the institution was consuming the evidence not by suppressing reports but by reclassifying the infrastructure that generated them.

She thought of the chalk-name wall beside the sealed hatch in Sector 7. Seventeen names when she'd first found it. She hadn't been back. The junction was restricted-corporate now. The names had been reclassified with the infrastructure.

She thought of Gauthier. *The evidence is anecdotal. A borrowed sensor and a list of names on a wall.* He'd been right. A journalist needed institutional data to challenge institutional power.

Now she had institutional data.

---

She closed the files. Killed the terminal. The room went dark except for the faint green pulse of the comm shelf — four units, each one a battery and a circuit and a refusal to connect to anything larger than itself.

She sat in the dark and listened to the hum. It came through the floor, through the desk, through the wall beside the cot. The conduit branch two floors below, carrying refined nitro to the residential strata above, the infrastructure pulsing the way all infrastructure pulsed in this city — constant, below notice, built into the architecture so thoroughly that the people who lived inside it had stopped hearing it the way they'd stopped hearing their own pulse.

She could hear it now. Not as background. As structure.

The ringing in her right ear persisted. Five weeks. She pressed her thumb against the tragus again — the same gesture, cartilage deforming, the body's refusal to accept a sound it couldn't cancel. The ringing sat inside the hum like a wire inside a cable. She couldn't pull them apart.

She opened a comm from the shelf. Not for publication. Not for Gauthier, not for The Wire, not for anyone. A note — raw, unstructured, the kind she composed when a story's shape exceeded what she could hold in her head and what any single location could safely store.

*The pattern is architectural. Infrastructure density maps from Arctic-7 confirm co-origin — nitro throughput and void breach share geographic source points. Neo-Shanghai replicates the same pattern at city scale. 18 disappearances cluster at highest-density junctions. 7 reclassifications seal oversight. This is not drift. This is design.*

She encrypted the note and added it to the dead-hand deposit. A new entry in the sealed package that would auto-distribute if she missed two consecutive check-in windows. The machine would carry it. The automated architecture she'd built in Talia's back room would do what the institutions refused to — hold the record regardless of what happened to the person who compiled it.

She checked the timer. Thirty-nine hours. She would ping before she slept, reset the window, continue building.

She turned off the comm. Set it on the shelf. Four units.

She lay on the cot. The frame conducted the hum through the mattress, through her ribs, through the sternum — a frequency she could trace now through the schematics she'd memorised, from the conduit branch beneath the building through the trunk-lines that fed the junctions that fed the residential blocks that housed the people who disappeared first.

The pattern was behind her eyes. Not diminished. Not resolved. Held. Encrypted on a dead comm in a room nobody knew existed, one copy among several distributed across a network designed to outlast any single node.

Including her.
