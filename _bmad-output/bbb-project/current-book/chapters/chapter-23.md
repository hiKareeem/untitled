The deployment order was dated three days before it arrived.

VS-2175-0089. Forward position, Sump perimeter, Neo-Shanghai Military District. Duration: indefinite. Evacuation authority: none issued. Stalker-7, full complement, attached to Forward Operating Base Meridian, Sector 12 junction complex, Sump/Mid-Levels boundary. Report to staging 06:00.

Nikolai read it on his datapad in the garrison quarters at 03:15 — three hours before staging, six hours before the unit would know. He read it the way he read RCI readings: once for the data, once for what the data meant, once for what was missing. The data was three sentences and a designation. What it meant was that Stalker-7 was being moved from the garrison to the perimeter — not in response to a breach event, not as part of a rotation, not under any operational protocol that appeared in the deployment manual. What was missing was the word *temporary*.

Every deployment order Nikolai had received in twenty years contained a duration. Ninety days was standard for forward positions. One hundred and twenty for extended operations. Even the open-ended deployments — the kind that command issued when the tactical situation was too fluid for fixed timelines — carried a review date. *Duration subject to reassessment at ninety-day intervals.* The language of institutional patience, which was not patience but the appearance of it.

This order said *indefinite*. No review date. No rotation schedule. No relief unit designated.

And underneath the duration line, in the administrative block where evacuation protocols were specified for any deployment within three kilometers of active or historical breach zones: *Evacuation authority: none issued.*

Not *evacuation authority: pending.* Not *evacuation authority: to be determined upon assessment of operational conditions.* None issued. The absence of the thing presented as an administrative category, as though not-authorizing-evacuation were a type of authorization.

He closed the datapad. Put on his boots. The quarters were the same as they had been since Sector 9 — bunk, desk, equipment rack, the drawer with the field log and the sidearm. The field log had seven new entries since the Sector 9 deployment. Three were micro-incursion reports. Two were RCI baseline observations. One was a note about the transit shaft frequency that he had written at 01:00 on a night when the hum had dropped to 7.6 — quieter than baseline, the kind of quiet that felt like an inhalation. The seventh was a single sentence he had written after reading the Sector 9 after-action summary from VEC liaison: *The breach in Sector 9-East has not been reclassified. It has been removed from the active monitoring list.*

He did not take the field log to staging. He would retrieve it after the unit was informed.

---

FOB Meridian was a repurposed infrastructure maintenance junction — a complex of reinforced rooms and corridors built into the Sump/Mid-Levels boundary at the point where the conduit network transitioned from residential-scale distribution to the heavy trunk-lines that fed the Mid-Levels grid. The facility had been designed for relay technicians. It had tool racks converted to weapon racks, calibration benches converted to operational displays, and sleeping quarters that still smelled faintly of insulation compound and the particular metallic tang that conduit infrastructure workers carried in their clothing after years of proximity to nitro-bearing systems.

The hum was different here. Not louder — not in the way that proximity to the Sump made things louder, not a difference of volume. *Closer.* As if the distance between the sound and the body hearing it had collapsed, the frequency no longer traveling through air and wall and floor but arriving directly, settling into the sternum the way a tuning fork settles into the surface it touches.

At the garrison, baseline was 8.1. At FOB Meridian, three hundred meters lower and two hundred meters closer to the Sump's trunk-line core, the baseline was 12.4. Not 8.1 with occasional spikes. Twelve-point-four, sustained, with no diurnal variation — the same reading at 03:00 as at 15:00, the same reading during a micro-incursion as between them. The instruments confirmed what the body already knew: the frequency had shifted from background to atmosphere.

Stalker-7 adapted the way Stalker units always adapted — through routine, equipment checks, and the professional fiction that a forward base was functionally identical to a garrison. Okonkwo ran the L-Frame maintenance schedule. Rask organized the sleeping rotation. Yeung calibrated the perimeter sensors. The operational displays showed the Sump perimeter in real time — a three-dimensional map of conduit infrastructure, breach history, RCI gradients, and residential density rendered in the institutional color code: blue for stable, amber for elevated, orange for active breach, and the grey of residential blocks that existed as context but not as concern.

Nikolai briefed them at 07:30 on the first morning.

"Forward position. Sump perimeter, Sector 12. We are here because the breach frequency in the Sump has exceeded the garrison response threshold — four micro-incursions in eleven days, baseline RCI elevated across Sectors 7 through 14. Command has determined that a forward presence improves response time." He tapped the display. "Our operational zone is the boundary between the Sump and the Mid-Levels proper. Sectors 10 through 14, junction complex network. We respond to breach events within this zone. We maintain perimeter monitoring. We coordinate with VEC telemetry for RCI tracking."

Standard language. Standard briefing. The words that converted a deployment into a procedure.

"Duration is indefinite. Rotation will be addressed when the operational situation stabilizes."

He watched their faces. Most of them absorbed the word *indefinite* the way they absorbed RCI readings — as data, filed and categorized, no emotional weight attached because the institutional vocabulary did not carry emotional weight. That was the vocabulary's design.

Kowalski's jaw tightened. Not at *indefinite*. At the sentence Nikolai had not said — the sentence that lived in the deployment order's administrative block, in the line about evacuation authority, in the absence that was not an oversight but a decision.

"Questions."

Silence. Then Rask, from the second row, her voice flat in the way voices go flat when the question is not really a question: "Rules of engagement for civilian encounters at the perimeter?"

"Standard containment protocols. Civilians within the operational zone during a breach event are directed to evacuation routes. Evacuation routes are posted in all residential corridors within three hundred meters of the perimeter."

"And between events?"

"We are a military presence in a residential area. Professional conduct. Minimal interaction. We are here to respond to breach events, not to govern."

Rask nodded. The nod contained a question that did not require an answer because the answer was the deployment itself — a military unit positioned at the boundary of a residential zone with no evacuation authority and no end date.

---

The third micro-incursion came on the fifth day.

Stage 1. Sector 11, residential corridor, two hundred meters inside the Sump boundary. The breach registered on the forward sensors at 14:20 — the baseline after the event was higher than before it. Not by much — half a point, a point and a half, the kind of drift the VEC telemetry called seasonal variation. Except it wasn't seasonal. It was directional. Every micro-incursion left the field a little thicker than it found it. A ratchet. The instruments measured it. The reports filed it. The doctrine had no category for it. 

By the time Stalker-7 reached the site, the lattice had advanced twelve meters along the conduit routing and stopped. No absorbed. No casualties. The breach held at Stage 1 for forty minutes, then the RCI began to decay — 19.7 to 17.2 to 15.8 — and the lattice withdrew along the same path it had advanced, the translucent patches in the corridor walls reverting to opacity as the coherence field pulled back. Not dissipated. *Withdrew.* Retracing the conduit routing with the specificity of something that remembered the path it had taken.

Contained. Standard. The after-action report wrote itself.

The baseline was 15.8. Higher than 15.3. Higher than 12.4. The numbers were in the report. Nikolai did not write the word ratchet. He did not write directional. He wrote contained and standard and the after-action report wrote itself the way it always did, and the writing was the mechanism, and the mechanism was the complicity, and he knew this while he wrote it and he wrote it anyway because knowing and writing were not the same action and the action was what the doctrine measured.

Fifteen-point-eight. At 12.4 — FOB Meridian's background — the hum was a pressure you noticed when you stopped moving. At 15.8, the hum was in your jaw, in the bones of your hands when you touched a wall. The difference between ambiance and presence.

The baseline was not returning. Each micro-incursion left the ambient field higher than it found it — a ratchet, incremental, the kind of change that the monitoring systems were calibrated to track over quarters and years, not days and weeks. The instruments measured the numbers. The numbers said the trend was upward. The institutional vocabulary said *seasonal variation*.

Nikolai stood at the breach site after the withdrawal and watched the corridor return to its residential state — apartment doors, strip lighting, a child's shoe on the floor beneath a coat rack, the infrastructure of ordinary life layered over the infrastructure that carried the thing that had just probed twelve meters into a residential block and retreated.

Kowalski stood beside him. He had been quieter since Sector 9 — not the silence of insubordination but the attentive silence of a man processing information that doctrine could not file. The routing observation had moved through him. It had changed what his eyes did when they looked at a conduit map, what his body did when he stood in a corridor where the hum was thicker than the instruments said it should be.

"RCI is settling at 14.1," Kowalski said. "Pre-event was 15.3. Pre-deployment was 12.4."

"Noted."

"It's ratcheting."

The word was not doctrinal. The word was a mechanic's word — Kowalski's father had been an extraction-site engineer in the Baltic corridor, and the vocabulary of machinery sometimes surfaced in his observations the way Nikolai's Corridor upbringing surfaced in his ability to read conduit topology. *Ratcheting.* A mechanism that moves in one direction. A system that does not return to its previous state.

"Log the readings. Forward them to VEC telemetry with the standard request."

"The standard request that has not been answered for any of the previous three submissions?"

"The standard request, Corporal."

"Copy."

Kowalski walked back toward the FOB. Nikolai stayed. The corridor was empty — the residents had returned to their apartments during the breach withdrawal, the way Sump residents always returned, because the Sump was where they lived and the breaches were what happened where they lived and the alternative to returning was leaving, and leaving required transit authorization and destination housing and the institutional machinery that moved people from one place to another, and that machinery had not been activated for Sector 11, or Sector 12, or any of the sectors within Stalker-7's operational zone.

The hum in the corridor was 14.1. It had been 12.4 five days ago. It would be something else next week. The direction was not a question. The direction had not been a question since Vladivostok.

---

He mapped the evacuation routes on the seventh night.

Not under orders. There were no orders regarding evacuation — the deployment authorization was explicit in its absence. Nikolai mapped them because the field log had a section for operational assessment, and operational assessment of a forward position required understanding the terrain, and understanding the terrain meant knowing how many people were between the perimeter and the interior and how those people would move if they needed to move and where they would go and how long it would take and what infrastructure would carry them.

The answers were on the display. FOB Meridian's tactical overlay showed the entire Sump perimeter in Sectors 10 through 14 — residential blocks, conduit routing, transit corridors, junction complexes, emergency access shafts. The population data was aggregated from municipal records: 1.4 million residents within the five-sector operational zone. Seventy-eight percent in residential blocks within three hundred meters of primary conduit infrastructure. Forty-one percent in blocks directly adjacent to trunk-line junctions — the points where the conduit network's throughput was highest, where the hum was loudest, where the void had repeatedly demonstrated its preference for routing.

The transit infrastructure existed. Emergency corridors connected the Sump to the Mid-Levels at seventeen points within the operational zone. Each corridor could handle approximately four thousand civilians per hour under standard evacuation protocols. Seventeen corridors, four thousand per hour, 1.4 million residents. Roughly twenty hours for a full evacuation, assuming coordinated transit, staged departure, and no breach event during the movement.

Twenty hours. Not impossible. Not even difficult, by the standards of urban emergency management. The infrastructure had been designed for it — the corridors were wide enough, the transit shafts functional, the emergency lighting and ventilation systems maintained to the standards that municipal engineering departments maintained things that might someday be needed.

Or the standards they had maintained them to before the maintenance deferrals.

Nikolai pulled up the infrastructure integrity data. The display showed junction health across the operational zone — a color-coded overlay of the conduit network's structural status. Most of the junctions were blue or light amber. Several were deep amber. Four were marked with a notation he had not seen before: *Deferred — quarterly reassessment pending.*

Sector 4-7B. Sector 11-2C. Sector 6-9A. Sector 3-1D.

He did not know what the deferrals meant in operational terms. He did not know who had signed them or why or what the integrity percentages were that had been deemed acceptable for deferral. He knew what they looked like on the tactical overlay — four points in the conduit network where the infrastructure was degraded and the degradation had been classified as tolerable by someone who did not stand where the hum was loud enough to feel in the jaw.

He knew one other thing. The evacuation corridors nearest the deferred junctions were the corridors that ran closest to the highest-throughput trunk-lines — the same trunk-lines the void had been routing through since Sector 9. The evacuation infrastructure and the void's preferred propagation paths shared the same architecture.

To evacuate 1.4 million people, you would route them through the corridors the void was already using.

He closed the overlay. Opened the field log.

*Entry 2175-0089-07.*

*Forward Operating Base Meridian, day seven. Third micro-incursion contained (Stage 1, Sector 11, 12m propagation, 40 min duration, no absorbed). Baseline RCI continues to ratchet — pre-deployment 12.4, current sustained 14.1, no return to previous baseline between events. VEC telemetry requests unanswered (4 submitted, 0 acknowledged).*

*Deployment order VS-2175-0089 specifies: duration indefinite, evacuation authority none issued. Forward position faces inward — toward the Sump, not toward the Mid-Levels. Operational zone covers Sump perimeter, Sectors 10-14. 1.4 million residents within the zone. Seventeen emergency evacuation corridors. Transit capacity approximately 4,000 per corridor per hour. Full evacuation: 20 hours minimum with coordinated staging.*

*No evacuation planning has been issued. No pre-staged transit assets. No coordination with municipal emergency services. No civilian notification of military forward presence.*

*Four conduit junctions in the operational zone show deferred maintenance status. Evacuation corridors adjacent to these junctions share infrastructure with the trunk-lines the void has been preferentially routing through since Sector 9.*

He paused. The cursor blinked on the datapad's screen. Through the walls of FOB Meridian, the conduit infrastructure hummed at 12.4 — the local baseline, the frequency that was not garrison-normal and not breach-elevated but something between, the sound of a system operating above its designed threshold long enough that the monitors had adjusted and the word *elevated* had been replaced by the word *baseline*.

*The deployment geometry does not correspond to breach response. Standard breach response positions the forward unit between the breach origin and the civilian population — interposition. This deployment positions the unit between the Sump and the Mid-Levels — containment. The perimeter faces inward. The operational displays are oriented toward the Sump interior, not toward the Mid-Levels approach corridors.*

*We are not here to respond to breaches. We are here to hold a line.*

*I do not know what the line is for. The deployment order does not say. The field does.*

He closed the field log. Placed the datapad on the desk — not in the drawer, because the forward base did not have a drawer, because the desk was a calibration bench and the quarters were a relay technician's station and the forward operating base was a maintenance junction redesignated for a purpose the deployment order specified in three sentences and the infrastructure had been built for decades before anyone issued the order.

He checked his hands.

Steady. The same hands that had signed the after-action report for Sector 9. The same hands that would sign the after-action report for every micro-incursion that the forward sensors detected and Stalker-7 contained and the field log recorded and the institutional vocabulary converted into the language of seasonal variation and standard containment protocols. The same hands that would hold whatever came next, because the deployment was indefinite and the evacuation authority was none and the baseline was ratcheting and the line faced inward and the hands did not shake because the hands had never shaken, not at Vladivostok, not at Sector 9, not at any of the deployments where the field log said one thing and the after-action report said another and the gap between them grew wider and wider until the gap was not a gap but a posture, a way of standing in the world that required steady hands and a flat voice and the daily discipline of not assembling the data into the picture it had been forming for twenty years.

The hum at FOB Meridian was 12.4. It had not changed since they arrived — the same amplitude, the same frequency, the same quality of presence that filled the junction complex the way heat filled a room. It was steady in a way that the garrison baseline had never been. Not the steadiness of a system in equilibrium. The steadiness of a system that had stopped fluctuating because it knew where it was going.

Kowalski was in the corridor when Nikolai stepped out. Standing at the operational display, studying the tactical overlay. The conduit map. The trunk-lines and junction nodes and evacuation corridors and residential blocks and the relationship between them that the institutional color code rendered in amber and blue and grey.

Kowalski did not look up. His finger was tracing one of the evacuation corridors — Corridor 7, the primary route from Sectors 11 and 12 to the Mid-Levels transit hub. The corridor ran parallel to a trunk-line junction for two hundred meters. Two hundred meters of shared infrastructure between the thing that carried people to safety and the thing the void was using as a highway.

"The evac routes follow the conduit paths," Kowalski said.

"They were designed that way."

"I know." His finger stopped on the junction where Corridor 7 met the trunk-line. "Has anyone issued an evacuation plan?"

"Evacuation authority is none issued."

Kowalski looked at him. The expression was not surprise — surprise required new information, and the absence of evacuation authority was not new information. It was the expression he had worn in Sector 9 when the lattice had bypassed the dead-end corridors and followed the trunk-lines — the face of a man watching something arrange itself into a shape he could not unknow.

"Copy," Kowalski said.

He turned back to the display. Nikolai walked past him toward the sleeping quarters — past the operational displays, past the weapon racks that had been tool racks, past the reinforced conduit housings that ran through the junction complex and hummed at 12.4 and carried the thing that the deployment order positioned Stalker-7 to contain and the evacuation plan that did not exist would have routed 1.4 million people alongside.

The hum followed him. It did not change. It did not need to.
