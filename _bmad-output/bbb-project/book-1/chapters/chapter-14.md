## Draft v1

The commune had been a relay station once. You could see it in the bones of the place — the reinforced concrete foundations, the mounting brackets where signal equipment had been bolted to the walls, the rectangular scars on the roof where antenna arrays had stood before the grid pulled out and someone had stripped the hardware for salvage. The building itself was ugly in the way all infrastructure was ugly: designed for function, abandoned when the function moved on, repurposed by the people the function had left behind.

Fourteen people lived here. Nephthys counted them as she and Imani approached from the western road — a dirt track that had been paved once, the asphalt crumbling at the edges, dandelions forcing through the cracks with the patient violence of things that outlast. Three figures in the small garden plot beside the building, working raised beds constructed from salvaged conduit housing — the orange NitroCore logos still visible on the metal sides, faded but legible, the branding of the world that had discarded them repurposed to grow food. Two more on the roof, repairing a solar collector with tools that looked older than either of them. The rest inside, visible through windows that had been stripped of their original polymer sheeting and refitted with salvaged glass — imperfect, warped, casting the interior light into shapes that reminded Nephthys of the stained glass in the churches of her childhood. Not sacred. But not nothing.

Imani had found them. She always found them — the communes, the shelters, the networks of excommunicates and dissenters and simply displaced people who had built something in the spaces the grid had vacated. It was a skill born of necessity. The Outlands had taught them that survival required infrastructure, and in the absence of the institutional kind, the human kind would have to do.

"This is the one," Imani said. "The commune near the grid edge. The ones I heard about at the relay south of Bruges."

Nephthys nodded. The chorus was quiet here — present, always present, but muted. The relay station's conduit infrastructure had been stripped, the trunk-line connection severed, the residual coherence reduced to a whisper that pressed against her awareness like a hand resting on a shoulder. Not painful. Not urgent. The migraine that had been her constant companion since the dead town was a dull weight behind her left eye rather than the ice-pick precision of an active episode. She could think. She could observe.

The clarity was useful. She did not trust it. The chorus interrupted her theology — forced her to attend to the thing itself rather than her ideas about the thing. Without the interruption, the ideas ran unchecked.

A woman came out to meet them. Fifties, weathered, wearing a layered combination of salvaged textiles and what Nephthys recognized immediately as Church underlayers — the linen shift, the woven belt, garments that had once been worn beneath vestments and were now worn beneath everything else because they were what she owned and what she was. The woman's hands were earth-stained. Her eyes moved from Nephthys to Imani and back, assessing with the particular attention of someone who had learned to read strangers for threat before reading them for anything else.

"You're the one from the Rhine corridor," the woman said. Not a question.

"I am Nephthys."

"I know who you are." The woman's gaze dropped to Nephthys's left hand — to the brand. The broken circle, white scar tissue against dark skin, the mark of the Silenced that Nephthys had stopped trying to hide before she had stopped wanting to. "Maren told us you were coming. From the commune outside Metz."

Word had travelled. Nephthys had visited two communes before this one — the settlement outside Metz and the group at the relay south of Bruges. She had not asked anyone to send word ahead. They had done it because she carried something they recognized, and among the Silenced, recognition was enough.

"My companion is Imani," Nephthys said. "She is tired. We have been walking for four days."

The woman looked at Imani. Something in her expression reorganized — the assessment completed, filed, replaced by a different kind of attention. Practical.

"There is food," the woman said. "And space. We do not have much, but we do not turn away the Silenced."

She did not say *excommunicated.* She said *Silenced.* The Church's own word — the formal term for the rite that had stripped Nephthys of her vestments and burned the brand into her palm. The woman used it the way Nephthys used it: not as the institution intended, as a mark of shame, but as a credential. *You were Silenced because you heard something the institution could not accommodate. So were we. Come inside.*

They went inside.

---

The commune ate together. This was not remarkable — practical communal living required shared meals, shared labour, shared resources. But the meal had a structure that Nephthys recognized. The gathering. The silence before eating. The oldest member — a man in his seventies, bone-thin, with the careful movements of someone managing chronic pain — spoke a few words. Not a formal prayer. Not the Radiance opening. Something simpler: *We are here. We have food. The hum carries.*

Three sentences. No theology. No liturgical form. But the cadence was there — the rhythm of a people who had grown up inside the Church's acoustic architecture and carried its patterns in their syntax even after the institution had discarded them. The hum carries. Not *the Grid provides.* Not *the Voice sustains.* The hum carries. Stripped of doctrine, reduced to observation. The most honest prayer Nephthys had heard since her ordination.

They ate. Root vegetables from the garden, rehydrated protein, bread made from grain traded with a farming settlement twelve kilometres south. The food was adequate. The company was something else.

Nephthys listened. This was what she did — what she had always done, what had made her the Church's best theologian and then its most dangerous heretic. She listened with the same discipline she had brought to scriptural exegesis — not for the words but for the structure beneath the words, the architecture of attention that distinguished testimony from recitation.

The commune members spoke during the meal. Not directed at her — at each other, the ordinary conversation of people who had built a life in the margins. Who would repair the eastern wall. Whether the solar collector would survive another winter. A dispute about water purification tablets. The texture of daily survival in a place the grid had forgotten.

But underneath the domestic talk, another conversation. Fragments. Asides. A woman mentioning that the hum had been louder last week near the old converter station. A man noting that his dreams had changed since moving to the commune — not nightmares, not visions, just a quality of sound in the dreaming that hadn't been there before. The oldest member, the one who had spoken the meal-prayer, saying quietly to no one in particular: "The ground remembers."

Nephthys ate. She listened. She did not speak.

Imani, beside her, was watching. Not the commune. Nephthys. She could feel Imani's attention the way she could feel the residual chorus — a presence at the edge of perception, steady, attentive, measuring something she could not quite name.

After the meal, the woman who had met them — her name was Dara, Nephthys learned, a former Prelate from a parish in the Rhineland, Silenced eight years ago for refusing to stop conducting unauthorized vigils at a void scar near her church — sat across from Nephthys at the cleared table and said:

"You want to hear the testimonies."

"I want to listen," Nephthys said.

"Everyone who comes here wants to listen. The difference is what they do with what they hear." Dara's hands were flat on the table — a gesture Nephthys recognized. Grounding. The posture of someone who had stood at an altar and needed to feel something solid beneath her palms before speaking. "We have twelve people with experiences. Not all of them talk about it. Some do, when they're ready. I will not press them."

"I would not ask you to."

"I know." Dara studied her. "Maren said you were different from the others who've come through. She said you listen like a theologian and speak like a priest. She meant it as a compliment. I am not sure it is one."

Nephthys did not respond. Dara's caution was earned. People who spoke like priests had authority, and authority in the Outlands was a currency that could be spent on others without their consent.

"There is one who wants to speak to you," Dara said. "Lene. She has been waiting since Maren's message arrived."

---

Lene was young — twenty-six, perhaps twenty-eight. Former acolyte. Not Silenced — she had left the Church voluntarily, which in some ways was harder. The Silenced had the brand, the ritual, the clean severance. Those who simply walked away had nothing but the walking, and the questions that had driven them to walk, and the absence of answers that the institution had once provided even when the answers were wrong.

She sat in a small room at the back of the relay station — a space that had once housed signal processing equipment and now held two chairs, a low table, and a window facing east. The room had the quality of a confessional, not by design but by use. This was where the commune's members came to speak about the things that did not fit into the ordinary conversation of daily survival. The hum in the dreams. The sensation at the old converter station. The ground remembering.

Nephthys sat across from her. Imani stood by the door — not inside the room, not outside it, occupying the threshold with the precision of someone who had learned that her role was to witness without participating. She had her arms crossed. Her jaw was set. She was watching Nephthys the way she had been watching all evening — with an attention that was no longer only concern for her health.

"Tell me what happened," Nephthys said.

Lene's hands were in her lap. She was twisting a piece of cord between her fingers — not resonance beads, not Church objects, just cord, something to hold.

"It was three years ago," she said. "I was still in the Church. Still an acolyte. I was stationed at a parish in Cologne — the Church of the Blessed Convergence, do you know it?"

"I conducted the Radiance there twice."

Lene nodded. Something in her posture loosened — the recognition of shared ground. Nephthys had been in the same halls. Had spoken the same words. Had heard the same hum through the same walls. It mattered.

"There was an incursion. Small. Sub-threshold — the VEC classified it as a point-three event. A void scar appeared in the parish garden, near the conduit housing along the eastern wall. Two metres in diameter. No one was absorbed. The Church conducted a Breach Vigil — three days of harmonization, the standard response. I was assigned to the overnight shifts."

She paused. The cord twisted.

"On the second night. Three in the morning. The vigil was supposed to be continuous harmonization — you sing, you match the hum, you maintain the resonance. Standard doctrine. But I stopped singing. I don't know why. I just — stopped. And I listened."

Nephthys's hands were flat on the table. The brand touching wood.

"What did you hear?"

"Not what the Church says." Lene's voice was steady. She had told this story before — not often, not easily, but enough times that the words had found their shape. "The doctrine says the hum during a vigil is the Voice at its strongest. That harmonization strengthens the barrier. That what you feel is divine protection."

"And what did you feel?"

Lene looked up. Her eyes were dark, and in them Nephthys saw something she had not expected. Not fear. Not awe. Not the defensive defiance of someone who knew their experience contradicted everything they had been taught.

Calm.

"Peace," Lene said. "Not comfort. Not safety. Not the warm feeling the Church promises when you harmonize correctly. Something — structured. Something that had a shape, the way a room has a shape, the way a building has architecture you can feel even when you can't see it. I stopped singing, and the hum changed. Not in pitch. Not in volume. In *quality.* As if — as if what I had been adding to the signal was just noise, and when I stopped adding noise, I could hear what was underneath."

Nephthys did not move. The residual chorus in this building was a whisper — a ghost of a ghost. But she could feel it respond. Not to Lene's words. To the structure Lene was describing. The architecture of the experience resonated with the architecture of her own, and the resonance was not metaphor. It was correspondence.

"I stayed quiet for eleven minutes," Lene said. "I know because the vigil timer was running. Eleven minutes of listening without singing. And in those eleven minutes I felt — I don't have the right words for this. The Church gave me words for everything except what I actually experienced. I felt something that was not hostile. Not benevolent. Not divine. Not demonic. Something that was *present,* the way another person is present in a room even when they have not spoken. Something that was aware of the scar, and aware of the parish, and aware of me listening, and wasn't threatened by any of it."

"Responsive," Nephthys said.

Lene stared at her. "Yes. That's the word. I've been looking for that word for three years."

"The chorus is responsive." She heard the word settle between them — Lene's word, the word Lene had been searching for, and Nephthys had given it to her. Not because she understood the chorus. Because she had been listening to it longer. "I do not know what it means. But I know it is not what the Church claims. It is not one voice. It is many. And it listens back."

She heard herself speaking and recognized the cadence. The declarative certainty. The absence of qualification. She had spoken this way from the pulpit for twenty years. The setting had changed. The cadence had not.

"The Church calls it the Voice," Nephthys continued. "Singular. A broadcast from a divine source. But what you describe — what I have heard — is not one voice. It is many." She paused. The word she wanted was *chorus.* The word she had used since the Silencing, the word that had cost her everything. "When you stopped singing, the hum changed. I want to understand why."

Lene's cord had gone still in her hands. She was looking at Nephthys with an expression Nephthys recognized — the face of someone who had carried an experience alone and just heard it named by someone else.

"Is that why they Silenced you?" Lene asked.

"They Silenced me because I told them the Voice was not one. That the hum was composite. That the Church's most sacred sound was not what they claimed."

"And they already knew."

Nephthys looked at her. The statement was not a question.

"Yes," she said. "Malachi already knew."

Lene was quiet. The room was quiet. The residual hum pressed against the walls — faint, persistent, the ghost of an infrastructure that had once carried a city's worth of energy and now carried only its own echoes. Outside, the Outlands evening was settling in — long light, cooling air, the sounds of a commune preparing for night.

"I left the Church after that night," Lene said. "Not immediately. It took me four months. But I knew. From the moment I stopped singing and the hum changed — I knew the doctrine was wrong. Not about everything. About *this.* About what the hum contains."

"You were brave."

"I was afraid." Lene's hands tightened on the cord. "I'm still afraid. I heard something that I can't explain inside the framework I was raised in, and I left the only institution that offered a framework at all, and now I live in a relay station and grow vegetables and tell this story to people who come through, and I don't know what the peace meant. I only know it was real."

Nephthys reached across the table. She placed her hand on Lene's — the branded palm, the broken circle, the scar tissue warm against the younger woman's knuckles. A pastoral gesture. Automatic. The touch that said *I see you; I am still here; this is real but we are not alone.*

"It was real," Nephthys said. "What you heard was real. It was not the Voice. It was not a hallucination. It was not what the Church names it or what the VEC would classify it as. You heard something underneath. I have heard it too. I do not have the right words for it yet. But I will not tell you it was nothing."

She heard the words leave her and felt their weight. She had meant to listen. She had come to collect testimony, not to offer interpretation. But Lene was looking at her the way people looked at someone who had given them a name for a thing they had been carrying nameless, and Nephthys did not know how to give a name without giving a framework, or how to give a framework without becoming the authority she had left the Church to escape.

From the doorway, Imani said nothing. Her arms were still crossed. Her jaw was still set. But her eyes had moved — not to Lene, not to the room, but to Nephthys's hand on Lene's hand. The branded palm turned downward. The gesture of someone offering certainty to someone who needed it.

---

Later, in the room they had been given — a converted storage space with two cots and a window facing the Outlands dark — Imani laid out the transit papers.

"Dara's contact came through," she said. "Forged credentials. Good enough to pass the automated checkpoints into the Mid-European corridor. From there we can reach the eastern transit hub and buy passage to Neo-Shanghai on a cargo route. It'll take ten days."

Nephthys was sitting on the cot, the resonance beads in her hands. They were warm. They hummed. The nitro inside the glass responded to even the faint residual coherence of this place, and the vibration against her palms was the closest thing to comfort she had left.

"Lene's experience," she said. "The peace she described. The structured quality. The responsiveness."

"I heard."

"Imani, she experienced what I experience. In a different place, under different circumstances, at a different distance from the infrastructure. The shape of the experience is the same — the composite hum, the responsiveness, the difference between what the Church prescribes and what the hum actually contains."

"I heard," Imani said again. Her voice was level. She was folding the transit papers into the waterproof lining of her pack — methodical, precise, the logistics of survival managed with the same competence she brought to everything. "One testimony. One person. In a commune of fourteen people who have built their lives around the belief that the Church is wrong about the void."

"She is not the only one. Dara described twelve members with experiences. The commune outside Metz had four. The relay station near Bruges had two. Independent observations, Imani. Consistent structural features across different locations, different people, different—"

"Different people who have all left the Church." Imani's hands stilled on the pack. "People who need the Church to be wrong. People who need what they experienced to be real, because if it is not, they walked away from everything for nothing."

The words were careful. Measured. Not unkind. Imani did not attack Nephthys's theology. She tested it. She applied the pressure that would reveal whether the structure was load-bearing or decorative, and the pressure she applied was always the same: *confirmation bias.* The observation that the people who validated Nephthys's framework were the people who most needed it to be true.

Including Nephthys.

"You think they hear what they want to hear," Nephthys said.

"I think you hear what they tell you and recognize what you already believe." Imani met her eyes. "That is not the same thing as being wrong. But it is not the same thing as being right, either."

The hum was there. Faint. Residual. The ghost of the infrastructure pressing against the walls of a building that had been abandoned by the system and repurposed by the discarded. Nephthys held the resonance beads and felt them vibrate — the nitro-glass spheres responding to the ambient coherence with the fidelity of an instrument she had not designed but had learned to play.

"Lene used the word *responsive,*" Nephthys said. "Before I offered it. She described the structural features before I named them. That is not projection, Imani. That is correspondence."

"She described an experience. You provided the framework." Imani's voice was quiet. "And now she has a framework. And you have a convert. And neither of you noticed the difference."

Nephthys did not respond. The accusation was not an accusation — it was a mirror. Imani held it up the way she held the medical kit: not because she wanted to use it but because refusal to look was its own kind of symptom.

A convert. The word sat in her like a stone. Not because Imani was wrong. Because Nephthys could not tell whether she was right. She had offered Lene a framework — not because the framework was proven but because it was *needed,* and the offering had felt like service, and service had always been her language for love.

She did not know when listening had become teaching. She did not know if there was a difference.

"I am not building a congregation," Nephthys said.

"I know you believe that."

The words sat between them. Imani zipped the pack. She checked the transit papers one more time — a habit, not a necessity. The papers were secure. The route was planned. Ten days to Neo-Shanghai, where the chorus would be loudest, where the Cathedral of the Living Sound waited, where whatever the hum contained would either resolve into meaning or consume her.

Nephthys looked at her. Twenty years old. Former acolyte. Months in the Outlands, carrying the medical supplies and the logistics and the daily weight of keeping alive a woman whose brain was being consumed by the thing she followed. Imani's face in the dim light of the converted storage room was composed. Her hands were still. Her jaw was set in the particular way it set when she was managing something she could not fix.

"We leave in the morning," Nephthys said.

"I know."

"Thank you. For the papers. For all of it."

Imani was quiet. Then: "Lene asked me, while you were with Dara. She asked me if you were a prophet."

Nephthys's hands tightened on the resonance beads. "What did you tell her?"

"I told her you were a theologian with a brain tumor and a very good ear." Imani pulled the blanket over her legs and turned to face the wall. "I didn't tell her which one I was following. I haven't decided."

The hum carried through the walls. Residual. Persistent. The chorus reduced to a murmur in a building stripped of its infrastructure, carrying nothing but the memory of what it had once transmitted. Nephthys held the beads and listened. The layers were there — faint, indistinct, the composite signal thinned by distance from functioning conduit. But present. Still present. The way Lene's experience had been present for three years, waiting for a framework, waiting for someone to say: *what you heard was real.*

She had spoken with certainty. She had offered Lene the thing she herself had been denied — validation. And the validation had felt like service, and the service had looked, from the doorway where Imani stood with her arms crossed, like the thing the Church did when it told the faithful what the hum meant and called it truth.

The pain was signal. The signal was the chorus. The chorus was a question.

She had come here to listen. She was not certain that was what she had done.

Outside, the Outlands dark held its silence, and the relay station's stripped walls hummed with what they remembered, and Nephthys lay awake and thought of the woman in the third row who was not singing, and wondered whether what Lene had heard and what she had heard were the same thing, or whether she had already begun to shape the answer before the question was asked.
