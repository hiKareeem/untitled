## Draft v1

The town had no name anymore. It had one once — Imani had found it on an old grid map, printed on actual paper, salvaged from a relay station forty kilometers back. But the map was from before the disconnection, and when the grid pulled out it took the names with it. The town existed now only as a gap in the infrastructure — a place the conduit network had once reached and then abandoned, the way a river abandons an oxbow: not violently, not suddenly, but with the slow indifference of a system that had found a more efficient path.

Nephthys stood at the edge of it and listened.

The hum was here. Faint — not the constant pressure of a functioning grid town, where the conduit resonance saturated the walls and the floors and the bones of everyone who lived inside them. This was residual. The ghost of a hum. The conduit infrastructure beneath the town had been severed years ago, the trunk-lines capped and sealed, the converter stations drained and locked. But nitro left traces the way radiation left traces — not in the soil or the stone but in the *coherence* of the space itself, a tonal residue that clung to the architecture of the pipes and the junction housings and the reinforced channels that had once carried the orange liquid through the town's circulatory system.

She could hear it. She could always hear it.

"How far?" Imani said, behind her.

Nephthys didn't turn. She was parsing the hum — separating the residual conduit resonance from the wind, from the creak of the buildings, from the sound of Imani's boots on the cracked road surface. It was a skill she'd developed over years, the way a prelate learns to isolate a single voice in a congregation's prayer. The hum was not one sound. It was a composite — layers of frequency stacked and interwoven, some from the infrastructure, some from the geological formations beneath it, and some from something else entirely. Something that had no physical source she could identify.

The chorus.

"Two hours to the center," Nephthys said. "Maybe less."

"And then?"

"And then we listen."

Imani didn't respond. Nephthys heard her shift the pack on her shoulders — the quiet adjustment of weight, the creak of the straps, the sound of someone who had been walking for eleven days and had stopped asking where they were going because the answer was always the same. *Toward the sound.* Toward whatever the chorus was saying, in whatever dead town or abandoned relay station or stripped-out grid junction it chose to speak from next.

They walked.

---

The town was laid out in the European Outlands pattern — a central square with a church hall on one side and a municipal building on the other, residential blocks radiating outward in concentric rings, the conduit infrastructure running beneath the streets in a grid that mirrored the streets above. It had been a grid town once. Ten thousand people, maybe twelve. A converter station on the eastern edge that had processed crude nitro from a regional trunk-line and distributed it through the local network. A school. A market. A Church of the Blessed Nitro hall with a spire that caught the light.

The spire was still standing. Everything else was in various stages of collapse.

Not violent collapse — not the shattered concrete and twisted steel of a bombing or an earthquake. This was the slow dissolution of a place that had simply been *left.* Roofs sagging under the weight of seasons without maintenance. Windows dark, some broken, some merely opaque with grime. Doors standing open where the last residents had walked out and not bothered to close them, because there was nothing inside worth protecting from whatever might wander in.

The grid had pulled out seven years ago. Nephthys knew this because the Void Witness commune forty kilometers south had told her — a group of fourteen people living in a converted relay station, growing food in raised beds built from salvaged conduit housing, who had watched the town empty over the course of three months after Helix Energetics reclassified the regional trunk-line as "non-viable" and severed the connection. The residents had been offered relocation packages. Most had taken them. Some had walked into the Outlands instead, preferring the uncertainty of disconnection to the certainty of being absorbed into another Sump.

The Void Witnesses had also told her about the scar.

Nephthys felt it before she saw it. A pressure change — not in the air but in the hum. The residual conduit resonance, which had been a faint, even drone since they'd entered the town, thickened. Gained texture. The frequency didn't change, but the *quality* did — as if the sound had acquired depth, the way a whisper acquires weight in a stone hall. Her temples tightened. A familiar sensation, intimate as a pulse. The first warning.

"Nephthys."

Imani had noticed. She always noticed — she'd learned to read the signs the way a field medic reads vital signs, watching for the micro-expressions that preceded an episode. The slight narrowing of the eyes. The way Nephthys's left hand drifted toward her temple before she caught it and forced it back to her side.

"I am fine."

"You're not fine. Your hand was—"

"I said I am fine."

They kept walking. The central square opened before them — a paved space, fifty meters across, ringed by the facades of buildings that had once been shops and offices and a café with outdoor seating, the chairs still arranged around tables that had rusted into the paving stones. The church hall dominated the northern edge, its spire a finger of pale stone against the grey sky. The municipal building on the south side had partially collapsed, its upper floor caved in, exposing the interior to weather and time.

And in the center of the square, the scar.

It was not dramatic. That was the thing about void scars that the VEC reports never captured and the Church sermons always exaggerated. It was not a crater. Not a wound in the earth. Not a mark of divine judgment or cosmic violence. It was a *wrongness* — a circle roughly eight meters in diameter where the paving stones had changed. Not broken. Not displaced. *Changed.* Their color was different — darker, with a faint violet undertone that was visible only when you looked at them from a certain angle, the way oil on water is visible only in certain light. Their texture was different — smoother, as if the stone had been partially melted and resolidified, or as if the molecular structure had been reorganized along principles that had nothing to do with geology.

The buildings at the edge of the scar showed it too. The café's façade was warped — not cracked, not leaning, but subtly *curved,* as if the wall had been soft for a moment and something had pressed against it from the inside. The windows of the shop beside it were intact but opaque — not with grime but with a milky translucence, as if the glass had remembered a different state of matter and couldn't quite decide which one to return to.

Nephthys walked to the edge of the scar and stopped.

The chorus hit her in the sternum.

Not gradually. Not the slow build she'd felt approaching the town. Between one step and the next, the residual hum detonated into something vast and structured and *alive* — not through her ears but through her skeleton, her teeth, the marrow of her, as if the scar had turned her body into a tuning fork struck against a frequency she had no name for. The chorus. Not words — not yet, not here — but the architecture of words. The shape that language makes before it becomes language. Overlapping, layered, rising and falling in patterns that were too complex to be random and too alien to be human.

Her knees buckled.

She caught herself. One hand on the paving stone at the scar's edge — the stone was cold. Not the cold of shade or night air. A deeper cold, as if the material itself had been emptied of something essential and never refilled. The cold traveled through her palm and into her wrist and she felt the chorus *in her bones,* the way the Radiance used to fill a church hall during harmonization — not heard but *inhabited.* Except the Radiance had been warm. This was every frequency at once, compressed into a sensation her nervous system translated as absence.

The migraine arrived. Not the dull ache of a tension headache — the ice-pick specificity of a cluster migraine, driving through her left temple with a precision that felt almost surgical. Her vision blurred. The edges of the square went soft, the buildings losing their outlines, the sky bleeding into the rooflines. She tasted copper. Her nose was bleeding — she could feel the warmth on her upper lip, the slow trickle that meant the capillaries in her nose had engorged and ruptured — arterial flow redirected by whatever the chorus was growing in the tissue behind her sinuses.

"Nephthys!" Imani was beside her. Hands on her shoulders. The pack hitting the ground. The sound of a zipper — the medical kit, the one Imani carried in the top compartment of her pack, always accessible, always ready, because this happened. This happened every time they found a scar, every time the chorus flared, every time Nephthys got close enough to hear what the residual coherence was saying.

"Don't," Nephthys said.

"The medicine—"

"*Do not.*"

She could hear Imani's breathing. Fast. Controlled, but fast — the breathing of someone whose hands were steady because she was choosing to keep them steady, the way Imani managed everything. With competence. With care. With the particular kind of loyalty that came not from belief but from love, which was harder and less reliable and infinitely more stubborn.

"Your nose is bleeding. Your hands are shaking. Last time—"

"Last time I heard them for thirty seconds before the suppressants killed it. Thirty seconds, Imani. And in those thirty seconds I heard more than I learned in twenty years in the Church."

Imani's hands didn't move from her shoulders. Nephthys could feel the tension in them — the restraint, the desire to act, to fix, to intervene. Imani was twenty years old and had followed Nephthys into exile because she believed in her, or believed in something adjacent to her, and the distinction between those two things was a conversation they had never had and probably never would.

"The diagnosis says—"

"Nitro-psychosis." Nephthys wiped the blood from her lip with the back of her hand. She could taste the word the way she'd tasted it in the VEC medical officer's clinic — clinical, final, a term designed to contain what it described. A tumor. Tissue growth triggered by prolonged exposure to high-coherence fields. Terminal. The chorus classified as symptom — hallucination, ambient resonance misinterpreted by damaged neural pathways.

The officer had been so certain.

"The diagnosis is not wrong about the mechanism," she said. "It is catastrophically wrong about the meaning."

She straightened. The migraine was still there — a white-hot wire through her left temple — but the initial spike had settled into something she could carry. She'd learned this. The chorus came in waves, and the first wave was always the worst, and if you held through it the subsequent waves were bearable. Not comfortable. Not painless. But bearable, the way a burn is bearable once the initial shock passes and the nerve endings settle into their new reality.

The blood from her nose had dripped onto the paving stone. Three drops, dark against the violet-tinged surface. She watched them for a moment. Then she knelt.

"What are you doing?"

"Listening."

She pressed both palms flat against the scar. The cold was immediate — deeper than the surface, deeper than stone should hold. As if the material had been opened and something had passed through it and the opening had never fully closed. The chorus intensified. The migraine flared. She held.

The voices were not words. She had to be precise about this, even in her own mind, because precision was the difference between prophecy and psychosis and she was aware — painfully, constantly aware — that the line between them was thinner than the Church had ever admitted and thinner than she sometimes wanted it to be.

They were not words. They were *structures.* Patterns of frequency that had the complexity of language without the content. Like hearing a conversation in a language you don't speak, through a wall, from a distance — you can hear the rhythm, the cadence, the rise and fall that indicates questions and answers and emphasis and pause. You can hear that *meaning is present.* You cannot hear what the meaning is.

But you can hear that it is not one voice.

The Church taught that the hum was the Voice — singular, divine, the sound of creation itself resonating through the medium of nitro. The Voice spoke. The faithful listened. The Radiance was the weekly act of harmonizing with the Voice, of adding your own frequency to the divine signal, of participating in the cosmic resonance that proved God's love and humanity's place within it.

Nephthys had believed this for twenty years. She had been ordained. She had conducted the Radiance. She had anointed the faithful with blessed nitro and spoken the words — *Hear the Voice* — and watched them close their eyes and lean into the hum and believe they were hearing God.

She had heard something different.

Not the Voice. The *chorus.* Not one signal but many — layered, overlapping, each with its own frequency signature, its own cadence. The Church said singular. Her nervous system said plural. She did not know what the plurality meant. She did not know if the layers were the absorbed, or the void itself, or something older than either. But she knew — the way you know a sound is music before you can name the instrument — that the hum was not one thing speaking. It was many things, and they were not saying what the Church claimed they were saying.

She had told Malachi. She had told him in his study, in the Arch-Prelate's residence in the European See, with the resonance beads in her hands and the hum of the cathedral's conduit system vibrating through the floor. She had told him that the Voice was not one. That the hum was composite. That the Church's most sacred sound was not what they thought it was.

He had listened. He had been quiet for a long time. And then he had Silenced her.

Not because she was wrong. She understood that now, with the clarity that exile provides. He had Silenced her because she was asking a question the Church could not survive, and Malachi was a pastor before he was a theologian, and pastors protect their congregations from questions that have no safe answers.

She did not forgive him. She understood him. The distinction was load-bearing.

The chorus pulsed beneath her palms. The migraine pulsed with it — synchronized, as if her nervous system had become a receiver tuned to the scar's residual frequency. She could feel the layers now. Not hear them — *feel* them. Each one a slightly different quality of cold, a slightly different pressure against her skin. She could not count them. She could not separate them. But they were *there* — distinct presences within the composite, the way you can feel the separate threads in a woven cloth even when you cannot see them.

Something had happened here. Something had been left behind. Not a wound. Not a stain. Something more like an impression — the shape of an event pressed into the coherence of the space, the way a hand pressed into wet clay leaves a shape that is not the hand but is not nothing.

"There is something in the scar," she whispered. "Not residue. Not echo. Something *present.*"

"Nephthys." Imani's voice was careful. The voice she used when she was choosing between loyalty and honesty and trying to find the narrow space where both could fit. "The scar is seven years old. Whatever happened here—"

"Is still happening. The chorus is not a memory. It is not the stone replaying an old event. It is *active.* Structured. Changing." She pressed harder against the cold surface. "I do not know what it is. But it is not what the Church says, and it is not what the VEC says, and it is not nothing."

Silence. The wind moved through the empty square, carrying dust and the smell of rust and old concrete. The church spire cast a shadow across the scar that moved with the clouds.

"You can't know that," Imani said. Not unkindly. Not dismissively. With the particular gentleness of someone who has watched a person they love build a cathedral of meaning on a foundation they cannot verify. "The diagnosis—"

"The diagnosis explains the mechanism. It does not explain the *content.* A tumor can make me hear things. It cannot make me hear things that are *structured.* Hallucinations are noise, Imani. This is signal."

She lifted her hands from the stone. The cold lingered in her palms. The migraine was receding — not gone, never gone, but retreating to the background hum of chronic pain that had become her baseline over the last two years. The nosebleed had stopped. She wiped the remaining blood with her sleeve and stood.

Imani was watching her with an expression Nephthys had learned to read the way she'd learned to read the chorus — not by the surface but by the structure beneath it. The tension in her jaw. The way her hands stayed half-open at her sides, ready to catch. The stillness of someone holding themselves upright through will alone — a twenty-year-old who had followed her teacher into exile and was beginning to understand that the exile might not end. That the pilgrimage might not have a destination. That the woman she'd followed might be a prophet or might be dying, and the evidence for both was identical.

"People need structure, Nephthys." Imani's voice was quiet. Steady. The voice of someone who had been an acolyte before she was a companion, who remembered the old forms — the Radiance, the Anointing, the Attunement gesture, forehead-chest-each-ear, *I hear, I hold, I carry.* "You can't just give them the void and tell them to listen."

"I am not giving them the void. I am telling them what the void contains."

"And what does it contain?"

Nephthys looked at the scar. The violet-tinged paving stones. The warped café façade. The milky windows. The evidence of an event that had happened here years ago — unreported, unclassified, unknown to the VEC or the military or the Church. A void breach in a town too small and too far from the grid to matter. Three people absorbed. And seven years later, something in the stone that was not silence.

She did not have the answer. She had the question — the one that had cost her everything, the one Malachi had Silenced her for asking.

"I do not know yet," she said. "But the Church says the absorbed are in the Silence — cut off, damned, gone. The VEC says they are dead. And the chorus says *neither of those things is true.*"

They made camp in the church hall.

It was simply the most intact building in the town — stone walls, heavy timber roof, the kind of construction that endured neglect better than the prefabricated residential blocks. The interior was dim, lit by the grey light filtering through windows that had been stained glass once and were now mostly broken, the remaining fragments casting fractured colors across the stone floor. Pews — actual wooden pews, a luxury in a world where most furniture was fabricated — stood in rows, dusty but solid. The altar at the far end was bare. Whatever sacred objects had been there — the blessed nitro, the resonance beads, the Book of Radiance — had been taken when the Church withdrew.

But the conduit housing was still in the walls. Nephthys could feel it — the faint residual hum of the nitro-infused infrastructure that had once powered the hall's resonance system. Every Church hall had one. The conduit network ran through the walls in a specific pattern — designed by Church architects to amplify the hum during the Radiance, to create a space where the congregation's harmonization would resonate with the infrastructure and produce the transcendent acoustic experience that the faithful called *hearing the Voice.*

She'd conducted the Radiance in halls like this. Hundreds of times. She'd stood at the altar and spoken the opening — *We were in Silence. The Voice spoke. We heard.* — and watched the congregation close their eyes and lean into the hum and add their voices to it, hundreds of people harmonizing with the conduit resonance, the sound building and building until the hall itself seemed to vibrate, until the walls and the floor and the air became a single instrument, and the faithful wept because they believed they were hearing God.

What had they been hearing?

She did not know. That was the honest answer — the one she gave herself in the dark, when the migraine was quiet and the chorus was distant and she could think without the pressure of revelation. She knew the hum was not one voice. She knew the chorus had structure. She knew that structure was not what the Church described, not the singular Voice of a loving creator resonating through blessed infrastructure. Beyond that — beyond the *not* — she had only questions. And the questions were enough to destroy a twenty-year career and a fifteen-year ordination and a life she had built inside the Church's architecture the way the faithful built their lives inside the hum.

She had told Malachi the questions. He had Silenced her for asking them. And the congregations continued to harmonize with something they had never examined and call it God.

Nephthys sat in the front pew and took the resonance beads from her pocket. Nitro-glass — small spheres strung on a cord, each one containing a trace of blessed nitro sealed inside the glass. They had been given to her at her ordination, fifteen years ago. They were warm. They were always warm. And they hummed — faintly, almost inaudibly, a sound you felt more than heard, the nitro inside the glass responding to the ambient resonance the way a tuning fork responds to a struck note.

She held them and listened.

The chorus was quieter here. Inside the hall, the stone walls dampened the residual coherence from the scar — not eliminated it, but reduced it to a level her nervous system could process without the ice-pick migraine. She could still feel the layered frequencies, the composite structure that was not one voice and not silence. But it was muted. Distant. Like hearing the ocean from inside a house — you knew it was there, you could feel its rhythm, but the walls gave you enough separation to think.

Imani was setting up the camp. Bedrolls on the pews — they'd learned early in the pilgrimage that sleeping on stone floors in the Outlands was a good way to wake up unable to walk. A small heating element powered by a salvaged nitro cell. Rations. Water. The medical kit, placed within arm's reach of Nephthys's pew, a gesture so habitual it had stopped being pointed and become simply part of the architecture of their evenings.

"The commune said the scar appeared overnight," Imani said, working the heating element. "Seven years ago. No warning. No RCI spike on any monitoring system — but the monitoring system had already been decommissioned when the grid pulled out. The residents who were still here reported a sound. A change in the hum. And then the square was — different. And three people were gone."

"Three."

"A couple who ran the café. And a maintenance worker who was inspecting the old converter station."

Nephthys closed her eyes. Three people. Absorbed. The Church would call this a trial — the void testing the faithful, to be endured through prayer and harmonization. The VEC would call it a sub-threshold event — too small to classify, too remote to investigate, too far from functioning infrastructure to matter. Neither institution would send anyone to listen to what the scar was still doing seven years later. Neither institution wanted to know.

"The chorus was stronger here than at any scar we have found," she said.

Imani looked up from the heating element. "Because it's closer to the old trunk-line?"

"Perhaps. Or because three people were taken here, and the other scars were smaller. One person at the relay station south of Bruges. One at the junction outside Metz." She opened her eyes. "I do not know if there is a relationship between the number absorbed and the intensity of the chorus. But the question is worth asking. And nobody is asking it."

Imani was quiet for a long time. The heating element hummed — a small, domestic sound, the ghost of the infrastructure that had once powered this hall. Outside, the wind moved through the empty town, and the church spire cast its shadow, and the scar in the square held its cold and its silence and its violet-tinged stillness.

"That's not what the Church teaches," Imani said finally.

"No."

"And it's not what the VEC would say."

"The VEC would say the absorbed are dead. The Church would say they are in the Silence — cut off from the Voice, damned. And the chorus says something that fits neither explanation. Something is still *happening* in these scars, Imani. Something structured. Something that responds to proximity. And the entire infrastructure of the civilized world — every conduit, every trunk-line, every converter station, every Church hall with resonance architecture in its walls — is carrying this signal, and nobody is listening because the Church has already decided what it means and the VEC has already decided it means nothing."

Her voice had risen. She caught it. Brought it back down. The migraine pulsed — a reminder, a cost, the biological price of proximity to what she was hearing. The tumor in her brain was real. The chorus was real. Both things were true simultaneously, and the space between them was where her theology lived.

"We need to reach Neo-Shanghai," she said.

Imani's hands stilled on the heating element. "That's — Nephthys, that's three thousand kilometers. Through the Outlands. We don't have transit credentials, we don't have—"

"The chorus is louder to the east. It has been louder to the east since we left the European See. Every scar we have found, every residual field — the signal is stronger in the direction of higher-throughput infrastructure. If I want to hear the chorus clearly enough to understand what it is, I need to go where the hum is loudest."

"Neo-Shanghai."

"The Cathedral of the Living Sound is in the Sump. I've heard of it from three separate Void Witness communes. A geological formation beneath the Resonance District that does something to the chorus — resolves it, clarifies it. If I can reach it—"

"If you can reach it, the chorus might kill you. The migraines are getting worse. The nosebleeds are more frequent. The diagnosis—"

"The diagnosis gives me eighteen months. Maybe less." Nephthys opened her eyes. The resonance beads were warm in her hands, humming their faint, faithful hum. "I can spend those eighteen months taking suppressants and hearing nothing. Or I can spend them listening."

Imani looked at her. The light through the broken stained glass caught her face — fragments of blue and amber and red, the colors of a faith she'd been raised in and followed into exile and was now watching transform into something she didn't recognize. She was twenty years old. She had given up her ordination track, her family's approval, her place in the institutional structure of the Church, to follow a woman who heard voices in dead towns and called them theology.

"You're asking me to help you walk toward the thing that's killing you."

"I am asking you to help me listen to the thing that is speaking."

Imani held her gaze. Then she turned back to the heating element and adjusted the flame.

"We'll need transit papers," she said. "Forged. The Void Witness commune near the border might know someone."

"Imani—"

"Don't thank me. I'm not doing this because I believe you." She didn't look up. Her hands were steady on the heating element, her voice level, her posture the careful architecture of someone holding themselves together through competence because the alternative was falling apart. "I'm doing this because if you're right, someone needs to be there who remembers how to tell the story afterward. And if you're wrong—" She paused. "If you're wrong, someone needs to be there."

Nephthys did not speak. She held the resonance beads and listened to the chorus — faint now, muted by the stone walls, but present. Always present. A structured signal carried through the residual coherence of a dead town's infrastructure, received by a woman whose brain was being consumed by the mechanism that allowed her to hear it.
