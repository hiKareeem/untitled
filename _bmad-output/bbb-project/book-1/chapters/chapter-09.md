## Draft v1

The room cost four hundred credits a week and smelled like the last occupant's cooking oil. Mirelle Dubois sat cross-legged on the floor with the contents of her investigation laid out in front of her in three rows.

Row one: paper. Actual paper — not printed from a frame, not generated from a template, but written by hand on thirty-two sheets of recycled pulp she'd bought from a vendor in the Hum Market who sold it by weight and didn't ask what it was for. Handwritten notes. Handwritten timelines. Handwritten names. Dao Suen. Chen Yue. Fourteen others — fourteen names she'd confirmed across five sectors in the last eight weeks, fourteen conduit maintenance workers who had walked toward trunk-line access points and not walked back, fourteen Feed entries that said *relocated* or *reassigned* or *credential transferred* in the same bureaucratic register that made disappearance sound like paperwork.

She'd written the names in chronological order. The first one she could date was eleven months before Dao — a converter station tech in Sector 7, name of Bai Lin, whose daughter had filed a missing persons report that was reclassified as an employment dispute within forty-eight hours. The most recent was nine days ago — a conduit welder in Sector 2, name of Ramos, whose partner had posted on the underground mesh and had the post removed within six hours for *unverified personnel claims.*

Sixteen disappearances. Five sectors. All within two hundred meters of a trunk-line junction or access point. All reclassified by the Feed within days. All invisible to anyone who wasn't cross-referencing municipal employment records with NitroCore infrastructure maps with residential block demographic data — and nobody was cross-referencing those things because nobody had access to all three databases simultaneously except Mirelle, who had access to none of them legitimately and all of them through the particular combination of borrowed credentials, cultivated sources, and patient, methodical theft that she called reporting.

Row two: data. Two encrypted drives — thumb-sized, solid-state, commercial hardware she'd bought in the Hum Market and wiped to factory spec before loading. Drive one held the compiled dataset: sixteen disappearances, RCI readings from eleven of the sixteen sites, NitroCore's public infrastructure reports for the last three years showing a pattern of tolerance-band widening that tracked with the disappearance timeline, and the Feed notices — every *relocated,* every *reassigned,* every *no disruption expected,* cataloged by date, sector, and proximity to conduit infrastructure.

Drive two was a mirror. Identical data. Different encryption key. One would stay with her. One would not.

Row three: her notes. Not the investigation — the *analysis.* What she thought the data meant. What she couldn't prove. What she suspected. Written in the raw, angry register she never published — the voice that existed between the clean sentences of her filed pieces and the careful inventory of her evidence. *They're not relocating people. They're losing them. The infrastructure is eating its workers and the company is filing the losses as transfers and nobody is counting because the count belongs to NitroCore and NitroCore has decided that sixteen is within tolerance.*

She didn't know if that was true. She didn't know what *the infrastructure is eating its workers* meant in mechanical terms. She had no mechanism. She had geography and timing and a Feed that lied and families who knew it lied and a chalk tradition on concrete walls that documented the truth in the only medium nobody could reclassify.

She sat with the three rows in front of her and turned her stylus end over end between her fingers. The stylus was a holdover — she wrote on paper because paper didn't ping the mesh and didn't get swept in a frame audit and didn't exist on any server NitroCore could subpoena. Same model she'd used at the bureau in Abidjan, where she'd learned that the difference between a journalist and a target was how many copies of the story existed and who held them.

One copy was not enough. One copy meant that removing her removed the story.

She picked up the second drive and held it in her palm. Small. Smooth. Cold from the floor.

She put the drive in her jacket pocket. She packed the paper into the false lining of her coat — thirty-two sheets, flat, invisible to a pat-down, felt like just another part of her frame, but findable if you knew where to squeeze. She put the first drive in her boot, under the insole, next to the RCI sensor she still hadn't returned.

She left the room. The door locked behind her — mechanical, no mesh connection, the reason she'd chosen this particular room in this particular building in Sump Sector 3, six blocks from the brightline and three strata above and below anything NitroCore would call interesting.

---

The route to the Resonance District was not direct. Mirelle had planned three variations, each calibrated to the shift schedule of the transit corridors and the patrol patterns she'd mapped over five weeks of walking the same routes at irregular intervals.

Today: Variation B. South through the Sector 3 commercial strip — crowded at this hour, the second-shift workers flowing toward the transit stairs in a current dense enough to disappear into. East at the Sector 4 junction, where the commercial corridor narrowed and the surveillance coverage thinned because NitroCore had downgraded their junction-access cameras to motion-detect after the last infrastructure budget reallocation. South again through the trunk-line underpass, where the hum was louder and the crowd was thinner and the light shifted from standard Sump-orange to something deeper — the saturated amber of first-generation conduit infrastructure, pipes laid a century and a half ago, the metal walls worn translucent by decades of nitro throughput.

She adjusted her ocular implant. Left eye — a subtle iris shift as the gain compensated for the darker spectrum. The implant was seven years old, mid-range, the kind of hardware a Sump worker might have. She'd had it tuned by an augmentation tech in the Hum Market — different contact from the Chén Academy engineer who'd calibrated her RCI sensor, different skill set, same principle: pay in favors, never in traceable credit. It recorded what she saw without pinging any network. The data lived in local storage behind her left orbit and transferred to her burner drive via physical cable once a day, in private, with the frame powered down.

The Resonance District announced itself before she reached it. Not visually — the streets looked the same, the same narrow corridors, the same low ceilings, the same feed-screens scrolling the same indifferent updates. It announced itself in her bones. The hum changed. Deepened. The vibration that had been a background presence since she'd left the commercial strip became a physical pressure — in her jaw, her sternum, the bone behind her ears. The Resonance District sat on first-generation trunk-lines, six of them converging beneath the neighborhood at the highest-throughput junction in the city, and the sound they produced was not the manageable drone of the regular Sump. It was older. Louder. The kind of sound that lived in your skeleton and rearranged your sleep.

She'd been in the Sump almost seven months. The nitro-ear had started three weeks ago — a faint, high-pitched tone in her right ear that surfaced when the ambient hum dropped, like a ringing that had always been there but needed quiet to be heard. It wasn't quiet, of course. There was no quiet in the Sump. But there were gradations, and her ear had started finding them.

The district's streets were narrower than the commercial strip. Fewer feed-screens. More counter-surveillance hardware in storefront windows — mesh scramblers, frame jammers, signal-dampening paint. The Hum Market was two blocks east, and its economy bled into the surrounding blocks: information brokerage, black-market ampoules, identity credential leasing, all conducted in the chronic high-RCI environment that kept corporate security from staying longer than they had to. The Resonance District policed itself by discomfort, not law. If the hum didn't drive you out, the Hollow Legion's territorial markers would, and if those didn't, the readings above thirty would make the decision for you the next time your frame flagged a health advisory.

Mirelle's frame didn't flag health advisories. She'd disabled that function in her second week.

She checked her route. No tails — she'd verified at three checkpoints built into the variation: a reflective surface at the Sector 4 junction, a deliberate stop at a food vendor where she could watch the approach corridor while buying something she wouldn't eat, and a left turn at the trunk-line underpass that committed her to a thirty-meter stretch with one exit and clear rear sightlines. Clean. Or clean enough. Certainty was a luxury she'd stopped expecting around the time the third source declined to speak to her.

She turned onto the street where Talia Ravid kept her node.

---

The node didn't look like anything. That was the point. From the street it was a repair shop — frame servicing, implant calibration, the kind of business that existed in every Sump commercial strip and attracted exactly the right kind of foot traffic: transient, anonymous, people who needed hardware fixed and didn't want to explain where the hardware came from.

The front room had a counter, a woman Mirelle had never seen before, and a display of refurbished frame components arranged on a backlit shelf. The woman looked up. Mirelle gave the current month's phrase — six words in Cantonese that changed every thirty days and which Talia distributed through a dead-letter system that required physical presence to retrieve.

The woman pressed something under the counter. A door behind the display shelf slid open with the mechanical efficiency of hardware that got used several times a day.

Mirelle walked through.

The back room was larger than the front — the building's exterior footprint lying about its interior, a common Resonance District arrangement, walls shifted, spaces repurposed, the architecture as fluid as the information that moved through it. The room was cool, well-lit by a spectrum that wasn't conduit-orange, and dense with hardware: storage racks, encrypted relay stations, three displays running data streams Mirelle didn't try to read, not because she didn't want to — because she *did* want to — but she was only four minutes early to a meeting she should've been five minutes early to. The hum was present but muted — Talia had invested in insulation, the serious kind, the kind that cost more than the hardware it protected.

Talia Ravid sat at a workstation in the center of the room with her back to the door. She didn't turn around.

"You're four minutes early," she said. Her voice was even, unhurried — the register of someone who tracked time the way other people tracked weather. Automatically. Without investment. "I charge for wasted time. I don't charge for early."

"I appreciate the distinction."

Talia turned. Ghanaian-Israeli, ageless in the way that good augmentation made possible — her skin smooth, her hair close-cropped, her eyes carrying the faint luminescent ring of military-grade optical hardware several generations above what Mirelle had in her left orbit. She wore a plain gray coverall. No jewelry. The only thing on her workstation besides the displays was a mug of tea and a data interface that looked like it could process more information per second than The Wire's entire Paris bureau.

"The package," Talia said. Not a question.

Mirelle reached into her jacket and set the second drive on the workstation. Small. Smooth. The whole weight of sixteen names in a piece of hardware lighter than a coin.

Talia didn't touch it. She looked at it the way an appraiser examines an uncut stone — evaluating not the object but the market it would move through.

"Contents."

"Sixteen confirmed disappearances across five Sump sectors. All conduit maintenance workers. All within two hundred meters of trunk-line infrastructure. All reclassified by the Feed as relocated or reassigned. RCI readings from eleven of the sixteen sites — all above baseline. NitroCore infrastructure reports showing tolerance-band widening that tracks with the disappearance timeline. Feed notices, demographic data, source interviews anonymized. My analysis."

"*Your* analysis." Talia's eyebrow shifted — a millimeter, the smallest possible expression of professional interest. "You're editorializing."

"I'm *contextualizing.* The data speaks for itself. The analysis is what the data says if you listen."

"My clients prefer to do their own listening."

"Your clients can delete the analysis file. It's partitioned."

Talia's mouth moved in what might have been approval. She picked up the drive and inserted it into the interface. Data structures populated on the displays, file trees expanding, the encrypted contents unpacking into Talia's system with the smooth efficiency of hardware designed for exactly this kind of intake.

"Clean encryption," Talia said. "Your contact at the Academy?"

Mirelle didn't answer. Talia didn't expect her to.

The data finished loading. Talia scrolled through the file tree — fast, practiced, her eyes moving with the faint luminescent pulse of her optical hardware processing the information faster than her conscious attention could follow. She paused on the RCI readings. Paused again on the geographic overlay — the map of disappearances laid against the conduit infrastructure map, sixteen points across five sectors, all clustering around trunk-line junctions.

"That's a pattern," Talia said.

"That's the story."

"It's a pattern. It becomes a story when someone publishes it. Right now it's a dataset with a compelling geographic correlation that any competent analyst could attribute to proximity bias." She turned from the display. "Why are you bringing this to me instead of your editor?"

"My editor wants a second source. A body. A VEC report. Something that isn't chalk on a wall."

"Your editor is correct."

"My editor is in Paris. Eight time zones from the nearest chalk name."

Talia regarded her. The luminescent ring in her eyes pulsed — slow, processing. Then she reached for her tea and took a sip. The mug was chipped. The tea smelled like mint.

"What do you want?"

"Dead-hand registration."

The room's ambient hum shifted register — or Mirelle imagined it did. The attenuated vibration through Talia's insulated walls. The quiet tick of cooling systems. The electrical whisper of hardware doing what hardware did.

"Terms," Talia said. Her voice hadn't changed. The word was procedural. She might have been discussing a parts order.

"Seventy-two-hour rolling window. I ping a designated address every seventy-two hours. If I miss two consecutive windows —"

"One hundred forty-four hours."

"— the package auto-distributes to a pre-set recipient list."

"How many recipients?"

"Three."

"I'll need the addresses encrypted and sealed. I don't see them. I don't know them. The auto-distribute triggers on missed windows and routes to addresses I can't read. That's the architecture."

"That's the architecture."

Talia set down her tea. "The Babel price."

Black Babel didn't work for free, and Talia didn't work for money — or not only for money. The node's economy ran on information, and information was what Mirelle had.

"Source network metadata," Mirelle said. "Anonymized. No names, no locations, no identifying details. The structure — how many sources, what sectors, what access tiers, what institutional affiliations. The pattern of my network without the identities."

"That's valuable."

"That's the point."

"It's valuable because it tells my other clients where to look for the kind of people who talk to journalists. You understand that."

Mirelle understood it. The metadata wouldn't identify her sources — she'd stripped every node to its categorical skeleton. But the skeleton was valuable. It told anyone who bought it that someone had cultivated sources at these institutional levels, in these sectors, with these access patterns. A map of vulnerability — not of the sources themselves but of the gaps in NitroCore's information security that the sources represented.

"I understand it."

"And you're comfortable with it."

"I'm comfortable with the tradeoff."

Talia studied her. The luminescent ring was steady — not processing, *observing.* "Most of my clients negotiate. They want a better price. They want to pay less. You're paying exactly what I quoted and you're not pushing back." She paused. "That tells me something about your timeline. You're not in a hurry because the story is ready. You're in a hurry because you're not sure you'll be around when it is."

Mirelle picked up her stylus from the workstation where she'd set it down and turned it end over end between her fingers. Once. Twice.

"Register the dead-hand," she said.

Talia held her gaze for another second. Then she turned back to the interface and her fingers moved — fast, precise, the mechanical vocabulary of someone who had built and maintained information architecture for longer than Mirelle had been a journalist.

"Package received," Talia read from the registration confirmation on her display. "Dead-hand registered. Client: M. Expiry: seventy-two hours rolling. Node RD-14." She paused. "The first window starts now. Seventy-two hours. Miss one, nothing happens. Miss two —"

"I know the terms."

"— the package goes where you told it to go and I never see the addresses and I never know what happened and as far as my records are concerned, I stored a data cache for a client who stopped checking in." Talia turned from the display. "That's the service. No sentiment. No follow-up. No posthumous investigation. I'm a node, not a mourner."

"I'm not asking you to mourn."

"Good. I'm expensive and bad at it." She picked up her mug. The tea had gone cold. She drank it anyway. "Anything else?"

"Is there anything crossing your node I should know about? Sump-adjacent. Infrastructure. NitroCore classifications."

Talia's expression didn't change. But the luminescent ring pulsed — once, slow — and Mirelle recognized that pulse. She'd seen it in their previous transactions, the handful of times she'd bought data from this node, always small purchases, always specific, always paid for in information or in favors she'd rather not have owed. The pulse meant Talia was deciding what to sell her.

"There's a classification upgrade on seven Sump-adjacent conduit junctions," Talia said. "Effective next quarter. Maintenance access reclassified from municipal to restricted-corporate. The authorization chain moves from local supervisors to NitroCore's regional infrastructure directorate."

"Which junctions?"

Talia named them. Three of the seven were on Mirelle's map — junctions within two hundred meters of disappearance sites.

"The price?"

"Free." Talia set down the mug. "I profit from what you do with it. You publish, markets move, my other clients get nervous, prices go up. The chaos you create is good for business." She paused. "The data you just gave me is worth more than the dead-hand fee. Consider the junction list a receipt."

Mirelle didn't thank her. Talia didn't expect thanks. The transaction was complete — the architecture built, the failsafe registered, the price paid.

She picked up her stylus and put it in her pocket.

"Seventy-two hours," Talia said.

"Seventy-two hours."

Mirelle walked out through the repair shop. The woman at the counter didn't look up.

---

The Resonance District was louder at street level than it had been inside Talia's node. The insulation had done its work — the transition from the node's attenuated hum to the district's full-spectrum vibration was like stepping from a sealed room into open water. Mirelle felt it in her jaw, her sternum, the ringing in her right ear that spiked and then settled into the constant high tone she'd been carrying for three weeks.

She walked north. Back toward the Sector 3 commercial strip, but not the same route — Variation C, the return leg, designed to avoid retracing her approach. Through the market-adjacent streets where the Hum Market's commerce spilled into the surrounding corridors — vendors selling ampoules, frame components, counter-surveillance tools, all under the orange glow that was deeper here, more saturated, the light of pipes that had been carrying nitro since before the Sump was the Sump.

Her coat was lighter. The jacket pocket empty now — the mirror drive with Talia. Thirty-two sheets of paper still in the lining, the first drive still in her boot. The data she'd given Talia was identical to the data she was carrying, held now by someone whose business model depended on keeping it intact.

Sixteen names. Five sectors. A geographic correlation that any competent analyst could attribute to proximity bias. But Talia had said *that's a pattern* first, before she'd said anything else, and Talia's professional interest was not easy to earn.

She thought about Gauthier. *What do you actually have?* She had more now. She had the junction reclassifications — seven conduit junctions moving from municipal to restricted-corporate next quarter. Three of them on her map. That wasn't anecdotal. That was NitroCore restructuring access to the same locations where workers had disappeared, and the restructuring hadn't been announced publicly, and the timing was the kind of thing that made patterns into stories.

She needed to get inside those junctions before the reclassification took effect. Before restricted-corporate access meant biometric clearance levels she couldn't borrow or steal. She had weeks. Maybe less.

The tolerance bands were widening. The Feed was reclassifying. The access was being locked down — not because something was wrong but because the definition of wrong was being adjusted to accommodate whatever was happening, and Mirelle was watching the adjustment in real time and documenting it on recycled paper because that was the only medium that didn't exist on any mesh.

She reached the Sector 3 commercial strip. The crowd absorbed her — second shift ending, third shift beginning, the Sump's metabolism cycling workers through its corridors the way the conduit network cycled nitro through its pipes. She moved with the current. Unremarkable. Her frame running the visible layer that said *Sump resident, textile sector, credential tier Basic* while the private partition recorded nothing and the implant behind her left eye stored the route home in local memory.

A feed-screen scrolled above the transit stairs. Weather. Transit advisory. Employment listings.

And between them, in the same font, at the same scroll speed:

**PERSONNEL UPDATE: Conduit maintenance restructuring continues across five sectors. All affected personnel reassigned. No disruption to service expected. — NitroCore Sump Division**

*Five sectors.* The language was expanding. When she'd started, the Feed notices had been local — single junctions, individual crew restructurings, the bureaucratic grammar of one disappearance at a time. Now it was five sectors. The reclassification was outpacing her count.

She didn't stop walking. She'd learned that eight weeks ago, outside Lien Suen's building — you don't stop in the current. You don't become the obstacle the flow routes around. You move with it and you record what you see and you keep the data on hardware that doesn't exist on any network and you check in every seventy-two hours so that the machine you've built keeps holding the weight.

The hum was in her teeth. The ringing in her right ear was steady — the new constant, the sound her body had started making in response to the sound the infrastructure made in response to whatever was happening in the trunk-lines beneath the Resonance District where six first-generation conduit lines converged at the highest-throughput junction in the city and the readings were above thirty and the people who lived on top of it called the sound *normal* because they'd never heard anything else.

She climbed the transit stairs toward the brightline. The orange glow thinned as she ascended — conduit light giving way to the filtered white of the Mid-Levels, the architectural boundary where the Sump ended and the city above it could pretend the Sump didn't exist.

She had seventy-two hours. Then she would ping the address Talia had given her, and the timer would reset, and the architecture would hold.

Seventy-two hours. Then seventy-two more.
