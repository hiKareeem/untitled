## Draft v1

The woman was waiting on the bench.

Not the same bench. The welded one in the ascent shaft corridor had been removed — cut at the mounting bolts, the metal still bright where the torch had gone through. This bench was wood. Composite. Bolted to the wall outside the water reclamation plant on Level 2, where the corridor widened into what the municipal planning documents called a "community gathering node" and what everyone else called the place where the benches were.

She looked the same. Grey temples. Water-recycling patch on her jacket. Hands that had spent decades touching infrastructure that didn't care if you were in the way.

Fuxi sat.

"Thursday," she said.

"Thursday," Fuxi said.

She didn't ask about the reading. She didn't ask about Chen Wei leaving forty-seven minutes early or the automated alert or the VEC cross-reference to a document he couldn't access. She sat with her hands in her lap and waited the way people waited when they knew the other person would talk when they were ready.

Fuxi was not ready.

He said: "It didn't self-correct."

"No," she said.

"Forty-three point one. Sustained."

"We know."

The hum pressed through the walls. Not louder. Not different. The same composite frequency it had always been — the accumulated signal of a hundred trunk-lines filtered through concrete and polymer and the mass of a neighbourhood built on top of infrastructure that was, as of two days ago, doing something it had never done before.

"How do you know?" Fuxi said.

"Because we've been watching the same readings you have," she said. "Different access. Same data."

She pushed back her sleeve. The frame on her forearm was older than Fuxi's — the housing split along both edges, the display dim enough that she had to angle her arm toward the corridor light to make the readout visible.

She extended her arm so he could see.

A table. Columns: date, location, RCI peak, duration. Sixty-three rows. The last entry was today's date, 43.1, sustained. The entry above it was from eleven days ago. The entry above that was from three weeks before. The spacing matched his log. The numbers matched his log. The pattern was his pattern, rendered in someone else's file.

"You're not the only one counting," she said.

Fuxi looked at the display. At the numbers. At the dates that lined up with his dates and the peaks that lined up with his peaks and the durations that tracked the acceleration he'd been documenting for fourteen weeks in a file hidden in a gasket-requisition partition that nobody audited.

"Who else?" he said.

"Enough," she said. She deactivated the frame. Pulled her sleeve down. "You want to meet them?"

---

The meeting was in a decommissioned pump station three levels below residential — one of the old municipal water nodes that had been bypassed when the grid consolidated in the fifties, left in place because removing infrastructure cost more than sealing it. The access corridor was narrow. The door was steel. The lock was mechanical — no frame interface, no biometric reader, just a keypad with buttons worn smooth by years of the same six-digit code.

The woman — she still hadn't given her name — punched in the code. The lock clicked. The door opened.

Inside: twelve people.

Not a crowd. Not a rally. Twelve maintenance workers sitting on overturned crates and sections of decommissioned pipe in a room lit by two battery lamps and the glow of cracked frames. The air smelled like coolant and old concrete. The hum was different here — muffled by the pump station's thermal shielding, reduced to a bass drone that Fuxi felt in his sternum more than heard.

They looked up when he entered. Nobody spoke. Nobody stood. They just looked — the way shift workers looked when someone new joined the crew and the crew was deciding whether the new person knew what they were doing.

Fuxi knew what he was doing. He'd been doing it for thirteen years.

The woman gestured to an empty crate. Fuxi sat.

"This is Fuxi," she said. "Trunk-line monitor. Grade 3. Deep Sump, Section 4-East. Thirteen years."

A man across the room nodded. Older than Fuxi. Fifties. Conduit burns on both forearms. "Wei," he said. "Pressure management. Twenty-two years."

A woman to Fuxi's left: "Lian. Junction housing. Sixteen years."

Around the room: names, specializations, years of service. Conduit techs. Pressure monitors. Junction mechanics. Thermal management. The accumulated expertise of two hundred and fourteen years of maintaining the infrastructure that kept the city running.

Nobody asked why he was there. Nobody asked what he'd seen. They already knew. That's why he was sitting on a crate in a decommissioned pump station with eleven other people who had spent decades touching the same pipes he had and noticing the same things he'd noticed and filing the same reports he'd filed and watching those reports get absorbed into a system designed to metabolize inconvenient information.

The woman who'd brought him here — the one with the grey temples — sat on a section of pipe near the door. She activated her frame again. Opened a file. Held her arm out so the room could see.

"Forty-three point one," she said. "Step-function. No correction. Sustained for forty-eight hours and counting."

Nobody reacted. They'd already seen it. They'd been watching the same readings Fuxi had been watching, through whatever access they had — monitoring bays, junction telemetry, pressure management interfaces, the distributed sensor network that every maintenance worker in the Sump carried in their frame and their body and their thirteen or sixteen or twenty-two years of knowing what normal looked like.

"That's the eighth spike," she said. "Fourteen weeks. Acceleration pattern confirmed. Decay intervals: forty seconds, thirty-four, twenty-six, twenty-two, nineteen, fourteen, eleven. Then nothing. Step-function."

She closed the frame. Looked at Fuxi.

"You've been logging them," she said. Not a question.

"Yes," Fuxi said.

"How many entries?"

"Sixty-two."

"Correlations?"

Fuxi didn't answer immediately. The correlations were the thing he hadn't written down — not in the log, not in any file. The correlations were the pattern underneath the pattern, the thing that turned anomalous readings into something else. Something that required a word he didn't have.

"Disappearances," he said. "Residential. Timing matches the spikes. Within six hours."

The room was quiet. Not the quiet of surprise. The quiet of confirmation.

Wei — the pressure management tech with twenty-two years — leaned forward. "We've been tracking the same thing," he said. "Different access points. Same pattern. Forty-one names over eleven weeks."

Forty-one. Fuxi's log had twenty-three. The gap was the difference between what one monitor could see and what a network could see. The gap was the shape of the thing they were all looking at from different angles.

"What are they?" Fuxi said.

"Not disappearances," Wei said. "Absorptions."

The word landed in the room like a reading that wouldn't self-correct.

Fuxi knew the word. Everyone in the Sump knew the word. Absorption was what happened during void incursions — the lattice advancing, the wet film on infrastructure, the people who touched it and didn't come back. Absorption was a Mid-Levels problem. A Spires problem. A thing that happened in breach zones, not residential corridors. Not in the Sump, where the void hadn't breached in eight years.

"There haven't been any incursions," Fuxi said.

"No," Wei said. "There haven't."

Lian — the junction housing tech — spoke for the first time. Her voice was quiet. Precise. The voice of someone who had spent sixteen years reading blueprints and work orders and installation logs. "The infrastructure is doing it," she said.

Fuxi looked at her.

"The conduits," she said. "The trunk-lines. The junctions. The network we maintain. It's not distributing nitro anymore. It's distributing something else."

She activated her own frame. Opened a file. Extended her arm toward Fuxi.

Photographs. Junction housings. Conduit access panels. Valve assemblies. The images were low-resolution — taken with a frame's camera in low light, the kind of documentation you did when you couldn't requisition proper equipment and didn't want to explain why you needed it.

"These were installed four years ago," Lian said. "Work orders said 'pressure management upgrade.' Standard municipal contract. We installed them. Signed off. Moved to the next junction."

She swiped to the next image. A valve assembly. Industrial-scale. The housing was marked with a serial number and a NitroCore logo.

"This is a flood valve," she said. "Designed to redirect trunk-line flow. Seal sections. Isolate zones." She swiped again. Another valve. Another junction. "There are forty-seven of them. Installed between 2171 and 2173. Every major junction in the Sump."

Fuxi stared at the display. At the valve assembly. At the serial number. At the NitroCore logo that marked it as corporate hardware, not municipal. At the installation date that was three years before the first spike in his log.

"Why?" he said.

"Because the system was always designed to do this," Lian said. She closed the frame. "The conduits aren't just power distribution. They're a delivery system. And we've been maintaining it."

---

The meeting lasted ninety minutes.

They didn't talk about sabotage. They didn't talk about whistleblowing or strikes or shutting down the grid. They talked about documentation. About correlating data. About building a record that was complete enough and specific enough that when the thing they were all watching finally happened — when the step-function spike became something worse, when the flood valves opened, when the delivery system activated — someone would know it had been visible.

Someone would know they had seen it.

"Keep logging," the woman with the grey temples said. She was standing now, near the door, her frame lit on her wrist. "Your file. Our frame. When this breaks, the data goes public. Not through official channels. Through networks that can't be suppressed."

"What networks?" Fuxi said.

"Black Babel," she said. "The Wire. Erasure List. People who've been building parallel information architecture for years because they knew the official channels were designed to fail."

Fuxi had heard of Black Babel. Everyone in the Sump had heard of Black Babel — the data relay network that moved information the Feed wouldn't carry, that connected resistance cells and underground media and anyone else who needed to move evidence past institutional gatekeepers. He'd never used it. He'd never needed to. He'd always filed his reports through official channels because official channels were the procedure and procedure was what a technician did.

The official channels had produced nothing.

"How does it work?" Fuxi said.

"You keep doing what you're doing," she said. "Document everything. When the time comes, we move the data. You don't need to know the mechanism. You just need to keep the file clean."

She looked around the room. At the eleven other people sitting on crates and pipe sections. At the frames glowing in the dim light. At the two hundred and fourteen years of accumulated expertise that had been dismissed and ignored and metabolized by a system designed to continue regardless of what the people maintaining it could see.

"We're not saboteurs," she said. "We're not revolutionaries. We're maintenance workers. We know how to read infrastructure. And the infrastructure is telling us what it's designed to do."

She paused. The hum pressed through the walls. Muffled. Persistent.

"Keep documenting," she said. "When this breaks, someone needs to know it was visible."

---

Fuxi walked home through corridors that looked the same as they had this morning.

The lights were on. The hum was the hum. A child ran past — not the same child from two days ago, a different child, playing in the narrow space between the wall and the conduit housing. The corridor smelled like cooking oil and recycled air and the accumulated scent of a neighbourhood that had lived here for decades.

Nothing had changed.

Everything had changed.

The file in his pocket was the same file it had been this morning. Sixty-two entries. The same gasket-requisition filename. The same directory that nobody audited. But the file wasn't his anymore. It was part of something larger — a network of files, a distributed record, forty-one names and sixty-three readings and two hundred and fourteen years of expertise that had been watching the same pattern from different angles and assembling the picture that no single person could see.

The picture was a delivery system.

The picture was forty-seven flood valves installed between 2171 and 2173.

The picture was a step-function spike that wasn't self-correcting and wouldn't self-correct because the system had transitioned into a state it had been designed to reach.

Fuxi reached his door. Stepped onto the mat. Pulled off his boots.

The apartment smelled like broth. Nuwa was at the stove. Her back to him. Her shoulders were tight — not the relaxed posture from last week, not the dropped tension of someone whose transfer plan was progressing. The tightness was back. The performance was harder to maintain.

She turned. Looked at him.

"Long shift?" she said.

"Yes," Fuxi said.

She didn't ask why. She turned back to the stove. Stirred the pot. The motion was automatic. Practiced. The same motion she'd been making since she was sixteen and he was nineteen and their mother was coughing in the bedroom and someone had to cook.

Fuxi sat at the table. Unclipped the frame from his wrist. Set it on the table. The display was dark. The file was closed. Sixty-two entries and a network he hadn't known existed and a meeting in a decommissioned pump station where eleven people had confirmed what he'd been documenting for fourteen weeks.

The infrastructure was a weapon.

He'd been maintaining it for thirteen years.

Nuwa brought the bowls to the table. Set one in front of him. Sat across from him with her own. She didn't look at the frame. She looked at him.

"You're not eating," she said.

Fuxi picked up the spoon. The broth was hot. The same broth she'd been making for years — protein supplement, vegetable base, the subsidized rations stretched into something that tasted like food. He ate. The motion was automatic. The taste was familiar.

Nuwa ate in silence. Her jaw was tight. Her hands moved with the same precision they always moved with — the efficiency of someone who had learned to do everything with minimal motion because motion cost energy and energy was the thing you conserved.

She finished first. Set down the spoon. Looked at him.

"The transfer application," she said. "It was denied."

Fuxi stopped eating.

"Reason code 14-J," she said. "Infrastructure personnel retention priority. They're not processing Mid-Level transfers for municipal service workers. Not this quarter."

She said it the way she said everything — calm, factual, the emotion underneath it locked down so tight that only the tightness in her jaw gave it away.

"When did you find out?" Fuxi said.

"This morning," she said. "The notification came through at 06:00. I didn't tell you because you were already at work and I didn't want to—" She stopped. Looked at her hands. "I didn't want to say it out loud."

Fuxi set down his spoon. The broth was half-finished. The bowl was still hot. The apartment was quiet except for the hum — the same frequency it had been for his entire life, for Nuwa's entire life, for their parents' lives before them.

"We can reapply," he said. "Next quarter."

"They're not processing transfers," Nuwa said. "Not deferring them. Not processing them. The retention priority is indefinite."

Indefinite. The word sat between them like a reading that wouldn't self-correct.

Fuxi looked at his sister. At her hands. At the chemical staining on her fingertips that she never mentioned and he never asked about. At the tightness in her shoulders that had been absent last week and was back now, heavier than before. At the performance of normalcy that was cracking under the weight of a system that had just told her the architecture of rising she'd been building for months was not going to happen.

"I'm sorry," he said.

Nuwa didn't respond. She stood. Picked up the bowls. Walked to the sink. Turned on the water. The sound filled the apartment — the hiss of recycled water through pipes that Fuxi maintained, that carried the nitro that powered the city, that were designed to do something else when the flood valves opened.

He thought about telling her. About the meeting. About the network. About the forty-seven valves and the step-function spike and the delivery system that had been installed four years ago under work orders labeled "pressure management upgrade."

He thought about telling her to pack a bag.

He didn't.

Because where would they go? The transit permits were denied. The transfer application was denied. The retention priority was indefinite. The system had sealed them in — not with pressure doors, not with manual bolts, but with administrative codes and retention priorities and the quiet machinery of a system that needed its maintenance workers exactly where they were.

Nuwa finished washing the bowls. Dried her hands. Turned off the light. Walked to her room without saying goodnight.

Fuxi sat at the table in the dark. The frame was still in front of him. The file was still closed. Sixty-two entries and a network and a meeting scheduled for next Tuesday.

The hum pressed through the walls.

He activated the frame. Opened the file. Typed:

*Entry 63. Transfer applications denied. Retention priority indefinite. Infrastructure personnel. Municipal service.*

He closed the file. The display went dark.

The apartment was dark. The hum was the hum. Nuwa's door was closed.

Tuesday was five days away.
