The hum in Block 14 had been wrong since morning.

Not louder. Nuwa had lived with loud her entire life — the low E-flat that rose through the floor of an apartment built on top of infrastructure no one had maintained since her father's time. Loud was the baseline. Loud was home. This was something else. The frequency had shifted from a drone to a pressure, the kind of sound that didn't register in the ears but in the chest, behind the sternum, where the body kept the things it couldn't name.

She was sitting at the kitchen counter when the power went.

No flicker. No stutter of lights trying to hold. The conduit glow through the frosted polymer window dropped from orange to nothing — a color she'd never seen in this apartment. The absence of orange. The blue textile on the mattress vanished into the dark. The Frame overlay on her implant scrambled, threw artifacts, reset to emergency mode: white text on black. A single notification populated.

*Municipal alert: Infrastructure event. Remain indoors. Do not approach conduit access points.*

She read it twice. The words were administrative. The hum was not.

The implant behind her left ear was itching — not the normal itch, the low-grade irritation she'd learned to sleep through by turning the disc toward the wall. This was heat. The external housing was warm to the touch when she pressed her fingers to it, and the warmth was climbing. She could feel the disc's edge through her skin, the housing seams that a better implant would have hidden beneath subderm plastic.

Nine hundred creds toward the upgrade. Three hundred short.

The number arrived before anything else — before the question of what was happening outside the window, before the calculation of what she should do about it. Nine hundred saved. Three hundred remaining. The tally ran itself, automatic, the way it ran every morning before she opened her eyes. Her mind's first language was arithmetic. Everything else was translation.

The hum thickened.

Not the way it thickened during power outages — she'd lived through dozens, knew the pattern, the conduit glow dimming and the resonance backing up through the trunk-lines like water pooling at a lower level. This was different. The sound was wet. That was the word her body chose before her mind could override it. *Wet.* As if the infrastructure beneath Block 14 had been flooded with something denser than what usually ran through it, something that carried the vibration differently — heavier, more saturated, alive in a way that conduit resonance was not supposed to be alive.

She stood. The chair scraped against the floor. Too loud in the dark.

The kitchen counter: half a meal. Rice in the pot, not yet seasoned. She'd been cooking — the one thing she chose, the one domain where she controlled what entered her body, what she tasted, what she made with her own hands instead of accepting what the canteen dispensed. The rice was still warm. The gas had cut with the power.

She counted without deciding to count. Rice: enough for two meals. Water in the filter jug: three liters. Emergency credit stash behind the water heater: four hundred and twelve creds, physical chip, unregistered. Implant charge: 47%, emergency mode. Fuxi's last message, six hours ago: *Double shift. Converter station. Don't wait up.*

He was in Deep Sump. Two hundred meters below where she was standing.

The hum pulsed. A low, dense contraction — not a spike, not a fluctuation. A squeeze. As if the infrastructure beneath the building had clenched. The compressed foam mattress vibrated. The blue textile shifted on its surface. The pot on the counter rattled against the burner grate.

Nuwa did not move toward the door. She did not reach for her jacket or her bag or the emergency kit she didn't have because emergency kits cost money and the municipal distribution points had been "temporarily suspended" for six weeks. She stood in the kitchen of an apartment she shared with her brother in a building she'd lived in since she was born, and she listened.

The hum had always been white noise. Tonight it had a direction.

---

The frosted polymer window faced the adjacent block — eighteen meters of dead air between Building 14 and Building 11, a gap that existed because the original construction codes required ventilation corridors for conduit heat dissipation. The gap was too narrow for sky. Too narrow for weather. Wide enough to see the walkway that connected the two buildings at the fourth floor, and the conduit access panel on Building 11's exterior wall, and the laundry lines that Mrs. Huang strung between the window brackets because the shared drying room on level two had been "repurposed for infrastructure access" three years ago and nobody had repurposed it back.

Mrs. Huang's laundry was still on the line. Dark shapes against nothing.

Nuwa went to the window. She didn't decide to go — her body moved the way it always moved through the apartment, along the path worn into the floor polymer by years of the same route: counter to window, window to mattress, mattress to door. The route avoided the bathroom pipe that ran along the east wall — metal, cold, the source of the worst hum-itch at night. She'd routed around it since she was a teenager without noticing she was routing around it.

She put her hand on the windowsill. The polymer was warm.

It should not have been warm.

The walkway between the buildings was dark. Emergency strips along the railing — the ones that were supposed to activate during power failures — had not activated. In the dark, the space between the buildings had no depth. Just shapes.

Then the shapes moved.

People. Three — four — coming down the walkway from Building 11. Running. The sound of their feet on the metal grating was sharp and wrong, percussive in a way that footsteps on grating shouldn't be. Too fast. Too hard. One of them was carrying something. A bag. A child. She couldn't tell in the dark.

The hum changed again. The wet heaviness that had been building beneath the floor surged — not upward through the building, but outward, along the conduit paths, through the metal infrastructure that connected every building in the Sump to every other building. The walkway between 14 and 11 was metal. The railing was metal. The grating was metal.

The person in front stopped running.

Nuwa watched. The emergency overlay on her implant was throwing data — RCI values she had never learned to read, numbers that meant nothing to her, that meant everything to Fuxi, that meant the difference between a maintenance log and a death sentence. The numbers climbed. She didn't know the threshold. She knew the person on the walkway had stopped, and the person behind them had stopped, and the two behind them were pressed against the railing, and the railing was glowing.

Not orange. Not the conduit glow she'd been born into, the color of home and industry and the air she'd breathed her whole life.

*Violet.*

The first person on the walkway was — she didn't have the word. Not falling. Not collapsing. *Joining.* The body wasn't coming apart. It was going into the grating, into the railing, into the conduit panel on Building 11's wall — sinking the way water sinks into concrete, except the shape held for a moment, the outline of a person pressed into metal, embedded, and then the metal had them and they were part of the walkway the way a bolt is part of a walkway.

The second person turned to run back. Got three steps. The violet reached them through the railing they were gripping.

Nuwa's hand was still on the windowsill. The polymer was hot now.

She should move. The thought arrived with the flat clarity of a shift schedule — a fact, an entry in the ledger of things that needed to happen in sequence. Move. Away from the window. Away from the walls where conduit paths ran behind the plaster. Away from the east wall pipe. Away from the floor where the trunk-lines hummed beneath six floors of concrete.

A hand hit the window.

Not the walkway. Her window. The frosted polymer — someone on the narrow maintenance ledge outside, someone who had climbed or crawled to the face of the building, and their hand was flat against the polymer from the outside and Nuwa's hand was flat against the polymer from the inside and between them was three centimeters of building material and whatever was happening in the infrastructure of Block 14.

The hand was small. A woman's. Short fingers. Nuwa could see the chemical staining on the fingertips, iridescent under the violet glow bleeding through the building's exterior conduit paths. Textile reclamation staining. The same staining on Nuwa's own hands.

The hand pressed harder. The polymer flexed.

Then the hand was not there.

Not removed. Not pulled away. *Taken.* The hand sank into the polymer the way the people on the walkway had sunk into the grating — drawn inward, the fingers flattening, the palm spreading, the outline holding for a moment like a fossil in setting stone. Then the window was just a window. The ledge outside was empty. The sound from the walkway had gone quiet.

Nuwa looked at her own hand on the sill. The staining on her fingertips. Iridescent.

She pulled her hand back. Held it against her chest. Felt her heartbeat through the knuckles — too fast, too shallow, a rhythm she recognized from the nights when the hum-itch flared and she lay awake calculating how many months until the implant upgrade. Her body's crisis language was the same for everything: heartbeat, lists, arithmetic.

Nine hundred creds saved. Three hundred short. The upgrade wouldn't stop the itching. The upgrade didn't exist anymore. The world that sold implant upgrades was on the other side of whatever was happening on the walkway.

She did not go to the door. She did not go to the window to look for Fuxi.

This was the choice. Not a moment of decision — not the kind of choice that announces itself with weight and the awareness that you are choosing. This was subtraction. She subtracted the door. She subtracted the window. She subtracted Fuxi's name from the list of things she could act on, the way she'd subtracted her father's name at eleven, the way she'd subtracted Lian's unanswered photo at twenty-one, the way she'd subtracted the transit permit and the implant upgrade and the room with a window above the hum-line — one item at a time, not grief, not surrender, just the arithmetic of what remained.

What remained: the kitchen. The water. The cloth.

She soaked the dishcloth under the filter jug. Three liters, minus two hundred milliliters. She pressed the cloth to her face. It was cold. The cold was the first real thing she'd felt since the power went.

Then she moved. Not toward the door, where the corridor was metal-framed and the stairwell ran alongside the building's primary conduit riser. Not toward the window, where the walkway grating was conducting whatever was dissolving people into infrastructure. Toward the back of the building. Toward the thick walls. Toward the room she'd never named but had been walking toward her entire life.

She took the back stairwell — the one with masonry walls instead of metal framing, the one that took three extra minutes but didn't make her implant itch. Wet cloth on her face. One hand on the wall. The concrete was cool. The hum was in it — the hum was in everything — but the wall didn't vibrate the way the metal pipes vibrated. The sound here was muffled. Indirect. Arriving through stone instead of steel.

Three floors down. The emergency strips in the stairwell were dead. The only light was violet glow bleeding through a gap where the masonry met the original building frame at the second-floor landing. She didn't look at it. She looked at her hand on the wall.

The staining on her fingertips glowed faintly in the violet light.

She kept moving.

---

The masonry annex was dark and cold and full of other people's abandoned things.

Nuwa pulled the door shut behind her. A manual door — no electronic lock, no biometric reader, no connection to the building's systems. A metal handle on a wooden frame set into poured concrete. She pushed a broken shelving unit against it. Not because the door needed barricading — nothing outside operated by opening doors — but because her hands needed something to do, and the act of moving a heavy thing into place gave her body a task that didn't require thinking about the hand on the window.

She sat in the corner farthest from the door. The floor was concrete. Cold through her work trousers. She pressed her back against the masonry wall and pulled her knees to her chest and held the wet cloth to her face and breathed.

The hum was here. Quieter than upstairs. Muffled by the density of the walls and the absence of conduit paths. But present — the low E-flat that had been the soundtrack of her life, the note her building generated because it sat on a decommissioned junction, the frequency she'd fallen asleep to every night since she could remember. The E-flat was still there. But braided into it — threaded through like a heavier strand in a rope — was the new sound. The wet sound. The pressure that felt directed.

She breathed through the cloth.

The air tasted like water and dust and the faint chemical sweetness she associated with the Sump's atmosphere — nitro residue, conduit off-gassing, the breath of infrastructure that surrounded every building and filled every gap and was now, outside this room, filling every person who hadn't reached a room like this one.

She could hear things through the walls. Not screams. Not anymore. The screaming had been brief — she'd heard it through the stairwell, sharp and then not sharp, the way a sound cuts off when its source stops being a source. What she heard now was the hum itself, but textured. Layered with frequencies she hadn't heard before, harmonics that didn't belong to the E-flat, that didn't belong to any note the building had ever produced.

The implant behind her ear was burning.

She reached up and pressed her fingers to the housing. The metal was hot — hotter than she'd ever felt it, hotter than the days when the RCI climbed and the itch became a throb and she lay awake counting the months until the upgrade. The emergency overlay in her peripheral vision was glitching: numbers, fragments, a notification that loaded half its text and froze.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

She stared at it. The words hung in her vision like a label on a shelf. An inventory tag. She was an item in a registry, and the registry had assessed her, and the assessment was that she did not warrant evacuating.

*None assigned.* Not "low priority." Not "deferred." The system hadn't deprioritized her. It had never put her in the calculation at all.

She closed her eyes. The notification remained — burned into the implant's display, persistent, the kind of system message that couldn't be dismissed because it wasn't a message. It was a classification. Classifications didn't have close buttons.

In the dark behind her eyelids, the hum filled the space. She could feel it in her spine. In her jaw. In the bones of her hands where she pressed them against her shins. The E-flat was still there — her note, her building's note — but the new harmonics were louder now, and they carried something she didn't have a framework for. Not words. Not meaning. A density. As if the conduit network that threaded through every wall and floor and ceiling of the Sump had taken so much into itself that it had changed its own resonance.

The wet cloth on her face was warming. She re-soaked it from the jug — three liters minus what she'd used. She was rationing without deciding to ration. The arithmetic was automatic. Three liters. Cloth soaking: approximately two hundred milliliters per application. Fifteen applications. She didn't know how long she'd be here. She didn't know if *how long* was a relevant question.

Time passed. She didn't know how much. Her implant's clock had frozen with the rest of the overlay. The notification was still there — credential tier, evacuation priority, the system's final word on what she was worth.

Then the hum detonated.

Not louder. Not the thickening she'd felt upstairs, not the wet pressure that had built through the stairwell. This was a different order — a single pulse that passed through the masonry walls as if they weren't there, through the concrete floor, through the metal handle of the door, through her spine and her jaw and the implant housing behind her ear. The pulse carried everything. Every frequency she'd ever heard in this building and frequencies she hadn't — a composite so dense it wasn't sound anymore. It was *presence*. A felt architecture of signal that filled the annex and her body and the space between her bones.

Eleven seconds.

She didn't count them. She couldn't have counted them. Her body locked — hands flat on the floor, back against the wall, jaw clenched, the wet cloth fallen from her face. The pressure found her through the concrete, through the bones of her skull, through the sinuses behind her eyes. The implant flared white in her peripheral vision — the housing cracked, a hairline split along the seam she'd repaired twice with salvaged adhesive. But the blood in her nose wasn't the implant. It was the pressure itself, the air in the annex thickening until something behind her face gave way. Not much. A trickle. Warm on her upper lip.

Then it passed.

The hum settled. Not to what it had been — the E-flat was gone, or buried so deep beneath the new harmonics that it was the same as gone. What remained was fuller. A sound that contained more than a building's resonance should contain.

Nuwa wiped her nose on her sleeve. Looked at the blood. Red-brown against the grey fabric of her work shirt. A small stain. She'd seen worse — the nosebleeds from high-RCI shifts at the plant, the headaches that lasted two days after a bad hum-itch cycle. Her body doing what it did when the infrastructure pushed too hard.

She picked up the cloth. Re-soaked it. Pressed it to her face. Breathed.

The annex was quiet now. Not silent — the hum was still there, the new hum, the full hum — but the sounds from outside had stopped. No footsteps on walkways. No sharp cutoffs. No new harmonics joining the composite. The addition was complete. Whatever the infrastructure had been absorbing, it had finished.

She was still here.

The concrete floor was cold. The masonry walls were solid. The metal handle on the door had cooled. The broken shelving unit was still propped against the frame. The bicycle wheel was still missing from its frame. The dust on the storage shelves was undisturbed.

She counted. Not creds. Not shifts.

Water: two liters remaining. Cloth: wet. Implant: cracked housing, 23% charge, overlay frozen. Body: nosebleed stopping, hands steady, heartbeat decelerating. Fuxi: unknown. Building: standing. World outside the annex: unknown.

She did not open the door.

She did not go to the window.

She sat in the corner of a room that the building's administration had forgotten, in a building that the city's infrastructure had designated as expendable, in a district that the system had decided was worth less than the power it generated — and she pressed the cloth to her face and she breathed and she waited.

She had always been waiting. The transit permit. The implant upgrade. The room with a window above the hum-line. She had organized her entire life around the wait — the careful accumulation of creds toward a threshold that moved faster than she could save. The wait had been her strategy. Her discipline. Her compliance.

The compliance had kept her alive.

Everyone who ran had run through metal corridors. Everyone who tried to reach the exits had reached for handles connected to conduit networks. Everyone who did something — moved, acted, chose — had moved through infrastructure designed to carry exactly what was being carried.

Nuwa had not moved. She had gone to the room with the thickest walls and the fewest connections and she had sat down and she had stayed. Not bravery. Not strategy. Not even decision. The accumulated habit of a woman who had spent twenty-four years routing along masonry walls because the metal ones made her implant itch.

The scar on her inner left wrist caught the faint glow of the cracked implant. Heated wire. Four girls. A pact. *We'll all get out before twenty-five.* She was twenty-four. One got out. One was "relocated." One married a foreman and moved three blocks east.

Nuwa was in a storage annex, breathing through a dishcloth, surrounded by the hum of everyone who hadn't made it to a room without pipes.

She was still.
