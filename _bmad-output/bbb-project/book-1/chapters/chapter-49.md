The pressure doors unsealed on the third day.

Not all at once. Sector by sector, the hydraulic exhalation running in reverse — a deep, slow inhalation as thirty-ton composite panels lifted from slots that had held them for seventy-two hours. Fuxi heard the first one through the converter station's frame: a vibration that traveled through the bolted steel and the concrete floor and the soles of his boots and arrived in his spine as a sensation he did not have a word for. Not relief. Not the end of something. The sound of a mechanism completing a sequence that had been designed to include both the sealing and the unsealing, the killing and the administrative aftermath.

He stood. His legs held. Three days on the converter station floor — concrete, cold through service trousers, the multitool in his fist for so long the handle's contour had pressed its shape into his palm. He had eaten nothing. The station's emergency supplies were a case of water and six protein bars in a cabinet behind the intake booth, rations for a two-person crew during a standard breach lockdown. He and Duan had split them. Duan had eaten his share methodically, one bar per day, water measured in the cap. Fuxi had eaten when Duan put food next to him. He did not remember the taste.

Duan was at the intake console. Same position he'd held since the second day — seated, hands flat on the surface, watching the displays return data in sequences neither of them could decode because the authorization codes running the network had never been theirs. Municipal employees reading corporate override outputs. Passengers watching the cockpit instruments from the cabin.

"Doors are opening," Fuxi said.

Duan looked at him. Sixty-two years old. Eighteen months from retirement. Three days of sealed-bay protocol in a room designed to keep conduit techs alive during the thing the conduits had been built to deliver. His face was the same face it had been every shift — weathered, patient, the face of a man who read historical fiction on a battered frame and checked his gauges every forty minutes. Except the eyes. The eyes had been different since the first pressure door sealed and the residential grid map on Fuxi's frame turned violet block by block.

"I'll stay," Duan said.

Fuxi didn't ask why. He understood why the way he understood pipe tolerances — not through language but through the body's recognition of a limit. Duan had worked the trunk-lines for forty years. Had maintained the infrastructure that had just been used. Had signed the same forms. Had walked past the same flood valves. The converter station was the last room where the work still made sense. Outside was the thing the work had made.

He left the multitool in his pocket. He did not close the private log — it was already closed, the eighth entry complete, the file sitting in a maintenance directory that would never be audited because the system that conducted audits had just performed its terminal function. He walked to the trunk-line corridor exit. The pressure door indicator showed green. Sector 4. Unsealed.

He pushed the door open. The hydraulics assisted — smooth, calibrated, the engineering still perfect. NitroCore built things that worked.

The ascent shaft was dark. Darker than a power outage. The conduit glow was gone — the orange that had been the color of every working day for thirteen years replaced by nothing. Emergency strips along the shaft walls provided a thin, intermittent light that caught the surfaces of things without illuminating them. The rungs of the access ladder. The junction housings at each level. The bolt heads and cable runs and trunk-line casings that he had touched ten thousand times, that his hands knew the way a reader's hands know pages.

The surfaces were violet.

He climbed. One hundred and eighty-seven rungs from the converter station to the first residential sub-level. He had counted them once, years ago, during a training exercise that required timing the ascent. The number had stayed. Numbers stayed. The hum was in the shaft — different from the infrastructure drone he had spent a decade learning to ignore. Fuller. Denser. The sound of a system that had taken something into itself and was still carrying it.

He did not think about what the hum contained. He climbed.

---

The Sump was still.

He emerged from the sub-level access hatch into a street he had walked six days a week for thirteen years. The maintenance route from the Deep Sump ascent shaft to the monitoring bay — left at the junction housing, past the decommissioned water treatment intake, under the walkway between Buildings 3 and 5, three hundred meters to the trunk-line service entrance. He had walked it in the dark. He had walked it in the orange glow. He had walked it in rain that came down through the ventilation gaps between the Sump's ceiling and the Mid-Level floor above.

He had never walked it in violet.

The frost was everywhere. A crystalline residue coating every surface, every conduit housing and junction hatch and pipe bracket and cable run. The infrastructure he had maintained was traced in violet the way a wiring schematic maps a building's hidden circuits — every pathway visible, every connection mapped, the hidden architecture of the Sump's conduit network made visible by the residue of the thing it had carried. He could see trunk-lines through walls. He could see distribution branches running beneath the street surface. The entire network — every line he had monitored, every junction he had serviced, every valve he had tested and signed off on — was outlined in a color that did not exist in the maintenance vocabulary.

The streets were empty.

He walked. Away from the monitoring bay. Away from any point the service had ever directed him. He turned right at the junction housing — away from the maintenance route, toward the residential blocks. Toward Block 14.

The buildings were intact. The infrastructure had not destroyed the architecture — it had used it. The conduit paths ran through walls and floors and ceilings, and the absorption front had followed those paths, and the paths led everywhere, and everywhere was now violet and empty. Apartment windows dark. Walkways between buildings silent. The laundry lines still strung between window brackets, clothes hanging motionless in air that had no current because the ventilation grid was dead.

He passed Block 7. Block 9. Block 11. Each one the same — standing, intact, coated, empty. The buildings looked like they were waiting for their residents to come home. The residents were in the walls.

The hum pressed against his skin. Ambient now. Structural. The sound of tens of thousands of patterns held in a substrate that had been designed to hold nitro throughput and was now holding something else. He could feel it through his boots the way he had always felt the trunk-line harmonic — except the harmonic was different, and the difference was the thing he would not think about, not yet, not while his legs were moving and Block 14 was ahead and the question of whether Nuwa was in the masonry annex or in the walls had not yet been answered.

Block 14. Sixth floor. The apartment where he'd lived since birth. The building where his father had come home smelling of coolant and his mother had coughed through the last year of her life and Nuwa had cut her own hair with a utility blade and talked about transit permits with enough self-deprecation to make it sound like a joke.

The building was violet. The trunk-line junction beneath it — the decommissioned junction that had hummed its low E-flat through every night of his life — was coated so thick the metal surface was invisible. The exterior conduit paths traced violet lines up the walls, branching at each floor, spreading into the distribution network that had fed every apartment.

He did not go to the sixth floor. He went to the annex.

The masonry annex was at the back of the building. Older construction — poured concrete, no metal framing, no conduit paths. The service had flagged it as a maintenance dead zone years ago: no access points, no trunk-line connections, no infrastructure worth monitoring. A storage room that the building's administration had forgotten. He had walked past it ten thousand times.

The door was closed. A shelving unit pressed against it from inside. He could see the edge of it through the gap between the door and the frame — wood, broken, shoved into place by someone who had needed something heavy between herself and the thing that was happening.

He knocked.

The sound was small. Knuckles on wood. The quietest sound in the Sump.

Nothing.

He knocked again. Pressed his hand flat against the door. The wood was cool. Clean. The masonry walls did not conduct what the metal conducted. This room had been the one room in the building that the infrastructure could not reach, and someone had known that, and the knowing was not engineering and was not geology but the accumulated body-knowledge of a woman who had spent twenty-four years living along masonry walls because the metal ones made her implant itch.

"Nuwa."

His voice was raw. Three days without speaking — the maintenance comm channel dead since the first pressure door sealed, Duan and he communicating in gestures and shared silences, the private log typed because the words had run out before the event began. His voice cracked on the second syllable. He said it again.

"Nuwa. It's Fuxi."

He heard movement. The shelving unit scraping against concrete. Slow. Deliberate. The measured pace of someone who had learned that careful was the only speed that kept you alive.

The door opened.

---

She was smaller than he remembered.

She was the same height, the same compressed build, the same narrow shoulders and angular jaw and dark eyes. But something had contracted. The space she occupied in the doorway was less than the space she had occupied in the apartment, at the kitchen counter, at the window where she stood and looked at the ventilation gap between buildings and talked about getting out. She was folded into herself the way she folded into herself when the hum was bad — shoulders forward, chin down, taking up less.

Her hands were stained. The iridescent chemical residue from the textile plant, the permanent mark on her fingertips that he had noticed a thousand times without commenting on. The staining caught the dim light from the emergency strips in the corridor and glowed faintly violet, and for a moment he could not distinguish the staining from the frost, the residue of her work from the residue of the thing that had consumed the work and the workers and the entire district that had contained them.

She looked at him.

He looked at her.

The cloth was still in her hand. Wet. Soaked from a jug he could see on the floor behind her — the emergency supply, rationed, the arithmetic of survival that she had been running since before the power went. Her implant housing was cracked. A hairline split along the seam she'd repaired with salvaged adhesive. A trace of dried blood beneath her nose. The work shirt with the stain on the sleeve.

She was alive.

He stepped forward. She did not step back. He put his arms around her and she put her face against his chest and he held her the way he had held nothing in three days — the multitool in his fist, the console edge under his palms, the cold concrete floor through his trousers. Those were the things he had touched. Metal and stone and the hard surfaces of a room designed to keep him alive while the system he maintained killed everyone above him.

This was different.

She was warm. The warmth came through his service jacket and settled against his sternum and his ribs and the muscles of his arms that ached from three days of gripping and not-moving. Her shoulders fit under his hands. Her hair — uneven, self-cut, the utility blade's imprecision that he had never mentioned and she would have been furious if he had — was against his jaw. She smelled like dust and water and the faint chemical sweetness of the Sump's atmosphere, which was the only atmosphere either of them had ever known.

He did not speak. She did not speak.

The hum was in the corridor behind him. The new hum. The full hum. It pressed against the masonry walls of the annex the way it pressed against everything — patient, ambient, carrying in its structure the weight of the people who had lived in this building and the buildings around it and the blocks and sectors and residential clusters of a district that had been home and was now a substrate.

He held his sister. She held him.

The emergency strips flickered. The dim light caught the violet frost on the corridor floor and the clean concrete of the annex threshold — the line between what the infrastructure had reached and what it had not. His boots were on one side. Her bare feet on the other. The door open between them.

He could feel her breathing. The rhythm of it — faster than resting, slower than panic. The rhythm of someone whose body was recalibrating from crisis to something that was something short of safety — the absence of immediate threat, which was the closest the Sump had ever come to safe.

His hands on her shoulders. Her hands gripping the fabric of his jacket at the back, the fingers closed, the knuckles pressed into his spine. Not letting go. Not the way you hold someone you're happy to see. The way you hold something you were not sure still existed.

The quiet lasted. He did not count it. He did not measure it. For the first time in thirteen years of shift discipline and private logging and methodical documentation of every deviation from baseline, Fuxi did not count.

---

She pulled back first.

Back — just enough to see his face, her hands still on his jacket, his hands still on her shoulders. Her eyes were dry. Not because she wasn't feeling what she was feeling. Because Nuwa's body processed crisis the way his processed data: on delay, in private, later.

"I'm not going back," she said.

Her voice was quiet. The sardonic Nuwa was gone. The humor drained, the sentences contracted to their minimum load-bearing structure. Three words and a negation. The shortest sentence she'd spoken to him since the shimmer at the market that neither of them had acknowledged.

He looked at her. At the cracked implant and the bloodstain and the cloth still in her hand and the chemical staining on her fingertips that glowed faintly in the emergency light.

"I know," he said.

---

The relief shelter opened on the fifth day.

Municipal services — what remained of them, what had survived above the pressure doors and the severed grid and the political reality that the Sump was now an Anchor Zone — established processing stations at the boundary between the Sump and the Mid-Levels. Three stations on the north perimeter. Two on the east. The transit shafts that the pressure doors had sealed were reopened, and the people who had survived — in masonry annexes, in geological pockets, in the path-poor dead zones of a district designed for maximum throughput and therefore maximum lethality — were directed upward through corridors that still carried violet frost on every metal surface.

Fuxi and Nuwa walked together. Through the scoured streets. Past the buildings that stood like monuments to the population they had once held. Past the junction housing where he had reported spikes and signed forms and filed the entries that no one above him had ever read. Past the walkway between Buildings 11 and 14 where the grating was violet and smooth and the laundry still hung and the people who had walked across it were in the railing now.

The relief station was a prefabricated structure at the base of Transit Shaft 7 — the same shaft that Sump workers used every day to ascend to the Mid-Level service entrance, the same corridor Fuxi had walked six days a week, the same infrastructure that had been as familiar as a heartbeat and was now the threshold between a dead district and the city that had killed it.

Processing was efficient. Biometric scan. Temporary ID. Classification: Anchor Zone survivor, Sump-tier, eligible for re-housing allocation. The system that had tracked Nuwa's implant and denied her transit permit and assigned her an evacuation priority of *none* was now offering to put her somewhere new. Different database. Same architecture.

Fuxi took the temporary ID. Watched the processor — a Mid-Level civil servant in a clean uniform who had never been below the transit shaft — stamp the form and direct him to the re-housing queue. A line of people. Survivors. Sump residents who had found the masonry rooms and the stone buildings and the path-poor pockets, who had pressed cloths to their faces and held still and survived by the logic of a district that had taught them where the walls were thick and where they weren't.

He turned to Nuwa.

She was holding her temporary ID. Looking at it. The laminated card with her biometric data and her classification and the institutional vocabulary that reduced what had happened to a form with fields.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Re-housing eligibility: Confirmed. Processing status: Active.*

She looked at the re-housing queue. Forty, fifty people. Waiting. Patient. The Sump had trained them to wait — for shifts, for permits, for credits to accumulate toward thresholds that moved faster than they could save. They waited now the way they had always waited. The system had killed their neighborhood and was offering them a new one, and they were standing in line because standing in line was what the system had taught them to do.

Nuwa did not get in line.

She looked at Fuxi. The same look from the annex doorway — contracted, quiet, the humor gone, the sentences shortened to their minimum. But something else. Something he had not seen before. The look of someone who had subtracted everything that could be subtracted and was standing in what remained.

"I know," he said again. Because he did.

She put the temporary ID in her pocket. Without drama. Without gesture. She put it in her pocket the way she put everything in her pocket — efficiently, without waste, the economy of a woman who had never had enough space for what she carried.

She walked toward the transit shaft. Away from the re-housing queue. Away from the processing station. Toward the crowd — the Mid-Level crowd that moved through the shaft's upper corridors, the flow of city residents going about the business of a city that had just consumed its lowest district and was already reclassifying the loss.

She walked into the crowd. The crowd absorbed her — not the void's absorption, not the lattice's inscription, but the city's indifference. Another small person in a flow of people. Another body below the threshold of notice. The invisibility that had been imposed on her for twenty-four years, chosen now. As the only act available to a woman who had survived a system designed to spend her and was declining, with her feet, to be spent again.

Fuxi watched her go.

She did not look back. He had not expected her to. Looking back was a gesture for people who were leaving something they wanted to remember. Nuwa was leaving something she wanted to forget, and the forgetting began with not-looking, and the not-looking was the first thing she had chosen without calculating its cost.

She disappeared into the Mid-Level crowd. The transit shaft swallowed her the way the transit shaft swallowed everyone — indifferently, efficiently, without record.

He stood at the relief station. The re-housing queue moved forward. A processor called: "Next." The system continued.

Jun's multitool was in his pocket. The weight of it against his thigh — the same weight it had been every shift for thirteen years, the steel warm from his body, the handle worn smooth by two generations of hands that had maintained the infrastructure that had just been used to kill the neighborhood that had made both of them. The tools on his belt — junction wrench, thermal probe, the precision instruments of a Grade 3 trunk-line monitor — were the artifacts of a profession that no longer existed. Not because the conduits had stopped running. Because he had stopped believing that running them was maintenance.

The Kindling woman's voice in his memory. The water-recycling corridor. Grey temples. Scarred hands. *Your file. Our frame. The picture resolves.*

He had the file. Thirteen years of baseline data, eight logged spikes, the acceleration pattern that predicted the event before the event had a classification. He had the body-knowledge — every junction layout, every pressure tolerance, every access route, every maintenance schedule in the Deep Sump network. He had the thing the Kindling needed: not ideology, not anger, not the political framework that turned grief into action. He had the infrastructure. He knew where it was strong and where it was vulnerable. He knew because he had spent his life making it stronger, and the knowledge that had made him a good technician was the same knowledge that made him exactly the asset a resistance movement needed.

He did not get in the re-housing line.

He turned from the transit shaft. Walked back into the Sump. The violet streets. The empty buildings. The hum that carried everything the old hum had been hiding.

Thursday was today.

The Kindling met on Tuesdays and Thursdays. The water-recycling corridor was in Sector 3, Deep Sump, accessible through the maintenance routes he had walked for thirteen years.

The pressure doors were open. He walked.
