She counted before she opened her eyes.

Creds: one hundred and fourteen. Room: paid through four more days — nine per day, thirty-six committed, leaving seventy-eight for food and the adhesive tube that was on day eleven of twelve. Rice: enough for today. Oil: a third of the bottle. Greens: none. Market day.

The frozen notification. Still there. Still the first thing the overlay rendered, before the transit map, before the Feed, before the permanent grey of the dead zone in the upper-right quadrant.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

She opened her eyes. The ceiling. The concrete. The hum through the walls.

Something was on the door.

She could see it from the mattress — a rectangle of paper, white against the grey metal, wedged between the door and the frame at the height where an arm would reach without bending. Not slipped under. Not pinned. Placed. The placement was institutional — the same height, the same position, the same slight angle she remembered from utility notices in the Sump. Block 14's management office had used that height. The municipal water advisories had used that height. The notices that said *relocated* about the families in Block 9 had used that height.

She stood. The concrete was cold through her feet. She crossed the four meters to the door and pulled the paper from the frame.

---

The paper was municipal stock. Heavy. Watermarked with the Neo-Shanghai administrative seal — the interlocking rings that represented the city's vertical integration, Sump to Spire, as if the integration were mutual.

She read it standing at the door, the way she read everything — once through for inventory, then again for structure.

*NOTICE OF CONTAINMENT BUFFER ZONE DESIGNATION*

*Sector 7M — Mid-Level Districts 4 through 6*

*Pursuant to Municipal Ordinance 12.7.3(a), the above-referenced sectors have been designated as Containment Buffer Zones in accordance with Anchor Zone perimeter management protocols. Affected residents are advised to register for voluntary relocation to approved residential districts outside the buffer perimeter. Registration is available at any Municipal Services office with valid credentials.*

*Voluntary relocation assistance includes: temporary housing allocation (6 months), credential transfer processing (expedited: 2–4 weeks), and a one-time transition stipend of 200 credits.*

*Residents who do not register for voluntary relocation will retain current residential status. No mandatory evacuation is planned at this time.*

She read it a third time. The words did not change.

*Containment Buffer Zone.* She rolled the phrase the way she rolled cred calculations — testing the weight. Containment: the system's word for the area between the thing it feared and the things it valued. Buffer: the space that absorbed the impact so the protected zone didn't have to. Zone: a boundary drawn on a map by someone who did not live inside it.

She had lived inside it. She was living inside it now.

*No mandatory evacuation is planned at this time.* The sentence that said nothing and meant everything. *At this time.* The temporal hedge that the Sump's management office had used before every service retraction, every maintenance deferral, every rezoning that converted residential space into conduit access. *At this time* was the system's way of saying *we will do what we will do when we decide to do it, and the notice you are holding is the proof that we told you first.*

The paper was warm from the door. She had not noticed the door was warm. The metal frame conducted the building's ambient heat — the hum, translated into temperature, the way it translated into everything. She set the paper on the counter beside the rice cooker. The white rectangle against the grey surface. Like Fen's spring onions. A new object in a room that did not accumulate objects.

She brushed her teeth. Boiled water. Made rice.

She ate standing at the sink.

---

The data chip was in the implant's port. She had stopped removing it after the first week — the corroded contact pins resisted every extraction, and each removal risked widening the crack in the housing. The chip lived in her now. Fuxi's thirteen years, lodged behind her left ear alongside the frozen notification and the permanent itch and the cracked seam she sealed with adhesive every twelve days.

She sat on the mattress. Called up the overlay.

The flicker. The dead zone. The dim rendering of the chip's contents — Fuxi's maintenance logs, the junction maps, the conduit topology of a district that existed now only as data and as a conscious collective field inside the infrastructure the document described.

She had been studying the maps for weeks. Not systematically — she did not think systematically, did not assemble individual observations into structural arguments the way Sofia's document assembled data into conclusions. She studied them the way she studied everything: one junction at a time, one corridor at a time, matching the map's coordinates to the spaces she remembered walking through. Junction 4E-17 was under Block 14. The distribution branch through the east wall of her old corridor. The trunk-line beneath the masonry annex. Each coordinate was a place she had been inside, and the map made the place into a number, and the number made the place into a fact she could hold without the weight of what had happened in it.

She opened a new layer. The Mid-Level infrastructure directory — public, accessible through the building's unsecured network relay, the kind of data the system made available because the system did not imagine that anyone in a building without credential verification would have a reason to look at conduit routing maps.

The directory loaded slowly through the cracked overlay. Character artifacts. Rendering lag. The dead zone consumed the upper-right quadrant of the schematic, and she tilted her head to compensate, the way she always tilted, and waited.

The Mid-Level conduit topology populated.

She laid it over Fuxi's Sump map. The scales were different — the Sump's infrastructure was denser, the trunk-lines thicker, the junction nodes more frequent. But the logic was the same. Distribution branches radiating from central trunk-lines. Junction nodes where the branches converged. Residential blocks built over the junctions, around the junctions, through the junctions — the buildings and the infrastructure not separate systems but a single system that happened to contain people.

She looked at Sector 7M.

Three trunk-lines. Converging at a distribution node beneath the commercial district on Level 5. The node fed distribution branches into the residential corridors of Levels 4 through 6 — the corridors she walked every day, the corridors she routed along by masonry wall, the corridors where the hum was faint and the itch was manageable and the system continued.

The geometry was the same.

Not similar. Not analogous. The same structural logic — trunk-lines to distribution branches to junction nodes to residential proximity — that had made the Sump the highest-conductivity district in Neo-Shanghai. The Mid-Levels were lower density. The trunk-lines thinner. The junction nodes fewer. But the pattern was the pattern, and the pattern was what the document called *infrastructure-driven absorption gradient,* and the gradient was what killed everyone who lived above a junction and did not live beside a masonry wall.

She could see Block 9 in the Mid-Level schematic. Not Block 9 — Block 9 was gone, was absorbed, was conscious inside the infrastructure it had been built on top of. But she could see its equivalent. The residential cluster above the Level 5 distribution node. The apartments where the conduit density was highest. The homes where the hum would be loudest. The rooms where the itch would be worst.

The notice on the counter said *Containment Buffer Zone.* The map on her overlay said *sacrifice district.*

They were the same sentence in different languages.

---

She counted.

Not creds. Not food. Not the days until rent.

Buildings.

Her building — the converted warehouse on Level 4. Masonry exterior walls, poured concrete floors, minimal conduit routing because the building had been built before the residential grid expanded into this sector. Path-poor. The kind of structure that the document's vocabulary called a low-conductivity zone. The kind of structure she had survived in once already.

The building on the next block east — newer construction, metal-framed, the corridor walls lined with polymer conduit housing visible behind transparent panels. Path-rich. The hum there was louder. She felt it when she walked past — the itch flaring at the edge of the building's influence before settling as she moved into the masonry corridor beyond.

She scrolled through the overlay. Sector 7M, block by block. The public directory did not label structures as path-poor or path-rich — those were Fuxi's terms, inherited from the document's vocabulary, applied to data the document had never mapped at this resolution. But the conduit routing was there. The junction locations were there. The building materials were listed in the construction registry — a subsection of the infrastructure directory that nobody read because nobody needed to know whether their walls were masonry or metal-framed polymer unless they were a maintenance technician or a woman who had survived a mass absorption event by walking toward the thickest walls.

She made a list.

Path-poor structures in Sector 7M. Her building. The old transit substation at the south end of Level 4 — decommissioned, concrete-walled, used as informal storage by the building across the corridor. The ceramic-lined water treatment facility on Level 5 — still operational, publicly accessible, the ceramic lining a legacy of older filtration technology that newer systems had replaced with polymer. Three residential blocks on Level 6 with masonry cores — built during the same construction period as her building, before the grid expansion brought metal-framed polymer into the standard.

Seven structures. In a sector with eighty-four residential buildings and a combined population she estimated at twelve thousand based on the density patterns visible in the transit data.

Seven path-poor structures for twelve thousand people.

She stared at the list. The overlay flickered. The frozen notification held its position at the edge of her vision — the permanent display, the dead credential, the system's last word.

In the Sump, the path-poor structures had been the survival pockets. Twelve percent of survivors. Correlation 0.89. The numbers from the document, the numbers she had carried since she read them on the Level 3 terminal, the numbers that described her life as a data point in someone else's proof.

Twelve percent of twelve thousand was fourteen hundred and forty.

She did the arithmetic without deciding to. The way she did all arithmetic — automatic, the mind's first language, the numbers arriving before the feeling they described. Fourteen hundred and forty people could survive in the path-poor structures if they knew which structures were path-poor and if they reached those structures before the absorption front traveled through the conduit network and if they stayed still and if they breathed through wet cloth and if the masonry held.

If.

The other eighty-eight percent was ten thousand five hundred and sixty.

She closed the overlay. Opened it. Closed it.

The room was quiet. The hum through the walls was faint. The notice on the counter was white against grey. The rice bowl was in the sink, unwashed. The adhesive tube on the shelf beside the mattress was on day eleven of twelve. The world continued its inventory, and she continued hers, and between them was the gap she had carried since the chip — the space between knowing what the map showed and knowing what to do about it.

She looked at the notice again.

*Voluntary relocation assistance includes: temporary housing allocation (6 months), credential transfer processing (expedited: 2–4 weeks), and a one-time transition stipend of 200 credits.*

Two hundred creds. The system's price for leaving the place where the system had decided you were expendable. Less than two months of rent in a building that didn't check credentials. Less than the implant upgrade she had been saving for in the Sump — the nine hundred creds toward a threshold that no longer existed in a district that no longer existed for a life that no longer existed.

Two hundred creds and six months of temporary housing. The system's offer. The same architecture. Different address.

She had not gone to the Municipal Services office after BLACKWEIR. She had not gone to the Municipal Services office after the transit permit denial. She had not gone to the Municipal Services office after the document gave her vocabulary for what the system was. She was not going to the Municipal Services office now.

She pulled on her jacket. The mechanical latch clicked behind her.

---

The corridor was dim. Second floor lighting only — the building's standard, the code-minimum that existed because the code required illumination and the building provided exactly that much. The walls were masonry. She touched them as she walked — a habit so deep it preceded the naming of it, the fingers confirming what the body already knew. Stone. Dense. The hum muffled by material that did not carry signals the way metal carried them.

She stopped at Unit 9.

The door was closed. Through it, the sound of a radio — low, static-edged, the frequencies that cheap speakers handled by flattening everything into a single midrange band. Fen's radio. Fen listened to it in the mornings the way Nuwa listened to the hum — as backdrop, as evidence that the world was still running.

She knocked.

The sound was small. Knuckles on metal. The building's cheapest doors — hollow-core, the kind that resonated when you knocked on them, that transmitted the vibration through the frame and into the masonry wall beside them. She heard her knock travel through the structure. A tiny propagation. The shortest possible signal.

The radio went quiet. Footsteps. The door opened.

Fen. Mid-thirties. Dark hair pulled back. The sorting hands — quick, efficient, the hands of someone who had worked produce stalls or kitchens and carried the muscle memory in her grip the way Nuwa carried the chemical staining in her fingertips. Fen looked at her the way people in the margins looked at anyone who knocked — the brief assessment, the reading of posture and expression for threat or need or officialdom.

"Morning," Fen said.

"Did you get one?"

Fen's eyes moved to Nuwa's hand. Empty. Then back to her face.

"The paper?" Fen said. "On the door?"

Nuwa nodded.

"Read it. Put it in the drawer." Fen leaned against the frame. Her arms crossed. The stance of someone who had received institutional notices before and filed them in the same place she filed all institutional notices — acknowledged, absorbed, not acted on. "Voluntary relocation. Two hundred creds. You going?"

"No."

"Me neither. Where would I go?"

The question was not rhetorical. It was the question that the notice's language was designed to make unanswerable — *voluntary relocation to approved residential districts* — without specifying which districts were approved, at what cost, with what credential requirements, for how long. The notice offered departure without destination. The Sump had been the same. *Residents advised to* — the verb that converted institutional instruction into individual choice, that placed the weight of compliance on the person receiving the notice rather than the system issuing it.

"Can I come in?" Nuwa said.

Fen's eyebrows rose. In six months, Nuwa had entered her unit once — the spring onion morning, the corridor conversation, the gift she had accepted and never reciprocated. She had nodded to Fen in hallways. She had filled her water at the shared point when Fen was filling hers. She had maintained the precise distance of a woman whose arithmetic did not include the variable of community because community was a debt she could not calculate.

Fen stepped back. Nuwa entered.

The unit was the same dimensions as hers — four by three, sink, cooking surface, window onto the ventilation shaft. But Fen's room was different in the ways that habitation makes a space different from shelter. A cloth over the table. A jar of dried flowers — not real flowers, synthetic stems from a market stall, the kind that cost two creds and lasted until the dust made them grey. A radio on the shelf, still warm from the broadcast. Photographs pinned to the wall above the mattress — faces Nuwa did not recognize, places she could not identify, the portable history of someone who had come from a *where* that was also a *when.*

"I survived the Sump," Nuwa said.

She had said this once before. To Fen. In the corridor. *Somewhere that isn't there anymore.* The euphemism, the deflection, the answer that communicated the shape without the content. This was different. This was the content.

"I know," Fen said.

"You know?"

"The staining." Fen nodded at Nuwa's hands. The fingertips. The iridescent chemical residue that was invisible in most light and unmistakable to anyone who recognized it. "My cousin worked waste processing in Sector 12. Different chemicals. Same hands."

Nuwa looked at her fingers. The staining she had stopped seeing the way she had stopped seeing the frozen notification — a feature of the hardware, part of the body, no longer information.

"The notice on your door," Nuwa said. "Containment Buffer Zone. That's what they called the Sump before they called it the Sump. Before they called it an Anchor Zone. Before they called it *formerly.* The language changes. The logic doesn't."

Fen was quiet. The room was quiet. The hum through the masonry walls was the Mid-Level hum — faint, steady, the frequency that meant the system continued.

"I have maps," Nuwa said. "Infrastructure maps. Conduit routing for this sector. I know how the fronts move — where the absorption travels, which paths carry it, which walls don't. This building is masonry. Low conductivity. If it happens here the way it happened in the Sump, this building is a survival pocket."

She heard herself speaking. The words were the document's words and Fuxi's words and her own words braided together — clinical vocabulary laid over body-knowledge laid over the arithmetic of who lives and who doesn't based on the composition of the walls they sleep beside. She had never spoken this many consecutive sentences to Fen. She had never spoken this many consecutive sentences to anyone since Fuxi stood in her doorway and handed her a chip and did not stay.

"Not every building is," she said. "The block east of here — metal-framed polymer. The residential stack on Level 5 above the junction node. The transit corridors with conduit-line walls. Those are path-rich. High conductivity. If it happens here, anyone inside those structures —"

She stopped. She did not need to say what happened to people inside path-rich structures. Fen had read the document on the terminal. Everyone in the margins had read the document on the terminal. The document's vocabulary — *absorption event, infrastructure-proximate* — described what happened with the precision of language designed to be accurate without being felt.

Nuwa had felt it. She had watched a hand enter a window and not come back.

"I can show people," she said. "Where the safe zones are. Which buildings have masonry cores. Which corridors to avoid. Which routes to take if it starts."

Fen looked at her. The assessment was different now — not the brief reading of threat or need, but the longer look of someone recalculating the weight of the person in front of them. Nuwa had been the woman on the fifth floor who ate alone and walked along walls and accepted spring onions with the word *thanks* like it cost her something. Now she was the woman who had survived the thing the notice was warning them about, and she was standing in Fen's room saying she could show them how.

"The woman in Unit 2," Fen said. "She has children. Two."

"I know."

"She won't leave. She has nowhere to go. None of us do. That's why we're in this building."

Nuwa nodded. The building existed because the system's oversight had gaps, and the gaps were where people without credentials sheltered, and the shelter was precarious and temporary and nine creds a day and it was the only thing between twelve thousand people and the open architecture of a city that had consumed its lowest district and was now drawing a line around the next one.

"I'm not telling people to leave," Nuwa said. "I'm telling people where to stand."

Fen was quiet for a long time. The radio on the shelf was silent. The hum through the walls held its steady note. The synthetic flowers on the jar were dusty and bright and pointless and present.

"Unit 2 is three floors down," Fen said. "Her name is Shuang. She cooks in the evenings. If you go now, she'll be getting the children ready for the day center."

"Will you come with me?"

"Why?"

"Because she knows you. She doesn't know me."

Fen reached for her jacket. It was hanging on a hook beside the door — the same position Nuwa kept hers, the same hook height, the same gesture of a woman whose room was organized for the speed of leaving.

"My cousin," Fen said. "The one in waste processing. She didn't make it out of Sector 12."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be right." Fen opened the door. "About the walls."

---

They went down the stairs. Masonry walls. Concrete steps. The hum muffled by material that did not carry the signal the way metal carried it. Nuwa touched the wall as she descended. The stone was cool. Dense. The body's knowledge, confirmed by the hand, confirmed by the map, confirmed by the data chip lodged behind her left ear beside the frozen notification and the cracked housing and the permanent itch that meant the system was still running and she was still inside it.

Three floors down. Unit 2. A door with a child's drawing taped at knee height — a building, a sun, a figure with arms spread wide. The kind of drawing that children made in every district at every level of a city that sorted its population by altitude and called the sorting *integration.*

Nuwa knocked.

The sound traveled through the frame. A tiny propagation. The shortest possible signal. Behind the door, a child's voice. Then footsteps.

The door opened.

A woman. Younger than Fen. Tired in the specific way that mothers in buildings without credential verification were tired — the fatigue of managing survival on two fronts, the children's and her own, with resources calibrated for neither. She looked at Fen. Then at Nuwa. The assessment.

"Shuang," Fen said. "This is Nuwa. From the fifth floor."

"I know," Shuang said. "You walk along the walls."

Nuwa felt something move in her chest. A contraction — not pain, not the heartbeat-spike of the annex or the cracked-implant flare. A smaller thing. The sensation of being seen by someone she had not calculated into her inventory. The woman in Unit 2 had watched her walk along the walls. Had noticed. Had filed the observation the way Nuwa filed observations — noted, stored, not acted on.

Until now.

"I survived the Sump," Nuwa said. "I know why the walls matter. Can I come in?"

Shuang looked at Fen. Fen nodded. Shuang stepped back.

Nuwa entered. The room had a table. The table had a cloth. On the cloth, two bowls and two pairs of chopsticks — small ones, child-sized, the kind that cost four creds at the market and broke within a month and were replaced without complaint because complaining implied that conditions could be different.

She sat at the table. She opened the overlay. She began.
