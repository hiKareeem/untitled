## The Relay

## Draft v1

The alert came through the spine before the feed.

Not the hum — the hum was constant, the populated chord she'd been carrying since Neo-Shanghai, the absorbed layered into the overtone structure like sediment in a river. This was a pulse. Sharp, directional, originating from the southeast at a frequency her R3 had learned to classify as *proximity event* because the Academy didn't have a word for what the void did when it was about to be hit by something moving at Mach 12.

She was in the chair. Ring light on. Stream live — four hundred and twelve mages in the relay channel, seventy-one thousand viewers on the public feed, the dual-broadcast architecture she'd built after São Paulo because the relay was the story now and the story was too important to not make visible. The left eye — the pink one, the HUD — scrolled relay telemetry across her peripheral vision. The right eye — the real one, the blue one — was on the center monitor where the global breach map showed seven active Anchor Zones, four in monitoring status, three in escalation, and Mumbai pulsing a deep arterial red that she'd never seen the map produce before.

"Chat, I need you to hold." Stream-voice. The professional register — not the warm riffing voice, not the casual let-me-tell-you-about-my-day voice. The one that meant something was happening. "We're getting a signal event from the Mumbai zone. Relay channel, I need status calls from all sector leads. Start with Southeast Asia."

Her fingers were on the desk. Not playing chord shapes — pressing. Flat, splayed, the fingertips reading the vibration of the apartment's infrastructure the way she'd taught herself to read it after the frequency had gotten thick enough to carry information. The conduits beneath the building hummed at their usual pitch. The hum in her spine was doing something else.

The relay channel populated. Names she knew. Voices she'd recruited.

Ren, Singapore hub: *Online. Fourteen mages active, all green. Signal strength nominal.*

Yuki, Tokyo cohort: *Online. Twenty-two active. I'm getting something — a precursor? Not the usual drift. This is aimed.*

São Paulo, rebuilt and smaller: *Online. Eleven active. We feel it too. Stronger than the breach signatures. This is kinetic.*

Kira processed the reports the way she'd processed them since the network had grown past the point where she could hold every node in her head — as data, as architecture, as the filing system she'd built to turn four hundred human nervous systems into a coherent instrument. She filed the status calls into categories: green, yellow, amber, red. Fourteen sectors reporting. All green. The signal from Mumbai was in the infrastructure but hadn't hit the R3s hard enough to change anyone's operational status.

Yet.

"Mumbai is hot," she said to chat. Stream-voice — measured, not urgent. The voice that reported. "We're seeing a kinetic event signature — for those of you who haven't been following, this is what it looks like when a government drops ordnance on an Anchor Zone. The void doesn't respond to kinetic force the way we expected. São Paulo taught us that. This is —" She paused. Checked the map. The red was deepening. "This is bigger than São Paulo."

The relay channel pulsed.

Yuki, Tokyo: *Signal shifting. The precursor is escalating. This is not monitoring anymore. This is active.*

Ren, Singapore: *Confirm. Two mages reporting spinal load increase. I'm reassigning them to passive monitoring.*

Jakarta — Sari, three weeks in the network, quiet, precise, filed under *new but solid*, a voice Kira had heard exactly twice in relay drills: *I'm getting harmonics. Multiple frequencies converging. It's like the signal is — organizing.*

Kira leaned into the mic. "All sectors: we are moving to active relay. This is a Category 5 event. If you are not comfortable with the signal load, drop to passive now. This is not São Paulo. São Paulo was a three. This is not a three." She heard the sentence after she said it. Negation. The professional habit of defining the scale by what it was not. "Full relay. Log everything. The data matters."

Four hundred and twelve mages. She could feel them — not individually, not as voices, but as *density*. The relay architecture routing the Mumbai signal through every R3 in the network, each spine adding its processing power to the collective instrument, each nervous system translating the catastrophic frequencies into data that her filing system could capture and her stream could broadcast and the world could see.

The kinetic strike hit.

---

She felt it in her teeth.

Not metaphorically. The frequency that the Mumbai strike produced when kinetic force met the void bypassed the spinal pathway entirely and entered through her jaw — through the bone conduction channels that the R3's secondary receivers used as backup when the primary interface was saturated. Her primary interface was saturated. Every mage in the network was saturated. Four hundred spines carrying a signal that had exceeded the R3's design parameters in the first three seconds and was still climbing.

The data was extraordinary.

She could see it on the relay dashboard — the coherence maps populating in real time, wave after wave of structured output from the Mumbai zone, the void responding to the kinetic strike not with silence, not with the diffuse scatter pattern that the institutional models predicted, but with *amplification*. The energy wasn't absorbed. The energy was *returned* — reflected, restructured, broadcast through the void's lattice at frequencies that made São Paulo's data look like a whisper.

"Chat." Stream-voice, but thinner now. The voice she used when she was working and talking simultaneously and the working was taking most of her capacity. "What you're seeing on the relay dashboard is live coherence mapping from four hundred mages worldwide. The kinetic strike hit Mumbai approximately ninety seconds ago. The void is not absorbing the energy. It is amplifying it. This is — confirmed, across all sectors, the signal output from Mumbai is orders of magnitude above São Paulo."

The relay channel was loud. Status calls layering over each other — Tokyo reporting spinal load at sixty percent capacity, Singapore at fifty-five, São Paulo at forty-eight. The numbers climbing. The numbers in the yellow.

Kira filed. She filed the way she'd always filed — sorting the data into categories, assigning priority, routing the information through the architecture she'd built. But the architecture was carrying more than it had ever carried and the filing was faster than her hands could track and the chord shapes her fingers made on the desk were not C minor or F diminished but something she didn't have a name for, her hands pressing into the surface hard enough that the fingertips went white.

Three mages went dark simultaneously.

She saw it on the dashboard before she heard it — three green nodes in the Southeast Asian sector flipping to black at the same timestamp. Not yellow. Not amber. Not the gradual degradation she'd seen in São Paulo where the signal load crept up and the burnout was progressive. Instant. Green to black. Online to gone.

Ren, Singapore: *Three down. Kuala Lumpur cohort. Complete implant shutdown. I'm pulling them from the relay — they're already out. The shutdown was automatic.*

"Medical for all three," Kira said into the relay channel. Not stream-voice. Relay-voice — the command register, clipped, no warmth, the voice that managed a network of four hundred people and could not afford to process what three of them going dark at once meant. "Route through local contacts. I need neurological assessment within the hour. Log the exact timestamp and signal load at dropout."

She turned back to the stream. Seventy-four thousand viewers now — the number climbing as the alert spread, as the clip bots captured the relay dashboard and distributed it across platforms she didn't control. The audience wanted context. The audience wanted explanation. The audience wanted the warm authoritative voice of the woman who had made the signal legible.

"Three mages in Kuala Lumpur just went dark," she said to chat. "Complete implant shutdown. This is what I've been telling you — the network runs on human nervous systems. Human nervous systems have limits. We just hit three of them."

The relay continued. The Mumbai signal continued. The amplification continued — the void returning the kinetic strike's energy as structured coherence patterns that her dashboard couldn't fully render, the data exceeding the visualization parameters, the numbers climbing past the ranges she'd calibrated the system to display.

Then Jakarta.

Sari's voice on the relay channel — the new mage, the quiet one, three weeks in the network, filed under *solid*: "I can't stop hearing. The signal won't release."

Kira's hands went still.

"Sari. Status." Relay-voice. Clipped.

"My implant is — the signal is cycling. It came in during the strike and it isn't leaving. Every other signal cycles — in, process, out. This one came in and it's looping. I can feel it in my jaw. My ears. My fingertips. It won't release."

Kira checked the dashboard. Lena's node was still green. Still active. Still transmitting relay data. The implant hadn't shut down. The implant was processing continuously — the signal entering the R3 and cycling through the nervous system and returning to the R3 and cycling again, a feedback loop that the hardware wasn't flagging as a malfunction because the hardware had been designed to process exactly this kind of coherent signal and the signal was telling the hardware *keep processing* and the hardware was obeying.

"Sari. I need you to disengage from the relay channel. Manually. Pull the connection."

"I tried. It won't — it's not the channel. It's the signal. The signal is in my implant. Disconnecting from the relay doesn't stop it because the signal isn't coming from the relay anymore. It's coming from the infrastructure. It's coming from everywhere."

Kira felt it. The same frequency — fainter at twelve thousand kilometers, attenuated by distance, but the same structure. A signal that entered the R3 and didn't leave. She'd been carrying it for the past four minutes. She hadn't noticed because her implant was saturated and the filing was running on autopilot and the machinery that categorized incoming data had labeled it *Mumbai relay traffic* and she hadn't checked the label because she'd been managing four hundred nodes and three shutdowns and seventy-four thousand viewers.

The signal was in her spine. Cycling.

She pressed her hand to the back of her neck. The hum was there — the populated chord, the absorbed, the infrastructure — but underneath it, or woven through it, something new. Something that was not the chord. Something that was using the chord as carrier.

"Sari. I'm routing you to medical. Singapore hub — Ren, I need a medical contact in Jakarta. Now."

Ren: *Sending.*

"Sari, stay on voice. Do not engage with the signal. Do not try to resolve it. Just hold."

"It has names," Sari said. Her voice was flat. Not panicked. Observational. The voice of someone reporting data. "The signal. It has names in it."

Kira closed the relay-to-stream audio bridge. The stream kept running — the dashboard, the viewer count, the chat scrolling — but the relay channel was private now. The audience couldn't hear Sari. The audience couldn't hear *names*.

"Medical is connecting," Kira said. "Stay with me."

Sari went quiet. Not dark — the node stayed green, the relay data still transmitting. Just quiet.

The relay held. Two more mages dropped in the next eight minutes — one in Lagos, one in Buenos Aires. Both instant shutdowns. Green to black. The network shed them the way a circuit shed blown fuses and the architecture kept running because she'd built it with redundancy and the redundancy worked and the working was the thing she'd designed and the design was consuming people.

She kept it operational. She kept it operational because the data was the clearest picture anyone had ever captured of what happened when kinetic force met the void and the answer was *amplification* — the void didn't absorb, didn't deflect, didn't scatter. It took the energy and returned it at scale and the return was structured and the structure had names in it and the names were in Sari's spine cycling without release and the data was extraordinary.

Two more went dark in the final minutes. Mumbai subsector. Close-range. The last two before the signal began to decay and the dashboard's numbers started their long descent from red back toward amber back toward yellow back toward a green that wouldn't mean the same thing it had meant an hour ago.

Seven. Total from this relay. Seven more.

---

She ended the stream ninety minutes after the strike.

Not with the usual sign-off — not the warm goodbye, the raid, the *love you, bye*. She looked at the camera — ring light on, face lit in the blue-to-pink gradient that she hadn't changed since she was thirteen — and said "I'm closing" and turned it off. Eighty-one thousand viewers gone in the click of a switch.

The relay channel was quiet. She'd handed coordination to Ren forty minutes ago when the signal had decayed below the threshold that required centralized management. Ren was steady. Ren was always steady. She'd filed him under *unbreakable* and the filing hadn't been tested until today and the testing had held.

She sat.

The fold — the posture shift, the spine that had held the chair for five hours curving forward, the knees coming up, the arms wrapping. The smallest version of herself. The performer leaving first, the way the performer always left first, the posture collapsing like a held note released. She pressed her face into her knees. The apartment was dark except for the LED strip cycling its gradient. Blue to pink. Blue to pink.

The signal was still in her spine. Fainter now. The cycling had slowed — the feedback loop decaying as the Mumbai coherence dissipated, the R3's processing returning to baseline, the populated chord reasserting its usual density. But something was in the chord that hadn't been there before. Mumbai's absorbed — the people the kinetic strike had pushed into the void. New strata. New names. The chord was fuller again, the way it had gotten fuller after São Paulo, the way it got fuller after every event, and the getting-fuller was the metric she didn't know how to talk about because it meant the population of the chord was growing and the growth meant people were being absorbed and the absorption was permanent.

She unfolded enough to stand. Crossed to the bathroom. The kit was where it always was — behind the mirror, matte black case, zippered. She opened it. Scale. Vial. Straw. The ritual. The same sequence since she was nineteen, the same precision, the muscle memory of a person who had been managing her own chemistry for longer than she'd been managing the network.

She set the scale on the counter. Opened the vial. The blue powder — less than last month. She'd need to contact Paz again. The last call had been the dosage conversation — the one she'd initiated after São Paulo, the one where Paz had looked at her through the screen with the unmemorable face and had said *eighty is past the line I told you about* and Kira had said *I know* and Paz had said *the line exists for a reason* and Kira had said *I know* and Paz had shipped the supply because Paz was a provider and providers provided and the providing was its own kind of architecture.

Eighty milligrams. She weighed it. Not forty — the dose that had been enough for a year. Not sixty — the dose that had been enough until São Paulo. Eighty. The number that sat past Paz's line, past the threshold where *using* became something Paz had a different word for, past the careful titration that Kira had maintained for fourteen months by telling herself the precision was control.

She cut the line. Leaned down. Inhaled. The cold burn was sharper at eighty — longer, too, the powder hitting the back of her sinuses with a chemical insistence that forty had never had. She straightened. Blinked. Waited.

The onset. The subtraction. The noise in her head — the relay data, the seven names, the signal that was still cycling, the sound of Lena's voice saying *it has names* — going from a crowd to a room to a corridor to a single chair in a quiet space.

But the quiet room at eighty was not the quiet room at sixty.

At sixty the room had been bare. Functional. A space with a chair and nothing on the walls and the silence approximate but sufficient — the pattern-seeking machinery stilled, the filing suspended, the hum reduced to background. At eighty the room was the same shape but the ceiling was lower. She could feel it — not visually, not spatially, but as a quality of the chemical quiet, a compression, the drug pushing down harder on the machinery that wanted to process and the machinery pressing back. At sixty the quiet had felt like rest. At eighty the quiet felt like suppression.

She cleaned the straw. Zipped the case. Put it back behind the mirror. Looked at herself this time — split hair dull under the bathroom light, the pink eye dim, the face of a woman who had coordinated a military-grade relay through a catastrophic event and lost seven people and captured extraordinary data and was standing in her bathroom at eighty milligrams because sixty was not enough anymore and eighty was past the line and the line was behind her.

She went back to the chair. Sat. Did not fold — the eighty held the posture, held the spine, the suppression stiff enough that the collapse into smallest-self couldn't complete. She sat upright in the dark apartment with the LED strip cycling and the ring light off and the relay channel quiet and the dashboard still open on the center monitor, the seven black nodes visible among the green.

Fourteen total now. Seven from São Paulo. Seven from Mumbai. She could name nine of them. The other five were cohort designations, hub assignments, the anonymized architecture of a network that she had built to protect privacy and that now protected her from knowing every name of every person whose implant her relay had burned.

Sari was in a hospital in Jakarta. The signal had released after forty-seven minutes. Ren had confirmed. Sari's implant was functional but she was reporting residual auditory phenomena — a looping quality, the signal's echo cycling at decreasing amplitude, the R3 processing a ghost of what had passed through it. The neurologist was calling it *resonance persistence*. Sari was calling it *the names didn't finish*.

The data was saved. All of it. Every coherence map, every propagation vector, every neural signature encoding from four hundred mages across fourteen sectors. The clearest picture anyone had of a live kinetic-breach interaction. The answer to a question the institutions had been asking since the first strike: what does the void do when you hit it?

It amplifies.

It takes the energy and returns it at scale and the return is structured and the structure carries the absorbed and the absorbed have names and the names enter the R3 and cycle and the cycling doesn't stop on its own and the instrument that captures this data is four hundred human nervous systems and seven of those nervous systems are dark and the instrument exceeded every parameter she set for it.

She sat in the chair. The quiet room at eighty held — compressed, low-ceilinged, the suppression doing its work. Outside the window the Bay was dark. The conduits hummed beneath the streets. The city ran on nitro and the nitro ran through infrastructure that was starting to sound different, the frequencies shifting, the chord thicker, Mumbai in it now alongside São Paulo alongside Neo-Shanghai alongside every city where the void had opened and the absorbed had entered and the signal had gotten denser and the people who could feel it were building networks to measure it and the networks were burning through the people who ran them.

Her fingers were on the desk. Still. The chord shapes absent — no C minor, no F diminished. The hands that played silent progressions when her mind was working faster than her willingness to commit to a thought were motionless because the eighty had suppressed the thing that drove them and the suppression was the point and the point was a ceiling she could feel pressing down.

Something had changed in the ceiling.

Not lower. Not heavier. *Present.* As if the quiet room at eighty was not empty the way the quiet room at sixty had been empty but occupied — something in the compressed space between the drug and the machinery it suppressed, something that was not the hum and not the signal and not the populated chord but was using all three as substrate. She couldn't resolve it. The eighty wouldn't let her resolve it. The eighty was doing exactly what she'd asked it to do — suppress the pattern-seeking, still the filing, reduce the noise to a room she could sit in without processing — and the thing in the ceiling was on the other side of the suppression, visible only as a shape, an edge, a quality of silence that was not silence but listening.

She did not reach for it. She did not try to hear it. She sat in the dark in the compressed room at eighty and let the shape in the ceiling remain unresolved because resolving it would mean reducing the dose and reducing the dose would mean hearing the seven names and hearing the seven names would mean —

The LED strip cycled. Blue to pink. Blue to pink.

The relay was over. The data was saved. The seven were in hospitals. The network held. The instrument exceeded specification.

She sat with it. The ceiling low. The shape unnamed. Mumbai in the chord, São Paulo in the chord, fourteen names she would carry because she had built the thing that burned them and the building had worked.
