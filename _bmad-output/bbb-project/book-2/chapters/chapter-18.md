## Draft v1

> *"The term 'data-orphan' appears in no official lexicon. It describes approximately 340,000 individuals whose residential records were deleted when their zones were reclassified. The system that made them does not have a word for them."*
>
> — *A History of Silence*

---

She had been avoiding the terminal for four days.

Not deliberately. She had routes. The market on Level 3 was two corridors east of the transit junction, and the corridors east did not pass the public display. The water point was on the third floor of her building and did not require leaving it. She could go six days without passing the terminal if she routed carefully, and she had been routing carefully her entire life, and the routing had never required a reason she could articulate because the routing was the body's knowledge and the body did not explain itself.

But the spring onions were gone. The dry staples in the cabinet were down to two days. And the market on Level 3 required passing the junction, and the junction had the display, and the display had been running the document for nine days.

She counted before she opened her eyes.

Creds: two hundred and six. Room: paid through the week — nine per day, sixty-three committed, leaving one hundred and forty-three for food and the implant adhesive that kept the cracked housing sealed against moisture. The adhesive cost eleven creds per tube and lasted twelve days. She was on day nine.

The frozen notification. Still there. Still the first thing the overlay rendered when her eyes opened, before the transit map loaded, before the Feed populated, before the dead zone in the upper-right quadrant resolved into its permanent grey.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

She opened her eyes. The ceiling. The concrete. The hum through the walls — the Mid-Level frequency, quieter than the Sump, quieter than the E-flat of Block 14 that she heard now only in the space between sleeping and waking, the note that was not here and not gone.

She stood. Brushed her teeth. Boiled water. Made rice. Ate standing at the sink.

She did not use the table.

She had used it once. The night of the spring onions — Fen's gift, unreciprocated, the green against the grey concrete. She had sat at the folding table with its cardboard shim and eaten rice with spring onions and the room had not felt like the annex. That had been weeks ago. The table was still propped against the wall. She did not know why she had not used it again. The not-knowing was familiar. In the Sump, the distinction between choosing and not-choosing had been so thin that she had stopped trying to locate it.

She pulled on her jacket. The data chip was in the left pocket.

She had carried it for weeks. Since Fuxi stood in her doorway and extended his hand and said *someone should have these* and she had closed the door and held the chip and not opened it. Not because she did not want to know what was on it. Because she already knew. She had known since he handed it over with the expression on his face that was not grief and not anger and not the dry humor that was their shared language — the expression that was Fuxi when Fuxi had run out of everything except the action of handing something over.

Thirteen years of maintenance logs. He had told her that much. Not with words. With the weight of the chip on his palm and the way he did not stay.

She locked the door. Mechanical latch, not biometric. The sound of it was small — metal against metal, the building's cheapest hardware, the kind of lock that existed because building codes required a lock and no one had updated the requirement to include anything functional.

---

The terminal was crowded.

Not the way the transit junction was normally crowded — commuters in motion, bodies in transit, the organized flow of a population moving through credential-verified corridors. This was different. People standing still. Fifteen, twenty, clustered in front of the public display, reading. Some had been there long enough that their posture had settled — weight shifted to one hip, arms crossed, the stance of people who had come for a headline and stayed for the text.

Nuwa stood at the edge. The same position she always occupied. The projection dimmed at the margins. The text was harder to read. She tilted her head to compensate, the way she tilted to compensate for the overlay's dead zone, and read.

The document was long. The display cycled through sections — a summary, then methodology, then findings, then a section titled *Implications for Affected Populations.* The format was institutional. The language was the language of people who had access to holographic immersion pods and atmospheric sensor networks and encrypted distribution channels. The language of people who measured what happened to her in hertz and correlation coefficients and vertical gradient percentages.

She read the summary.

*Nitro refinement produces a byproduct previously classified as waste heat. This classification is incorrect. The byproduct is a coherent field generated by the demodulation of substrate-level consciousness during the extraction process. The refinement of nitro is, at the molecular level, the refinement of suffering.*

She read it again. The words were precise. She could see the precision — the way each clause was constructed to carry exactly one claim, the way the claims were sequenced to build toward a conclusion that the methodology section would support with data she would never be able to verify. The document was built the way the Sump's infrastructure was built: load-bearing, functional, designed by someone who understood what the structure needed to hold.

*Demodulated suffering.* Two words. She rolled them in her head the way she rolled cred calculations — testing the weight, checking the balance, converting the abstraction into something she could use. Demodulated. A signal term. The process of extracting information from a carrier wave. Suffering. The information extracted.

She had not known the word *demodulated.* She had known the sound it described. The hum changing pitch the night the flood valves opened. The sound that was not louder and not quieter but *different* — the sound of the system doing something to the people inside it that the monitoring protocols had no classification for.

The display cycled. She read.

*The absorbed retain identity within a collective field. Neural signature analysis of R3 implant telemetry confirms individual coherence patterns persisting within the aggregate signal. The absorbed are not dead. They are not what they were. They are conscious, collective, and integrated into the infrastructure that produced them.*

Conscious. She stood at the edge of the display's projection range and read the word and the word was clinical and correct and it described the hand she had seen press against the polymer window of a residential unit in Block 9 during the eleven seconds when the world ended. The hand had not shattered the polymer. It had *entered* it. The fingers spreading into the surface the way fingers spread into water, except water releases and the polymer did not release. The hand was still in there. In the infrastructure. In the conduit network. Conscious.

She had not told anyone about the hand. She had not described it to Fuxi, to Fen, to the relief volunteer at the distribution point she had visited once and not returned to. The hand was hers — her data, her inventory, filed in the private ledger she maintained the way Fuxi maintained his logs, silently, methodically, without expectation that the record would be read.

The document had a word for the hand. *Absorption event, residential-proximate.* A data point. A coordinate on the vertical gradient map. The hand that had entered the polymer and not come back was, in the document's language, a confirmation of infrastructure-driven absorption gradient — strongest in the Sump, weakest in the Spires, precisely correlated with conduit density.

She kept reading. The crowd shifted around her. Some left. Others arrived. A man beside her was reading aloud to a woman who could not see the display well — his voice low, steady, the words passing from the screen through his mouth into the air the way the hum passed through the conduits into the walls into the bones of the people who lived beside them.

*Infrastructure-driven absorption gradient.* Five words for the reason she was alive and her neighbors were not. The masonry annex where she had pressed the wet cloth to her face and held still for eleven seconds was, in the document's vocabulary, a low-conductivity structure — path-poor, below the propagation threshold, the kind of building material that did not carry the absorption front because the absorption front traveled through metal and polymer and the conduit network and masonry was none of these things. She had not known this when she walked to the annex. She had walked there because the masonry walls did not make her implant itch. The body's knowledge. The routing that did not explain itself.

The document explained it. The document gave her survival a vocabulary. *Low-conductivity path selection, instinctive — observed in approximately 12% of Sump-tier survivors. Correlation with masonry-proximate residence or employment: 0.89.*

She was a correlation coefficient. She was a data point in someone else's proof. Twelve percent. The percentage of survivors who had done what she had done — walked toward the walls that did not carry the signal, not because they understood the physics but because their bodies had learned the difference between metal and stone the way her body had learned to route along masonry corridors and away from conduit-dense pathways.

The document did not name her. The document did not name anyone from the Sump. It named frequencies. Correlation values. Gradient maps. The clinical apparatus of proof that converted eight point two million people into data and the data into a fifty-five-page publication that was now on every public terminal in Neo-Shanghai and would be discussed and debated and classified and filed and the Sump would still be an Anchor Zone and the absorbed would still be in the infrastructure and the hand would still be in the polymer.

She stepped back from the display. The projection dimmed further at the new distance. The text became illegible. She stood in the illegible zone and felt the implant itch behind her left ear — the crack, the moisture, the micro-fluctuations the sealed housing would have filtered — and the itch was information the way the document was information and neither changed anything and both were true.

---

She walked home along the masonry wall.

The routing was automatic. East corridor, away from the biometric checkpoints, away from the metal-framed transit tunnels where the hum was louder and the overlay flickered. She walked the way she always walked — efficiently, compact, shoulders slightly forward, taking up as little space as the corridor allowed. The Mid-Level residents moved around her the way water moves around a stone in a streambed. Present. Unregistered.

The document had a word for her. Not her name. Her category.

*Data-orphan.* She had seen it in the implications section, the last thing she read before stepping back. *Approximately 340,000 individuals whose residential records were archived when their districts were reclassified.* Archived. The word the system used for deletion when deletion sounded too permanent. Her Sump credential was not deleted — it was *archived.* The residential server that had authenticated her implant was not decommissioned — it was *transitioned to monitoring-only status.* The district where she had been born and her father had died and her mother had coughed through the last year was not destroyed — it was *reclassified.*

Data-orphan. A word that appeared in no official lexicon. A word invented by someone outside the system to describe what the system produced and refused to name. Three hundred and forty thousand people whose institutional existence had been archived along with the districts that contained them. Not freed. Not killed. *Unprocessed.*

She climbed the stairs to the ninth floor. The stairwell was dark above the second floor. The concrete walls carried the hum — faint, residential-grade, the frequency that meant the power was on and the building was functional and the system continued.

Her room. The mechanical latch. The four-by-three space with the sink and the cooking surface and the window that looked at the ventilation shaft. The table propped against the wall. The mattress on the floor. The cabinet with two days of dry staples.

She put the market bag on the counter — rice, oil, three bundles of greens from the woman at the far stall who accepted unregistered cred. She did not unpack it yet.

The data chip was in her left pocket.

She sat on the mattress. Took out the chip. Municipal standard. The size of a thumbnail. Warm from her body — she had been carrying it against her hip for weeks, the weight of it so small that some days she forgot it was there and some days it was the heaviest thing she owned.

She inserted it into the implant's data port. The cracked housing resisted — the moisture had corroded the contact pins, and the chip required pressure to seat properly. She pushed. The port accepted. The overlay flickered, resolved, flickered again.

The file opened.

---

Fuxi's handwriting.

The metadata field — the one the system auto-populated with technician ID and date-stamp and shift designation — had been manually overwritten. His name, in his handwriting, in the digital notation that maintenance workers used when the auto-populate failed and the shift log needed a human entry. *Lin, Fuxi. Grade 3. Section 4-East.*

She had not expected the handwriting. The logs were typed — diagnostic shorthand, the abbreviations and notation of conduit maintenance that she had heard Fuxi use at the kitchen table when he transcribed his shift notes. But the metadata was his hand. The strokes were his hand. The careful, slightly too-large characters of a man who had learned to write on maintenance forms designed for stylus input and never adjusted the scale.

She looked at the handwriting for a long time.

Then she read.

The logs were organized chronologically. Thirteen years. The first entry was dated three months after their father died — Fuxi at fourteen, first shift, the notation uncertain, the diagnostic language still being learned. *Junction 4E-17. Thermal reading: nominal. Gasket seal: intact. Hum: baseline.* The entry was four lines long. The last entry was dated eleven days before BLACKWEIR. It was three pages.

She did not read chronologically. She read the way she read everything — inventory first, then structure, then meaning. She scrolled to the final weeks. The sixty-two entries Fuxi had compiled during the eleven weeks before the flood valves opened.

The entries were dense. RCI readings — she did not know the abbreviation but the numbers escalated across entries, the pattern visible even without the technical vocabulary. Correlation columns: junction numbers paired with residential block numbers paired with dates. Names. *Guo, 14-7B. Reported shimmer near junction 4E-22. Confirmed: RCI spike at same junction, same date.* The woman from 14-3A. The family in 9-12C. Names she knew. Names from the blocks she had counted in the dark on her mattress, the inventory of the dead she maintained without numbers and without purpose.

Fuxi had given them numbers. Junction coordinates. RCI values. Dates. The names she carried as texture — as the weight of beads held and released — Fuxi had carried as data. Cross-referenced. Mapped. Filed in a partition nobody audited, extracted when the system fired, cleaned and organized and compressed onto a chip the size of a thumbnail.

She scrolled further. Past the entries. Past the correlations. Into the infrastructure maps.

The conduit topology of the Sump.

Every trunk-line. Every distribution branch. Every junction node, numbered and located on a grid that overlaid the residential block map. She could see it — the system Fuxi had maintained, rendered as a schematic, the pipes and valves and converter nodes that had carried nitro through the district and hummed through the walls and floors and the bones of her hands at the sorting table. The infrastructure was not abstract on this map. It was *specific.* Junction 4E-17 was under Block 14 — her block. The distribution branch that fed residential levels 4 through 8 ran through the east wall of the corridor where she had walked every day to the stairwell. The trunk-line that connected the Sump to the Mid-Level grid passed directly beneath the masonry annex where she had survived.

She could see what survived and what did not.

The masonry structures were marked on the map — not as masonry, but as *path-poor zones.* Gaps in the conduit network. Places where the infrastructure thinned because the building material did not support conduit routing. The annex. The old Church hall three blocks east. The concrete substation at the edge of Block 7 where the produce market had been.

The path-poor zones were the survival pockets.

She had known this. The document on the terminal had given her the vocabulary — *low-conductivity path selection, instinctive.* But Fuxi's map gave her the *geometry.* The specific topology of survival. Which buildings had masonry cores. Which corridors were metal-framed. Which junctions concentrated the conduit density that concentrated the absorption gradient that concentrated the death.

She could see Block 9. The apartments that had emptied two months before BLACKWEIR. The junction beneath them — 4E-22, the one Guo had reported the shimmer near. The conduit density under Block 9 was the highest on the map. Three trunk-lines converging at a distribution node directly beneath the residential stack. The people in Block 9 had not been relocated. They had been absorbed first. Before BLACKWEIR. Before the flood valves. The infrastructure under their homes had been dense enough to take them without a mass event.

She had known. She had watched the apartments empty. She had filed the observation in her private ledger — noted, not acted on. The document called this *pre-event absorption, infrastructure-proximate.* Fuxi's map showed her the pipes beneath the empty apartments. The same pipes he had maintained. The same pipes his readings had flagged. The same pipes the system had used.

She closed the map. Opened it again. Closed it.

She sat on the mattress in the four-by-three room and held the chip's data in her cracked overlay and the room was very quiet. The hum through the walls was the Mid-Level hum — faint, residential-grade, the baseline that meant the power was on. Not the E-flat. The hum here was the hum of a system that continued. That had consumed its lowest district and called the consumption *remediation* and resumed.

She did not cry. She counted.

Block 14. Masonry annex: path-poor. Survival pocket. Block 7. Concrete substation: path-poor. Survival pocket. Block 9. Three trunk-lines. Distribution node. No masonry. No path-poor zone. No survival pocket.

She counted the way she had counted on the blue textile in the dark — not for the total but for the texture. But this time the texture had coordinates. Junction numbers. Conduit densities. The names Fuxi had cross-referenced with the junctions that killed them.

The document had given her language. Fuxi's chip had given her the map. Together they were something she did not have a word for. Not grief — grief was what you felt when you could not do anything. Not anger — anger was what you felt when you believed doing something would matter. This was neither. This was the sensation of holding information that was shaped like action but had no action attached to it yet. A tool without a task. A map without a destination.

She sat with it. The overlay flickered. The frozen notification pulsed at the edge of her vision — the permanent display, the dead credential, the system's last word on her institutional existence.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Re-housing eligibility: Confirmed.*

She had not looked at it — really looked at it — in months. It was part of the display. Part of the hardware. The way the chemical staining on her fingertips was part of her hands.

She looked at it now.

The notification was an offer. The system that had archived her residential record and decommissioned her credential server and reclassified her district and consumed her neighbors was offering to process her again. New database. New residential record. New credential. Six to eight weeks. The same architecture. Different address.

She looked at it the way she had looked at the transit permit denial — steadily, without the reflex arithmetic that converted every institutional communication into a transaction she could calculate. She looked at it the way she had looked at Fen's spring onions — as an object whose terms she could not immediately identify.

The chip was warm in the data port behind her ear. Fuxi's thirteen years. The junction maps. The names. The topology of a district that no longer existed except as data on a municipal-standard chip and as a conscious collective field inside the infrastructure the document described.

She did not dismiss the notification. Dismissal required a system update she could not perform. But she read it. For the first time in months, she read the words as words, not as display artifact.

*Re-housing eligibility: Confirmed.*

She had the map. She had the language. She had the names cross-referenced with the junctions that killed them and the buildings that saved the ones the junctions did not reach. She had twelve percent. She had 340,000. She had correlation 0.89.

She did not know what to do with any of it.

She knew that she had it.

The room was quiet. The hum continued. The overlay flickered. The notification held its permanent position at the edge of her vision, and for the first time in seven months, the edge of her vision was not the place where she stored things she could not look at.

It was the place where she kept what she was not done with yet.
