## Draft v1

The number changed while she wasn't watching.

Twelve million. She'd stopped checking at eight — not because the checking had stopped mattering but because the checking had started feeling like the thing it was: a person sitting in the dark refreshing a counter that measured the circulation of the worst moment of her life. Eight million people had watched her bleed. Now twelve million had. The difference was four million and the difference was nothing because the number had stopped being a number somewhere around two million and had become a fact — atmospheric, ambient, part of the architecture of her life the way the hum was part of the architecture of her spine.

She was sitting in the streaming chair. Not streaming. The ring light was off — had been off for six weeks, the longest she'd gone dark since she started broadcasting at eighteen. The LED strip still ran its blue-to-pink gradient across the ceiling because the LED strip didn't know she'd been grounded and the LED strip didn't care. The apartment was hers — not Academy housing, not a dorm, not a subsidized unit in the mage residential block where they could monitor her sleep patterns and her medication compliance and her streaming schedule. Hers. Paid for with six years of being a face on a platform. The streaming money. The sponsorship money. The appearance fees and the brand deals and the revenue from being Kira Calloway, mage, streamer, two hundred thousand followers who were now closer to four hundred thousand because the clip had done what clips did — it had made her more famous, more valuable, more visible at the exact moment she'd been told to disappear.

The Academy's legal notice sat in her HUD — the third one, the one with *formal proceedings* in bold. She'd read it eleven times. Not because she was considering it. Because the language was the kind of language she'd learned to read the way she read hum data — not for what it said but for what the structure of the saying revealed about the institution behind it.

*Ms. Calloway — Pursuant to Section 22.4 of the Varma Academy Mage Conduct Agreement, you are hereby directed to issue a public corrective statement attributing the acoustic event broadcast on [date] to equipment malfunction consistent with known R3 interface feedback patterns. Failure to comply within 14 days will result in formal proceedings under Section 22.7 (Public Misrepresentation of Academy-Administered Technology)...*

Equipment malfunction. She'd bled from her ears on camera. Eight hundred and forty-seven thousand people had watched it live, and twelve million had watched the clip since, and the Academy's legal team had decided the correct institutional response was to call it a warranty issue.

She hadn't responded. Not to the first notice, not to the second, not to the third. Six months ago the threat of expulsion would have scared her — losing the implant support and the health monitoring and the institutional architecture that had been managing her since she was ten. Six months ago she'd had a pharmacy, a health liaison, a stack of medications that kept the machinery running. She'd had a pill case. She'd had a ritual.

She'd left all of it on a nightstand in a medical bay and walked out. The gesture had lasted eleven days. On the twelfth, the brain's machinery had opinions about the gesture — opinions that didn't negotiate — and she'd found a civilian psychiatrist who wrote the prescriptions without the Academy's routing. Same molecules. Different architecture. The medications came back. The ritual didn't.

---

The fingertips were worse now.

Not the dropout — the absence she'd been filing for months, the two-second gaps where the nerve endings stopped reporting. That was still there, still happening, still filed. This was different. The fingertips *buzzed.* A low persistent vibration in the pads of her index and middle fingers, both hands, as if the R3 had decided to route signal through her extremities instead of keeping it in the spinal column where it belonged. She could feel it when she pressed her fingers to any surface — the desk, the arm of the chair, her own skin. A thrum. Not painful. Present.

She'd asked the mage group about it. Three others reported the same thing. Post-BLACKWEIR onset, bilateral, intermittent-becoming-persistent. No one had a name for it. The Academy health bulletin from eight months ago — the one about "structured auditory phenomena" being "implant resonance feedback" — didn't cover extremity symptoms because extremity symptoms didn't fit the narrative that the implant was the source and the mage was the problem.

She pressed her fingers to the desk. The thrum. She filed it.

The hum was different now. Everyone said that — the mages in the group, the Academy bulletins that kept trying to normalize it, the Feed articles that ran headlines about "post-BLACKWEIR atmospheric shifts" as if *atmosphere* was the word for 8.2 million people. The hum in her spine had been thick before BLACKWEIR. Tight. Aligning. Structured, in the way she'd first noticed in Neo-Shanghai when the chord had clarified into something that felt like it was trying to mean something. Now it was *populated.* That was the only word she had. The chord hadn't gotten louder or denser or more organized — it had gotten *fuller*, as if the signal she'd been carrying since she was ten had acquired additional voices, additional frequencies, additional *presence*, and the R3 was parsing all of it and feeding it into her skeleton and her skeleton was vibrating with it in a way that was not shaking, was not tremor, was something she didn't have clinical language for because the clinical language had been designed by people who didn't carry the signal in their bones.

Not shaking. Vibrating. The difference mattered. Shaking was her — her body responding to overstimulation, to the load the implant was placing on a nervous system that had been running at capacity since before BLACKWEIR. Vibrating was the signal — external, structural, the thing in the hum that had changed when 8.2 million people were absorbed and the void had not gotten quieter.

She didn't say that in the group chat. She said "the hum is different" and "post-BLACKWEIR onset" and "anyone else getting extremity symptoms" because clinical language was safe language and safe language didn't get you flagged under Section 14.3 and Section 14.3 was still technically in effect even though the Academy had expelled her and the Academy couldn't expel her from a regulation and also enforce the regulation on her simultaneously but the Academy was trying because the Academy's legal team was very good at doing two contradictory things at the same time.

---

The Lumina was at sixty now.

She'd been at forty for over a year — the nightly dose, the routine, the shutdown procedure that worked the same way every night: after stream, before bed, scale and vial and straw, the cold burn, the subtraction. The quiet room. Forty milligrams and the pattern-seeking machinery went still and the hum receded to background and she could sleep. Forty had held through the escalation, through the bumps, through the nights when the extra fifteen had crept from once a week to twice to three times.

Forty didn't hold anymore.

The quiet room at forty had a crack in it now — the hum leaked through. Not the full chord, not the bone-deep populated signal that sat in her spine during the day, but enough to keep the machinery running, enough to keep the patterns assembling themselves against the chemical barrier that was supposed to make them stop. She'd gone to fifty. Fifty held for two weeks. Then the crack came back. Sixty held. For now.

She didn't think about what came after sixty stopped holding. She was very good at not thinking about the things that came next.

Her contact was a woman named Paz. Not her real name — probably not her real name, possibly not her real anything. Kira had found her through channels that existed because mages needed things the Academy didn't provide and the Academy's health infrastructure was designed to manage mages, not help them, and the distinction between management and help was the distinction between Section 22.4 and an answer. Paz dealt pharmaceutical-grade Lumina — the same compound the Academy prescribed for implant-related neurological regulation, the same molecule, the same purity, obtained through supply chains that ran parallel to the institutional ones because institutional supply chains required institutional compliance and institutional compliance required signing things Kira would not sign.

She met Paz at the usual place — a tea shop in the Mission, the kind with real leaves and no surveillance and a back booth where two people could sit and conduct a transaction that looked, from any distance, like two friends having a conversation they'd had before.

Paz was already seated. Dark hair, mid-thirties, the kind of face that was designed to be unmemorable — not ugly, not beautiful, not anything that would catch in a witness's description. She had a cup in front of her. She didn't look up when Kira sat down.

"Sixty's not going to last," Paz said.

"It's lasting."

"You went from forty to sixty in four months. That's not lasting. That's a curve." Paz stirred her tea. She didn't look at Kira the way the pharmacist hadn't looked at her — but for a different reason. The pharmacist didn't look because looking wasn't part of the transaction. Paz didn't look because looking was an exposure risk and Paz managed exposure the way Kira managed the performer: professionally, habitually, as a condition of the work.

"I need another thirty days at sixty."

"Thirty days at sixty is eighteen hundred milligrams. You were at twelve hundred four months ago." Paz set the spoon down. "The compound's clean. I'm not worried about purity. I'm worried about your ceiling."

"My ceiling is my problem."

"Your ceiling is my problem when you show up in five weeks asking for seventy-five and I have to decide whether the person sitting in front of me is making a choice or riding a curve." Paz reached into the bag beside her — canvas, unremarkable, the kind anyone carried — and set a case on the table between them. Matte black. Small. The same size as the kit behind Kira's bathroom mirror. "Thirty days. Sixty. Same price. Cash."

Kira paid. Cash, because cash didn't route through the Academy's financial monitoring — one of the sixteen systems she'd disconnected from when she walked out of the medical bay. The disconnection was freedom and the freedom was also the absence of a net and she didn't think about nets because thinking about nets was thinking about falling and she wasn't falling. She was adjusting. There was a difference.

Paz slid the case across the table. "You know where I am."

"Yeah."

"If you hit seventy-five, we're having a different conversation."

"I'm at sixty."

"You're at sixty *now.*" Paz picked up her tea. The meeting was over.

Kira took the case. Put it in her bag. Left the tea shop and walked into the Mission district and the Bay Area hum sat in her spine the way it always sat — populated, structured, the chord that had been evolving since she was ten now carrying frequencies she could name and frequencies she couldn't — and the R3 parsed it all and the sixty was going to hold and the quiet room was going to hold and she was in control.

She was almost certain she was in control.

---

The mage group had forty-seven members now.

She didn't know when it had crossed from a private channel into a network. Somewhere between the four messages in her inbox — Ren, Lena, Yuki, the anonymous voice — and the forty-seven nodes spread across nine cities, the thing she'd reluctantly joined had become the thing she was running. Not officially. Not with a title or a mandate or an organizational structure that the Academy could point to and cite under Section 14.3. Just — she was the one who organized the data. She was the one who asked the questions. She was the one who had said "yeah, I felt it" when she could have said nothing and the saying had made her the center of something she hadn't asked to be the center of.

She opened the telemetry thread on her HUD. The pink scroll of her left eye, the interface that never turned off, the data running down the edge of her vision the way it ran down the edge of her life — persistent, ambient, impossible to ignore.

The thread was organized by city, by timestamp, by magnitude. Her system. She'd built it over weeks — not because she had training in data architecture but because she was a streamer and streamers organized information for audiences and the fact that this audience was forty-seven mages instead of four hundred thousand viewers didn't change the fundamental skill. You categorized. You labeled. You made the raw data legible for people who needed to see the pattern but couldn't assemble it alone.

**São Paulo — Tomás (verified, R3, active deployment)**
*Micro-incursion 2176.03.12, 14:22 UTC. Duration: 8 seconds. Structured auditory phenomena: confirmed. Pattern: repeating sequence, 3 intervals, ~45 second spacing. Magnitude: low. Geographic correlation: conduit junction 7-West.*

**Lagos — Lena (verified, R3, active deployment)**
*Atmospheric anomaly 2176.03.15, 09:41 UTC. Duration: 12 seconds. SAP: confirmed. Pattern: repeating sequence, 4 intervals, ~90 second spacing. Magnitude: moderate. Geographic correlation: FAS trunk-line 3, northern district.*

**Mumbai — Asha (verified, R2, research post)**
*Micro-incursion 2176.03.18, 22:07 UTC. Duration: 6 seconds. SAP: confirmed. Pattern: repeating sequence, 3 intervals, ~45 second spacing. Magnitude: low. Geographic correlation: NitroCore infrastructure hub, eastern corridor.*

She scrolled through the thread. Fourteen reports in the last month. Three continents. The same structure — repeating sequences, consistent intervals, geographic correlation with conduit infrastructure. She'd started logging them the way she logged everything: filing, cataloguing, the mechanism of control applied to data that was increasingly uncontrollable. But the filing had turned into something else. The filing had turned into a dataset.

She didn't know what the dataset meant. She knew what it showed: that the structured auditory phenomena the Academy called "implant resonance feedback" were happening globally, independently, with consistent internal architecture, correlated with conduit infrastructure, and increasing in frequency post-BLACKWEIR. She knew that the intervals matched — forty-five seconds for micro-incursions, ninety seconds for larger events, the ratio consistent across every report. She knew that the geographic distribution mapped onto high-density nitro infrastructure. She knew that the Academy's Section 14.3 said she shouldn't know any of this because "peer discussion of unverified phenomena" was "discouraged."

She copied the latest reports into the master log. Anonymized — names stripped, locations generalized, identifiers removed. Not because she didn't trust the network but because the network trusted her and trust meant protecting the data and the people who generated it and the distinction between data and people was a distinction the Academy had never made and Kira did.

The log was getting large. Fourteen months of structured auditory phenomena reports from mages across nine cities. Timestamps, magnitudes, patterns, geographic correlations. The kind of dataset a scientist would build a correlation model from. The kind of dataset that could validate atmospheric predictions, prove that the thing in the hum was not feedback and not noise and not the mages' problem but a phenomenon with structure and consistency and implications that the Academy's health bulletins were designed to obscure.

She didn't know any VEC researchers. She didn't need to. The data existed. Someone would eventually need it.

---

The messages about Nephthys had started three weeks ago.

Not from the mage group — from the broader network, the followers, the audience she wasn't broadcasting to but that was still watching. The four hundred thousand who'd seen the clip and followed the account and were now watching a dark channel, waiting for the streamer who'd bled on camera to come back and explain what had happened. They sent messages. Thousands of them. She didn't read them all — couldn't. But the ones about Nephthys she read, because the ones about Nephthys were different.

*@KiraCalloway — have you heard about the woman in Neo-Shanghai? The one who was excommunicated? She says she can hear them. The absorbed. She has a whole congregation now. You should interview her.*

*Kira — I know you're not streaming but when you come back you HAVE to talk to Nephthys. She's saying the same things the mages are saying but from the Church side. The Void Witnesses are growing. It's connected.*

*Hey Kira long time viewer. There's a movement in Neo-Shanghai — Nephthys, the excommunicated priestess. She runs what she calls a counter-liturgy. Listening sessions. People go silent and then describe what they hear in the hum. Some of them are describing the same structured patterns your mage network is logging. Different language, same phenomenon. She's the Church's version of you.*

She'd read that last one three times. *The Church's version of you.* The sentence sat in her mind the way the hum sat in her spine — not comfortable, not painful, just present. She didn't know Nephthys. She knew the name — everyone knew the name after the Silencing, after the Feed had turned a disgraced priestess into a symbol of institutional overreach. But knowing the name and knowing the person were different things and Kira had learned the difference between public knowledge and private truth the way she'd learned everything else: by being famous and discovering that fame was the distance between what people knew about you and what was actually happening.

More messages. From mages, from followers, from people she didn't recognize.

*You need to go to Neo-Shanghai. Nephthys is doing the same work from the theological side. The listening sessions are generating data you don't have — experiential reports from non-mages hearing structured signal through Church infrastructure. Resonance chapels are conduit amplifiers. Her people are your people's civilian equivalent.*

*@KiraCalloway interview Nephthys. Please. The two of you together would break the Feed.*

Then one from Ren. Ren Matsuda, who documented everything, who'd been in the network since the beginning, who didn't send messages unless the message mattered.

*Kira — I've been corresponding with someone in Nephthys's circle. The acoustic profiles they're describing in the listening sessions match our telemetry. Same intervals. Same structure. Different vocabulary — they say "the chorus" where we say "structured auditory phenomena." But the data is the same. If you could talk to her — even off-record — the correlation would be significant.*

She sat with it.

Neo-Shanghai. Where the hum had been loudest, where the chord had clarified into something she'd called *words*, where she'd sorted pills on a hotel bed and typed a question into a group chat that she couldn't take back. Except the pill-sorting ritual had stayed behind with the Academy version of her — the thirty-day holder, the click of capsules into compartments, the ceremony that made unfamiliar rooms feel manageable. She swallowed them in a bathroom now. It was not the same. And she was expelled. The formal proceedings under Section 22.7 had gone through while she wasn't answering their notices, and expulsion meant no Academy travel authorization, no institutional cover, no mage health liaison in whatever city she visited. Going to Neo-Shanghai as an expelled mage meant going without infrastructure. Going to interview Nephthys on stream meant streaming, which meant violating the grounding, which meant the Academy's legal team would have documentation of her defying terms she'd already been expelled for defying — circular, absurd, and exactly the kind of institutional logic that worked because institutions didn't need logic. They needed compliance. The absence of compliance was the presence of consequence, and the consequence was always the same: you disappeared from the system and the system continued without you.

She could stream clandestinely. Encrypted mirrors, anonymized routing, the same architecture that had kept the 11-second clip circulating after the Academy tried to scrub it. The clip had twelve million views because the infrastructure of suppression was also the infrastructure of distribution — the takedown notices had turned the clip into contraband, and contraband was more valuable than content, and the encrypted mirrors had done what encrypted mirrors do.

She could go to Neo-Shanghai. She could find Nephthys. She could do the interview without the Academy's knowledge, on a platform they couldn't reach, through channels they couldn't monitor.

She could do all of that. She was also twenty years old and taking sixty milligrams of an illicit compound every night to keep the quiet room from cracking and her fingertips buzzed and her spine carried a signal populated by 8.2 million voices and the pattern-seeking machinery was running at a speed the Lumina could barely contain and she was sitting in a streaming chair in an apartment she paid for with the money from being a person she wasn't sure she was anymore.

She opened the reply field. Typed to Ren:

*Working on it. Need to figure out logistics. If I go to Neo-Shanghai it has to be off-grid — Academy legal is still active on my case and streaming violates the grounding terms. Can your contact in Nephthys's circle facilitate a quiet introduction?*

Sent.

She closed the message queue. Opened the telemetry log. Scrolled through the reports — São Paulo, Lagos, Mumbai, Kyoto, Bay Area, Accra, São Paulo again, Mumbai again — the pattern repeating across cities, across continents, the structure consistent, the Academy's explanation insufficient, the data growing faster than her ability to organize it and her ability to organize it was the only thing keeping it legible and legibility was the thing she did because she was a streamer and streamers made the invisible visible even when the invisible was a sound in the bones of every mage on earth that the institution responsible for those bones had decided was not happening.

The ring light was off. The LED strip cycled its gradient. The apartment was dark except for the pink glow of her left eye — the interface that never turned off, the HUD scrolling data she hadn't asked for about a world that was asking things of her she hadn't agreed to receive.

She touched the back of her neck. The implant housing. The bone. The hum — populated, structured, the chord carrying its post-BLACKWEIR density through a frequency space that was no longer the Bay Area baseline she'd grown up with but something new, something fuller, something that contained more voices than it had six months ago and the voices were not getting quieter and the Academy was not getting smarter and the network was not getting smaller and she was not getting better at pretending any of this was resonance feedback.

Twelve million views. Forty-seven mages. Nine cities. Sixty milligrams. One priestess in Neo-Shanghai who heard the same signal through different infrastructure and called it the chorus instead of structured auditory phenomena and whose followers were describing the same intervals Kira's network was logging and whose existence meant that the thing in the hum was not a mage problem or a Church problem or a science problem but a problem with no institutional container and the only people trying to hold it were a twenty-year-old expelled streamer and an excommunicated priestess and neither of them had a framework for what they were holding.

She wasn't streaming. She wasn't performing. She wasn't the face on the platform or the brand in the sponsorship or the mage in the Academy's promotional material. She was an antenna. She was a node. She was the place where the data converged because the data needed somewhere to converge and she was visible and she was stubborn and she had said *yeah, I felt it* when she could have said nothing and the saying had made her the center of something she hadn't chosen to be the center of and the not-choosing was the thing about her that the Academy had never understood — that she hadn't asked for the implant at ten or the platform at fourteen or the scream at twenty but she had all three and the having was not the same as the wanting and the difference between having and wanting was the space her entire life occupied.

---

She opened the Lumina kit. Scale, vial, straw. Sixty milligrams. The cold burn. The subtraction.

The quiet room.

She cleaned the straw. Zipped the case. Sat in the streaming chair with her knees pulled up and the ring light off and the LED strip cycling and the quiet room holding the way it held — not silence, not the absence of the hum, but the recession of it, the signal dropping below the threshold where the pattern-seeking machinery could reach, the populated chord becoming background, the background becoming the space where she could exist without assembling, without filing, without being an antenna for ten hours until the sixty wore off and the hum came back and she did it again.

She was going to go to Neo-Shanghai. She was going to find Nephthys. She was going to do the thing the messages were asking her to do because the messages were right and the data was right and the correlation between the telemetry and the listening sessions was right and someone needed to make the connection visible and she was the most visible person in the network and visibility was what she'd been trained for since she was fourteen even if the training had been for a different kind and the difference between performing and witnessing was the difference between curating and telling the truth and she was a witness now whether she wanted to be or not.

Not tonight. Tonight the sixty held and the quiet room was quiet and the ring light was off and the LED strip cycled and the twelve million kept climbing on encrypted mirrors she couldn't see from a platform she wasn't using in a life she was still trying to figure out how to live without the architecture that had held it together since she was ten.

She closed her eyes. The hum held. Quieter now. Populated. Waiting.
