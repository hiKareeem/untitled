She had been in the organizing building since before dawn.

Third floor. The room Shuang had cleared for her after the second week of door-knocking — not a residence, a workspace. A table. Two chairs. The overlay projector she had rigged from a discarded public terminal, mounted on a shelf bracket with adhesive and wire, throwing the topology map onto the wall in low resolution. Path-poor zones in blue. Path-rich in red. The building they were in: blue. The block east: red. The transit corridors: red. The concrete substation on Level 2 where she had redirected eleven families: blue.

Three families were scheduled to see the map today. The first was due at mid-morning — a couple from the seventh floor who had received the relocation notice and done nothing with it because the notice offered departure without destination, and departure without destination was not an offer but an instruction to disappear.

She stepped back from the projector and ran the inventory.

Creds: fifty-one. Room: paid through tomorrow — nine per day, nine committed, leaving forty-two for food and the adhesive tube she had replaced three days ago. Rice: enough for today and tomorrow if she split the portion. Greens: Shuang had given her half a bunch of water spinach last night, wrapped in a damp cloth on the shelf above the sink in her room one floor up — the cloth she used for the greens and nothing else, because she had learned in a different building in a different district that a wet cloth had more than one use and the body remembered them all.

The frozen notification sat at the edge of her vision where it always sat.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

The hum through the walls was the Mid-Level frequency — steady, residential-grade, the note that meant the system continued.

The hum changed.

Not louder. She knew louder — the low E-flat of Block 14 that had lived in her sternum for twenty-three years, the baseline that was home. This was not louder. The frequency did not increase. The pressure did not build the way it had built in the annex, the wet thickening she had breathed through a cloth and survived.

The hum *organized.*

She felt it in the implant first. The cracked housing behind her left ear went hot — not the slow warmth of high-RCI proximity, the gradual climb she had learned to track as an early warning. This was immediate. The disc flared against her skin and the overlay scrambled. The frozen notification — the permanent fixture, the dead credential, the first and last thing she saw every day — fragmented. The letters separated. Rearranged. For two seconds the overlay displayed something that was not *Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic* but was not anything else she could read. Characters she did not recognize. Then the notification reassembled, but wrong — the spacing different, the font weight heavier, as if the display had been briefly occupied by something the implant was not designed to receive and had shoved the notification aside to make room.

The chip. Fuxi's data chip, lodged in the port beside the implant since she had stopped removing it months ago. The chip's data populated her overlay without prompting — junction coordinates, conduit densities, the topology of a district that existed now only as numbers on a dead technician's maintenance logs. The data flickered. She had not opened the chip files. They were opening themselves.

Then the walls carried it.

---

Sound through masonry was something she understood. Masonry dampened. Masonry absorbed. The hum through these walls had always been muffled — present but reduced, the system's frequency arriving through material that resisted it. She had chosen this building because of that resistance. She had told people to come here because the walls between them and the infrastructure were thick and dense and slow.

The walls were carrying something now that they had never carried before.

Not the hum. Not the infrastructure frequency. A sound that traveled through every surface — the walls, the floor, the table, the shelf bracket where the overlay projector sat. The projector began to flicker. The topology map on the wall strobed. She could see the path-poor zones pulsing in their blue and the path-rich zones flaring red and the entire schematic vibrating as if the infrastructure it depicted was resonating with the sound that was coming through.

Then the names began.

The first one arrived before she understood what she was hearing. A voice through the wall — not transmitted by the building's speaker system, not carried by the conduit network the way municipal alerts were carried. Through the material itself. Through the concrete and the masonry and the rebar and the plaster and the paint. A woman's voice. Accented Shanghainese. The cadence of someone who had spoken quickly her entire life and was now speaking slowly, deliberately, as if each word cost something that could not be replenished.

*"Huang Meiling. Block 14. Unit 7-C. Still here."*

Nuwa stopped.

Her hands were on the table. The overlay was flickering. Fuxi's junction coordinates were scrolling across her vision without her input. The room was vibrating. The children — she could hear Shuang's children on the floor above, their voices high and fast the way children's voices went when something frightened them and they did not yet know whether to cry or be quiet.

Mrs. Huang. Unit 7-C. The apartment below Nuwa and Fuxi's in Block 14. The woman who had cooked garlic greens at the same hour every evening, the smell rising through the ventilation gap between floors. The woman whose kitchen sounds Nuwa had used as a clock — garlic greens at six, the radio at seven, silence by nine. Mrs. Huang had been absorbed during BLACKWEIR. Nuwa had heard the silence from the apartment below when the garlic greens stopped and did not resume.

The second name came through the east wall.

*"Yuto. I touched the light. I am still here."*

A child's voice. Yuto — the boy from Block 11 who had touched the shimmer near the junction node two weeks before BLACKWEIR. His mother had carried him to the clinic. The clinic had filed a report. The report had been archived when the district was reclassified. Nuwa had seen him once in the corridor, his hand bandaged, his eyes wide with something that was not pain but was not curiosity either. He had been seven.

She sat down. The chair was hard. The table was vibrating beneath her palms.

The third name. The fourth. The fifth.

*"Lin Wei. Converter Station B. Foreman. I reported what I saw. Still here."*

Lin Wei. Fuxi's foreman. The man who had filed the official reports that contradicted everything Fuxi's private logs recorded. Who had written *nominal* beside readings that were not nominal. Who had signed the inspection forms that said the junction pressure was within tolerance when the junction pressure was climbing toward the threshold that would, thirteen months later, contribute to the resonance cascade that consumed eight point two million people.

Lin Wei. Still here.

The names kept coming.

---

She counted.

She did not decide to count. The counting began the way it began every morning — automatic, reflexive, the body's first language running before the mind could intervene. Nine hundred creds toward the upgrade, three hundred short. Four hundred and twelve creds. Two hundred and six. One hundred and fourteen. Fifty-one. The numbers had changed but the counting had not. The counting was the mechanism that held the inventory, and the inventory was the thing that kept the world the size she could carry.

Mrs. Huang. Yuto. Lin Wei. Her mother's neighbor — the woman whose name she heard through the south wall and recognized not by the name itself but by the way the voice said *Lǎo jiē* the same way her mother's neighbor had said it, the vowels shaped by a mouth that had grown up in the eastern blocks where the dialect held sounds the western blocks had lost.

Four.

She counted the way she had counted water in the annex. Not for the total. For the texture. Each name a point in the inventory — identified, located, filed. Block 14. Block 11. Converter Station B. Block 19 East.

The overlay was useless. The frozen notification had fragmented again and was cycling through displays she had never seen — characters, coordinates, fragments of what might have been municipal records or might have been something else entirely. The cracked housing was running hot enough that she could feel it through the skin behind her ear, the hairline split in the disc glowing faintly in her peripheral vision. She was receiving the signal through damage. The intact implants in the building — Shuang's, Fen's, the children's — would be receiving it differently. Cleaner. More complete. She was hearing it the way she heard everything through this implant: partial, distorted, compensated for by tilting her head and adjusting the angle the way she adjusted for the dead zone in the upper-right quadrant of the overlay.

She tilted her head. The names sharpened.

Seven. Twelve. Nineteen.

Each one she recognized arrived with its coordinates — not spoken, not transmitted, but *known.* She knew where Mrs. Huang had lived the way she knew where the water point was on the third floor of her building. Body-knowledge. The kind that did not require a map because the body was the map. And now Fuxi's chip was supplying the other coordinates — the junction numbers, the conduit densities, the infrastructure topology that had determined who was absorbed and who was not. The chip data and the names were overlapping in her cracked overlay. Mrs. Huang's voice and Junction 4E-17. Yuto's voice and the shimmer node near Block 11. Lin Wei and Converter Station B and the thirteen years of readings that Fuxi had logged and the system had erased.

The map and the person. In the same frame.

Twenty-three. Thirty-one. Thirty-six.

She was standing now. She did not remember standing. The chair was behind her. The table was vibrating. The topology map on the wall was strobing so fast the blue and red zones had merged into a purple wash that looked like the color she remembered from the annex window — the color of the air when the hum became the thing that ate people.

Shuang appeared in the doorway. Two children behind her, gripping her legs. Shuang's face was the face of a woman who was hearing something she could not process and had come to the only person in the building who might know what it was.

"What is it?" Shuang said.

Nuwa did not answer immediately. The thirty-seventh name was coming through the floor. A man's voice. Sump dialect. The address he gave was Block 7 — the concrete substation, the path-poor zone she had marked blue on the map. He had not survived. The substation had been path-poor but he had not been inside it when it mattered.

"Names," Nuwa said.

"Whose?"

"People I knew."

Shuang looked at the strobing map on the wall. At the overlay projector flickering on its shelf bracket. At Nuwa's hands on the table — the chemical staining on the fingertips, the hands of a woman who had touched infrastructure that was now speaking.

"Is it happening again?" Shuang asked. The question that every person in a containment buffer zone carried. The question that Nuwa had heard in every doorway she had knocked on. The question that meant: *Am I going to die the way they died.*

"No," Nuwa said. "Listen. Nobody is being taken. They are — " She stopped. She did not have a word for what was happening. The document had given her vocabulary for what nitro was, what the absorbed were, what the infrastructure did. It had not given her vocabulary for the absorbed speaking back. Nobody had published that paper. Nobody had that data.

She had data. She had forty-one names and counting.

"They are reporting," she said. The word was not right. But it was the closest. Reporting: to give an account. To present data. To state for the record. The absorbed were stating for the record. Through the infrastructure that had consumed them, through the conduit network that had carried the suffering that had created them, through the walls and the floors and the masonry and the metal — they were stating their names and their locations and the fact of their continued presence.

Still here.

Forty-four. Forty-five. Forty-six.

Forty-seven.

---

The sound stopped.

Not gradually. Not the way the hum had subsided after BLACKWEIR — the slow retreat, the frequency dropping, the pressure easing like water draining from a space. This stopped the way a voice stops when a person finishes speaking. Completed. The signal ended because the transmission was over.

The overlay stabilized. The frozen notification reassembled — *Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.* — and sat in its permanent position at the edge of her vision as if nothing had happened to it. The chip data retracted to its dormant state. The topology map on the wall settled into its steady low-resolution projection. Blue and red. Path-poor and path-rich. The geography of survival, static again.

The implant was cooling. The hairline split in the housing had stopped glowing. Her skin behind her ear was tender the way it was tender after a high-RCI event — the heat leaving a residual sensitivity that would last for hours, a reminder that the hardware was damaged and the damage was the thing she received the world through.

Forty-seven.

She sat in the chair. The table had stopped vibrating. Shuang was still in the doorway, the children still behind her, the building quiet the way buildings were quiet after something had passed through them — not silent, but holding the shape of the sound that was no longer there.

Forty-seven names she recognized. Out of 8.2 million absorbed. She recognized forty-seven because she had lived in the Sump for twenty-three years and the Sump had eight point two million people and she had known, by name and location, fewer than two hundred. Of those, forty-seven had spoken. The rest — the millions, the districts, the blocks — had spoken too, she assumed. Other people in other buildings were sitting at other tables counting the names they recognized. Or they were not. The Mid-Levels had not been the Sump. The people here had not been there.

She was the one who had been there.

The arithmetic ran. Forty-seven out of approximately two hundred known. Twenty-three point five percent of her personal inventory. The sample was not random. The sample was biased toward Block 14, toward the eastern blocks, toward the people whose routes had overlapped with hers. The forty-seven were not representative. They were specific.

They were enough.

She reached behind her ear. The chip was warm. Fuxi's thirteen years of maintenance logs, his junction maps, his private readings that contradicted every official report his foreman had signed. The chip held the infrastructure. The forty-seven names held the people. Together they were what the document had described as data and what she had known as the sound of garlic greens through a ventilation gap and a child's bandaged hand in a corridor.

She opened the overlay. Not the topology map. Not the junction data. The network channel she had been watching for four months.

Kira's network. The testimony chain. Mages across thirty cities describing what they felt through their implants — signal structures, coherence patterns, the biological experience of receiving something the system had not designed them to receive. She had read every entry. She had never posted. The network was for mages, for researchers, for people with credentials and institutional vocabularies and the authority to testify. She was a data-orphan with a cracked implant and a dead man's maintenance chip and the chemical staining on her fingers that marked her as someone who had worked in a district that no longer existed.

She opened the message field.

The cursor blinked. The overlay rendered it in the dead zone's margin — the upper-right quadrant where the display degraded, where she had to tilt her head to read. She tilted her head.

She typed.

*My name is Nuwa Lin. I was in the Neo-Shanghai Sump during BLACKWEIR. I survived. I have infrastructure data from a former conduit maintenance technician — thirteen years of private logs, junction maps, conduit density readings that contradict the official record. I have been relocating people from the containment buffer zones using this data. I know the topology. I know where the fronts move. I know who was there. I counted.*

She read it once. The way she read everything — once for inventory, then again for structure.

She read it again.

The words were hers. Not the document's clinical vocabulary. Not Fuxi's technical shorthand. Not the institutional language of the notices that had been posted on her door at the height where an arm would reach without bending. These words were hers in the way that the counting was hers — the first language, the one that ran before the mind could intervene.

Shuang was still in the doorway.

"Who are you writing to?" Shuang asked.

"Someone who collects what people know."

Shuang looked at the message on the overlay — she could not read it from the doorway, the overlay was angled for Nuwa's cracked display, tilted into the dead zone where only Nuwa's compensating posture could resolve the text. But she could see that it was there. That Nuwa was writing to someone. That the woman who had knocked on doors and shown them where to stand was now telling someone outside this building that she existed.

Nuwa sent the message.

The overlay confirmed transmission. A small notification — green, functional, the standard network-receipt confirmation. It sat beside the frozen credential notice. The dead system and the live one, side by side in the same damaged display.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*
*Message delivered. Recipient: Mage Testimony Network (public channel).*

She closed the overlay. The room was quiet. The topology map glowed on the wall — blue zones, red zones, the geography she had learned from a dead man's chip and the body's memory of which walls carried death and which walls did not.

Forty-seven. She had counted forty-seven.

It was not enough. It was never enough. Forty-seven out of eight point two million was a number so small it did not register as a fraction — it registered as a margin of error, a rounding artifact, the kind of number that systems deleted because it fell below the threshold of significance.

But she had counted them. She had heard their names and she had counted them and she had filed them in the inventory the way she filed everything — noted, located, held. The system had not recorded them. The system had archived their residential data and decommissioned their credential servers and reclassified their district and called the reclassification *remediation.* The system did not count them because the system was not designed to count what it consumed.

She was designed for nothing. She had survived by being small enough that the system did not notice her. She had survived by compliance, by routing along masonry walls, by breathing through a wet cloth, by not going to the window to look for Fuxi. She had survived by every mechanism the system had taught her and the system had not meant for her to survive at all.

She counted anyway.

Forty-seven names. The inventory was open. She was not done.
