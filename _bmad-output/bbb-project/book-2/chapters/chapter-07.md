---

The sixty-third session of the Accords Disciplinary Tribunal convened at 10:00 on a Tuesday.

Zeyad knew because his lawyer had told him, and his lawyer had told him because the Secretariat's scheduling system no longer sent notifications to his terminal. Six months of suspension had not removed his name from the building's visitor registry — the registry processed credentials, not status, and his credentials had been revoked but not deleted, the institutional distinction between *inactive* and *nonexistent* preserving his name in a database he could not access for purposes he could not authorize. He arrived through the east entrance. Visitors' protocol. The biometric panel that had read his diplomatic credentials for six years now read the temporary access badge the Secretariat issued to respondents in active proceedings — a grey card, unlaminated, bearing his name in the same institutional typeface as his original credentials but without the UGC emblem, the signatories' seal, or the holographic security thread that authenticated the holder as a person the institution recognized as its own.

He walked the Hall of Nations.

The Jordanian flag was still flickering. Third row, western wall. The emitter he had reported nine months ago — three maintenance requests, two follow-up annotations in the margin of his compliance review notes, one formal query to Building Services that had been rerouted to Grounds and Protocol and from there into the same procedural sediment where all unfunded maintenance requests settled. The glitch was worse. The lower third of the holographic standard shimmered in and out of projection every four seconds, the green triangle and white star dissolving into static, reconstituting, dissolving. The flag of a country whose diplomatic tradition he carried in his bones, represented in this building by a degrading emitter nobody had been authorized to fix.

He touched the portrait. Khalil. The eastern alcove, between Committee Chamber 3 and the glass case holding the original signed copy of the Accords. His hand found the frame — the same frame, the same lower edge, the same gesture that had opened every working day for six years — and rested for a fraction of a second and withdrew. The portrait showed a man shaking hands at the signing of a document whose Article 7 had produced a committee vote to monitor, whose Article 12, Section 8 had produced Zeyad's prosecution, and whose Article 12, Section 11 guaranteed his right to a defense constructed from the materials the prosecution chose to submit while prohibiting the introduction of the materials that proved the prosecution was protecting a genocide.

He continued toward Tribunal Chamber 1.

The pen was in his breast pocket. It had returned to his hand three weeks after the hearing where silence had been his final institutional act. Not because the conviction had returned but because the hand needed to write. Legal notes, now. Annotations in the margins of his own prosecution documents, the same analytical habit redirected at the machinery processing him. The leather notebook was new — the original had been photographed, published, and entered into evidence as Prosecution Exhibit 1. He had purchased the replacement at a stationery shop on the Rue du Rhône, three blocks from the building where his great-grandfather had purchased the pen in 2039. The shop still existed. The Accords still existed. The diplomat who had tried to enforce them did not, institutionally speaking, exist anymore.

---

Tribunal Chamber 1 was not Committee Chamber 3.

It was rectangular — designed for proceedings, not deliberation. The architecture communicated hierarchy rather than equality: a raised panel at the front for the three-member tribunal, a respondent's table facing the panel, a prosecution desk to the left, observer seating behind a low partition. The holographic display showed the session's docket — a single item, as it had been for sixty-two previous sessions: **Al-Fahim, Z. — Article 12, Section 8 — Unauthorized Disclosure of Classified Materials.**

The tribunal panel had not changed. Dr. Adeyemi, presiding — the Nigerian diplomat Zeyad had known for twenty years, whose children's names he still remembered from reception conversations, who had once said over coffee in the commissary that the UGC's purpose was to ensure that power answered to process, and who was now presiding over the sixty-third session of a process that answered to power. Delegate Ormond, signatory representative — the Australian trade liaison whose vote Zeyad had counted as reliable in seven consecutive compliance reviews and who now sat on a raised panel assessing the damage Zeyad's disclosure had caused to the signatory framework. Professor Vassiliev, legal scholar — the academic authority whose presence made the proceedings appear substantive rather than administrative.

His lawyer — Dr. Amara Diallo, retained through the Diplomatic Personnel Legal Fund because Zeyad's personal resources did not extend to specialized treaty-law defense — sat beside him. She had told him not to speak today. She had told him not to speak at most of the previous sessions. The tribunal's procedural architecture rewarded silence from the respondent and volubility from the prosecution, because the prosecution's statements became part of the record and the record was what the tribunal would cite when it issued its findings.

The prosecution presented.

"The Secretariat submits for the record Exhibit 217 — communication logs recovered from the respondent's institutional terminal, demonstrating that the unauthorized disclosure was transmitted through the UGC's own public-facing communication infrastructure, in direct violation of Classification Protocol 4, Annex B, which restricts the use of institutional communication systems for the distribution of materials classified under security protocol."

Exhibit 217. Two hundred and seventeen pieces of evidence documenting the disclosure. Not the genocide. The disclosure.

Zeyad wrote in the margin of his legal pad: *217 exhibits. Zero on BLACKWEIR.*

Dr. Diallo touched his arm. He stopped writing.

The NitroCore legal representative was present — not in the chamber but through the same secure-link terminal configuration that had occupied a seat in Committee Chamber 3 during the emergency session six months ago. A different face. The same expression: composed, calibrated, the professional certainty of a legal officer attending proceedings whose outcome the structural architecture of the room had determined before a single word was spoken. NitroCore was not a party to the prosecution. NitroCore was an "interested institutional observer" — a status the tribunal had granted over Dr. Diallo's objection in the fourteenth session, citing the corporation's "legitimate interest in the protection of proprietary materials referenced in the unauthorized disclosure."

The proprietary materials were evidence of premeditated infrastructure sacrifice. The tribunal called them proprietary materials. The language determined the frame. The frame determined the verdict. The verdict was being constructed exhibit by exhibit, session by session, in a rectangular room where the holographic flags did not flicker because the tribunal chamber had its own projection system, separately funded, properly maintained.

Zeyad sat still. The practiced composure — the same composure he had maintained through thirty-four years of compliance reviews, committee votes, emergency clauses, and the specific silence of a dead communication channel — held. It held the way it had always held: as architecture, not expression. The face was correct. The posture was correct. The hands were folded on the table in the position Dr. Diallo had suggested during their second session because folded hands communicated neither aggression nor submission, and the tribunal's assessment of the respondent's demeanor was, per Article 12, Section 14, a factor in sentencing.

The session lasted ninety-four minutes. Three new exhibits entered the record. Two procedural motions were tabled. One date was set for the next session — Thursday, 10:00 — the institutional calendar accommodating his prosecution with the same scheduling efficiency it had once accommodated his compliance reviews.

---

Dr. Diallo walked beside him in the administrative corridor. The corridor had not changed — the same institutional carpet, the same overhead lighting, the same distance between the tribunal chamber and the east entrance that Zeyad had measured in thousands of walks over six years but now measured differently, the way a man measures the distance between the building where he is being prosecuted and the door through which he is permitted to leave.

"There is a complication," she said.

She carried a datapad — encrypted, her own, not issued by the tribunal. She did not show it to him in the corridor. She steered them instead to a consultation alcove off the administrative wing — a small room reserved for legal conferences, windowless, acoustically dampened, the institutional architecture of private speech.

"A transmission arrived this morning through a relay I am not going to name in any filing," she said. "Addressed to you. Routed through intermediaries. Partially encrypted — my technical consultant says three of seven encryption layers have resolved, the remaining four are on a time-release protocol."

Mirelle.

He did not say the name. Dr. Diallo had not said the name. The back-channel — the encrypted diplomatic relay Zeyad had used to communicate with Mirelle Dubois, the relay he had written *I did not log this exchange* about in a notebook that was now Prosecution Exhibit 1 — had been public knowledge since his disclosure. But the relay's architecture was not his. It was Black Babel's. And Black Babel did not stop routing because a diplomat was prosecuted.

"What does it contain?"

"The decrypted layers show NitroCore infrastructure documentation. Routing tables. Procurement authorization chains." She paused. The pause was not hesitation — it was the calibrated silence of a lawyer choosing her next words for their evidentiary weight. "My consultant recognized the formatting. It is consistent with the materials referenced in your disclosure but not included in it. You published what you had. This is what you did not have."

His hand moved toward his breast pocket. The pen. He stopped it.

"The legal position is straightforward," Dr. Diallo said. "Anything you access is discoverable. If you open this transmission, it enters the evidentiary landscape. The tribunal can compel its production. The prosecution will argue it demonstrates ongoing contact with unauthorized information networks — which, under the current charges, constitutes a continuing violation rather than a historical one." She held his gaze. "The difference is significant. Historical violations carry censure and credential revocation. Continuing violations carry referral to signatory criminal courts."

Criminal courts. The Accords had been drafted to prevent resource wars. Their enforcement mechanism was being used to threaten imprisonment for a diplomat who had published evidence of a resource genocide.

"My advice," she said, "is to not open it."

The consultation alcove was quiet. The hum was present — muted, the same low-frequency vibration through the building's foundation that Zeyad had felt every day for six years and that he felt now through different shoes, from a different chair, in a room he had never had reason to enter when he was the institution's servant rather than its defendant.

"Who else received transmissions?" he asked.

"I don't know. The relay architecture does not disclose recipient lists. The time-release protocol is consistent with a dead-hand distribution — automated, scheduled, triggered by the originator's failure to send a cancellation signal within a defined window."

The originator's failure to send a cancellation signal. The institutional language for: Mirelle was dead. The dead-hand had activated because the hand was dead. The packages had been decrypting on schedule for six months, routing through Black Babel's architecture, finding their intended recipients through whatever network the journalist had built before the infrastructure she was investigating consumed her.

"She prepared this before BLACKWEIR," Zeyad said.

"The metadata is consistent with that interpretation."

He thought of Sofia Reyes — the name he had seen in the Feed during his prosecution, the atmospheric researcher whose simultaneous disclosure had been met with the same *methodology concerns* that had met his. She would have received her own transmissions. Different content. Different relay routes. The evidence distributed across recipients who did not know each other, assembled from the work of a woman who had understood — better than Zeyad, better than anyone who operated within institutions — that the evidence would survive only if no single point of failure could suppress it.

"Thank you, Dr. Diallo," he said. "I will consider your advice."

He did not tell her he had already decided.

---

The temporary housing was three blocks from the UGC headquarters — a diplomatic residence maintained by the Secretariat for personnel in transitional status, which was the institutional euphemism for personnel the institution had not yet finished processing. The room was adequate. A desk, a terminal, a window showing the lake and the low skyline of New Geneva and the building where Khalil's portrait hung in the eastern alcove and where a photograph in a silver frame sat on a desk in a small office in the administrative wing, second floor, east corridor, behind a door whose biometric panel no longer recognized his hand.

He sat at the desk. The pen was beside the new notebook. The temporary access badge was beside the pen — the grey card he removed from his pocket each evening and placed on the desk the way he had once placed his diplomatic credentials, the ritual of arrival persisting after the credential had been replaced by its institutional negative.

He opened the terminal. The Black Babel relay was there — not on the institutional network, which monitored his communications under the terms of his suspension, but on the encrypted layer that existed beneath institutional visibility because existing within it meant existing within the prosecution's evidentiary pipeline.

The transmission.

Three of seven layers decrypted. The resolved content rendered on his screen in formatting he recognized — not from his back-channel exchanges with Mirelle, which had been text-based, compressed, operationally terse, but from the NitroCore procurement documents he had obtained through his own institutional data requests before the cease-and-desist. The same formatting. The same document architecture. The same institutional typography that NitroCore used for internal routing authorizations — the documents that determined which infrastructure investments were approved, which conduit modifications were authorized, which maintenance schedules were altered, which populations were within the operational perimeter of systems whose throughput requirements exceeded their safety margins.

He read.

The decrypted layers contained routing authorizations for infrastructure modifications in the Neo-Shanghai Sump conduit network — modifications dated eighteen months before BLACKWEIR. Eighteen months before 8.2 million people were absorbed into the infrastructure that these documents authorized modifications to. The routing tables showed throughput capacity increases — not maintenance, not repair, not the routine infrastructure work that appeared in the quarterly compliance reports Zeyad had reviewed for six years, but *capacity expansion*. The conduit network's throughput ceiling had been raised. Systematically. Section by section. Starting from the Confluence — the junction point beneath the Resonance District where six first-generation trunk lines converged — and radiating outward through the Sump's secondary and tertiary conduit branches.

The modifications were consistent with the flood valve evidence Mirelle had documented in her field investigation — the photographs he had published alongside his own classified material, the evidence that the valves were not emergency equipment but amplifiers. The routing authorizations showed that the amplification infrastructure had been approved, funded, and installed through NitroCore's standard procurement pipeline — the same pipeline that produced quarterly reports to the UGC's Accords Compliance Review, the same reports Zeyad had read in Committee Chamber 3 for thirty-four years, the same reports that had never mentioned throughput capacity expansion because the reporting framework did not require disclosure of modifications classified as *operational optimization*.

Operational optimization. The institutional language for building the machine that would kill them, with the paperwork filed correctly.

He opened the notebook. The pen was in his hand — he did not remember reaching for it, the gesture as unconscious as touching the portrait frame, the hand finding the instrument because the hand had spent thirty-four years reaching for it when information required processing and the processing required ink.

He wrote in the margin of the first routing authorization: *Pre-positioned. 18 months. Procurement chain: Spire-level authorization required for capacity modifications exceeding baseline + 15%. Who signed?*

The question formed the way his questions always formed — not as accusation but as procedural anomaly, the gap between what the document contained and what the institutional framework should have required. Someone had authorized these modifications. The authorization chain would show who. The remaining four encryption layers presumably contained the chain, because Mirelle had understood — from outside, from below, from the Sump corridors where the infrastructure hummed at the frequency of a civilization consuming itself — that the evidence was not in the data. The evidence was in the signatures.

He wrote three more questions. The handwriting was steady. The pen moved with the same precision it had always moved with — small, controlled, the lettering of a man who had spent his career writing notes that would be filed and cross-referenced and cited in committee proceedings. These notes would not be filed. They would not be cross-referenced. If the tribunal discovered them, they would become exhibits in his prosecution — evidence of continuing engagement with unauthorized information networks, his refusal to comply with the institutional process that was processing him, his persistent conviction that the record mattered even when the record's only function was to document his destruction.

The pen wrote anyway. The hand could not stop. The institutional habit survived the institution's betrayal the way the hum survived BLACKWEIR — persistent, structural, embedded in the architecture of the man the way the frequency was embedded in the conduit infrastructure. Four questions in the margin. The notebook was new. The questions were old — the same questions he had been asking since a delegate in Committee Chamber 3 had said *contained* and the word had lodged in his institutional vocabulary like a stone in a mechanism designed for glass. They had multiplied. Forty-seven in the old notebook. They had been published. They had produced prosecution instead of answers.

He closed the terminal. The screen went dark. The routing authorizations remained in the relay's encrypted cache — accessible, discoverable, evidence of a crime committed by the institution prosecuting him for documenting it.

The lake was visible through the window. New Geneva's low skyline. The UGC headquarters, forty-two floors, the tallest structure in a city that had chosen not to build upward. Somewhere in that building, in the small office on the second floor of the administrative wing, the photograph in the silver frame sat on a desk he could not reach — Khalil's face, the pen still wet, the expression of a man who had understood that the document he was signing was imperfect and that the alternative to signing was worse. One hundred and thirty-one years later, the document's emergency clause had produced a vote to monitor, the transparency provision had produced a prosecution, and the respondent rights guarantee had produced a defense that could not use the evidence that proved the defense was necessary.

The remaining encryption layers would resolve. The authorization chain would emerge. The evidence would assemble itself — not because the institution demanded it but because a dead journalist had built a distribution system that continued to function after the system that killed her continued to function, and both systems were running, and both systems were producing output, and one of them was producing justice and the other was producing a tribunal, and the tribunal had a chamber and a docket and 217 exhibits and a properly maintained holographic projection system, and the justice had a dead woman's encrypted relay and a man with a pen writing questions in the margins of documents that would be used against him.

He picked up the pen. He opened the notebook to the next page.

He began to write.
