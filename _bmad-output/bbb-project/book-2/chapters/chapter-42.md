The Jordanian flag was worse.

Ten months. The holographic emitter in the northeastern corner of the Hall of Nations had been failing for ten months — the display cycling through render artifacts that made the flag look like it was dissolving from the bottom up, the green losing coherence first, then the white, the black holding longest because the black was the absence of signal rather than the presence of it. Ten months since the first session after BLACKWEIR, when Zeyad had noticed the flicker from the delegate's position he no longer occupied, and thought: *someone should file a maintenance request.* No one had filed a maintenance request. The building that housed the institution responsible for governing the most consequential energy source in human history could not repair a holographic emitter. The flag dissolved. The flag reconstituted. The flag dissolved again.

He watched it from the observation gallery.

The gallery ran along the upper perimeter of the Hall of Nations — a narrow balcony with fixed seating, originally designed for press and diplomatic observers, now occupied by the specific category of persons who had business with the UGC but no credentials to conduct it on the floor. Zeyad sat in the third row, eastern section, where the angle gave him a view of the Jordanian flag and the full arc of the session chamber below. The grey unlaminated access pass was clipped to his breast pocket — the same pass he had been issued ten months ago, the tribunal credential that granted access to the building but not the floor, that permitted observation but not participation, that allowed him to sit in the gallery and watch the institution process its emergency session the way a patient watches surgery through glass.

The pass had no expiration date. This was not an administrative oversight. The pass had no expiration date because the tribunal proceedings had no projected conclusion date, because the institution's response to his case was the same as its response to every systemic failure it could not categorize — to hold the anomaly in procedural suspension until the anomaly resolved itself or the system developed a framework for it. Ten months. The pass was wearing at the edges. The lamination that was not lamination — the grey stock, uncoated, the cheapest credential the UGC printing office produced — had softened from ten months of breast pockets and jacket linings and the specific friction of being carried by a man who had carried diplomatic credentials for thirty-four years and now carried this.

Below, the emergency session continued. The chamber was full — one hundred and twelve delegations, the holographic flags of one hundred and twelve member states arrayed in their standard positions, the amber institutional lighting that made every face look like it was cast in the same material. The session had been convened fourteen hours ago in response to the synchronization data — the same data that had produced emergency communications across every institutional channel, that had produced the specific quality of controlled urgency that Zeyad recognized from thirty-four years of watching institutions prepare for things they could not prevent. The agenda had seventeen items. They were on item four. The pace was institutional.

His pen was in his breast pocket. The notebook — the black hardcover purchased at the Rue du Rhône stationer ten months ago, now three-quarters filled with margin notes and procedural observations and the specific shorthand of a man who documented everything because documentation was the only act the institution still permitted him — was in his jacket's inner pocket. He did not take it out. The session below was procedural. The emergency was being processed. There was nothing to document that the official transcript would not already contain.

The hum was there. Through the building's foundation — the deep infrastructure, the conduit systems that ran beneath the Hall of Nations and powered the holographic displays and the translation systems and the climate controls and the institutional machinery that converted international governance into a temperature-regulated, simultaneously translated, holographically flagged performance of collective authority. The New Geneva hum. He had felt it for six years — since his first posting, since the first time he had walked the corridors of the Secretariat building and felt the vibration through the marble floors and thought: *the building hums.* The building had always hummed. The hum had always been institutional furniture — ambient, ignorable, the sound of infrastructure doing its work.

It did not sound the same.

He had noticed it the moment he entered the building this morning — the quality of the vibration through the lobby's marble, through the elevator's steel frame, through the gallery's composite flooring. The same frequency. The same register. But the texture was different. Finer. More organized. As if the signal that had always been infrastructure noise was being tuned by something that was not the infrastructure. In Lagos the hum had been wide and shallow — the distributed frequency range of a city that had chosen density over concentration. In the breach zone the hum had risen through his body — soles, ankles, shins, chest — with the deliberate quality of something that was not attacking but arriving. The hum had been in his chest when the news of Mumbai came through. The hum had been in his chest when the FAS containment protocol held and the Lagos Anchor Zone formed and 2.3 million people died six thousand kilometers away because a different institution had chosen a different response.

New Geneva's hum was in the foundation. Where it had always been. But Zeyad's body had been to Lagos, and the body remembered what the hum could become, and the foundation hum carried that memory the way a familiar room carries the memory of what happened in it last.

He sat in the gallery and waited for item five.

The hum changed at 14:37 UTC.

Not gradually. Not the way the Lagos hum had risen — the incremental ascent, soles to ankles to shins, the body tracking the escalation in anatomical stages. The New Geneva hum shifted in one movement. The foundation frequency — the ambient, ignorable, institutional vibration — reorganized. The building's systems carried it. Every system. The translation feeds, the holographic displays, the climate controls, the elevator shafts, the lighting arrays, the fire suppression network, the emergency communication channels. Every conduit junction in the Hall of Nations transmitted simultaneously.

The session below stopped.

One hundred and twelve delegations. The procedural grinding of item four — a motion to extend the emergency session's mandate by seventy-two hours, the institutional act of giving itself permission to continue meeting — halted mid-vote. The chamber's speaker system, designed to amplify the voices of delegates and translators, amplified something else. The holographic displays flickered — not the Jordanian flag's familiar dissolution but a systemic interrupt, every flag cycling through the same render artifact simultaneously, as if the display system's processing architecture had been repurposed.

Zeyad felt it in the gallery's flooring. The composite surface transmitted the building's frequency into his feet — not rising, not the Lagos ascent, but present in a way it had not been present thirty seconds ago. The hum was no longer institutional furniture. The hum was using the institution's infrastructure the way a voice uses a microphone — not generating the signal but amplifying what was already there.

Names.

They came through the building's speaker system — every speaker, every channel, the translation feeds carrying them in the frequencies that the translation system's architecture was designed to process as human speech. The system processed them as human speech because they were human speech. Individual voices. Hundreds. Thousands. Speaking through the institutional infrastructure that had been built to amplify the voices of delegates debating tariff schedules and containment protocols and the equitable governance of nitro-derived energy.

The names of the absorbed, spoken through the Hall of Nations.

Zeyad sat in the gallery and heard them. The institutional machinery that had suspended his credentials and filed his tribunal and processed his case through seventeen procedural stages without reaching a determination was now transmitting the voices of the dead through its own speaker system. The irony was not poetic. The irony was architectural. The building was doing what the building was designed to do — carrying voices from the floor to the gallery, from the chamber to the observers, from the institution to the record. The building did not differentiate between a delegate's motion on item four and the names of 8.2 million absorbed persons. The building amplified what it received.

He listened.

The names arrived with the quality he had heard described in the Lagos situation reports — not repetition but development, each name carrying a cadence that shifted, an emphasis that moved, a structure that was not recorded playback but present-tense speech. The absorbed were speaking. The absorbed were identifying themselves through the infrastructure of the institution that had failed to prevent their absorption. The names were not an accusation. The names were not a message. The names were a roll call — each voice reporting its own existence with the specificity of a person answering to their designation.

And then, among the names, among the fragments of sentences that accompanied them — operational contexts, locations, the last coherent thought before the boundary dissolved:

Mirelle.

Not a name he recognized from the general inventory. The voice he had heard in conference rooms and secure briefings and encrypted back-channel communications for four years. The voice that had called him *Ambassador* with the specific inflection that meant she was about to ask a question he could not answer through official channels. The voice that had stopped transmitting during BLACKWEIR — mid-sentence, mid-report, the last transmission cutting to static at the Lagos Bureau of the Associated Press.

Mirelle Dubois. In it. Present tense.

She was not saying her name. She was dictating.

*"Infrastructure pre-positioned. Absorption corridor confirmed, Sector Seven through Sector Twelve. Conduit density consistent with NitroCore internal documentation — the pre-positioning is deliberate, the corridor geometry matches the deployment schedule filed under—"*

The sentence she had never finished. The report she had been filing when the absorption wave reached the Lagos Bureau. The dead-hand transmission that had arrived on his secure terminal eight months ago — the NitroCore infrastructure documentation, the evidence of deliberate pre-positioning, the proof that the absorption corridors were not natural phenomena but engineered outcomes — had been the end of the transmission. The last thing Mirelle sent before the signal cut.

She was still sending.

The transmission carried her voice through the Hall of Nations' speaker system — the same institutional infrastructure that had carried her testimony to the tribunal three years ago, the same speakers that had amplified her cross-examination of the NitroCore representative, the same translation feeds that had processed her French-accented English into twelve simultaneous languages. The infrastructure knew her voice. The infrastructure had carried it before. The infrastructure carried it now — not from the witness stand but from inside the thing the institution had failed to prevent, and the voice was not testimony, not accusation, not elegy. The voice was reporting. Still reporting. Still doing the work. The journalist absorbed mid-sentence was mid-sentence still, the report continuing from inside the void with the same professional precision, the same source-verified specificity, the same *Mirelle* quality of stating facts in the order that made the institutional failure most architecturally visible.

*"—filed under Project SHEPHERD, subsection infrastructure, pre-breach deployment timeline. The corridor was active before the breach was declared. Repeat: the corridor was active before the breach was declared. The NitroCore deployment schedule—"*

The transmission stopped.

Eleven seconds. He would learn this later — the global duration, the simultaneity, the institutional measurement that converted the event into a datum. In the moment, the eleven seconds were not a duration. They were the time it took for Mirelle Dubois to continue filing her report from inside the void through the speaker system of the institution that had failed to act on her previous reports.

The chamber was silent. One hundred and twelve delegations. The procedural motion on item four suspended mid-vote. The holographic flags cycling through their post-interrupt recovery sequence — reconstituting, stabilizing, the Jordanian flag resuming its familiar dissolution pattern as the display system's processing architecture returned to its standard function.

Zeyad's hands were on the gallery railing. He had not gripped the railing. His hands were there — resting, the posture of a man who had been sitting in the gallery and was now holding the railing the way a person holds a railing when the floor has shifted beneath them. The shift was not physical. The floor was stable. The building was stable. The institutional infrastructure was returning to its baseline function. The hum was settling back to its foundation register — New Geneva's ambient frequency, the institutional furniture, the sound of a building that had just broadcast the dead through its own speaker system and was now resuming normal operations.

His pen was in his breast pocket. He did not reach for it.

He reached for his recorder.

---

The recorder was a device he had carried since Lagos.

Not the pen's equivalent — the pen was for the margin, for the notation, for the act of writing that converted observation into record. The recorder was for the thing the pen could not capture. He had purchased it at the airport in Lagos, the day after the Anchor Zone formed, the day after Mumbai, the day the hum was in his chest and the FAS containment protocol had held and 2.3 million people were dead. He had purchased it because the margin notes could not contain what the hum sounded like when it rose through the body. The margin notes could not contain the frequency. The recorder could.

He had not used it in New Geneva. There had been nothing in New Geneva that required audio documentation. The sessions were transcribed. The proceedings were recorded by the institutional systems. The margin notes were sufficient for the procedural observations the gallery permitted.

He used it now.

He held the recorder to the gallery's nearest speaker — the small directional unit mounted in the gallery's ceiling, designed to carry the translation feed to the observers above. The speaker was silent. The Voice had stopped. But the building's systems held residual signal — the acoustic decay, the infrastructure's settling pattern, the last frequencies dissipating through the conduit network the way the last notes of a chord dissipate through the body of the instrument. The recorder captured the decay. The recorder captured the silence that followed. The recorder captured the specific quality of a building returning to its institutional function after transmitting the voices of the dead.

He rewound. He played it back. Through the recorder's small speaker — tinny, compressed, the audio quality of a personal device — Mirelle's voice. Faint. Present tense. *"Infrastructure pre-positioned. Absorption corridor confirmed—"*

The recorder had captured her.

He put the recorder in his jacket pocket. He stood. He walked to the gallery's exit — the narrow corridor that connected the observation level to the main building, the route he had walked four times a week for ten months, the route that took him past the eastern alcove where Khalil's portrait hung and down the stairs to the session floor level.

The grey unlaminated access pass was clipped to his breast pocket. The pass permitted the gallery. The pass did not permit the floor. The security checkpoint between the gallery corridor and the session level was staffed by two officers who had checked his credentials four times a week for ten months and who knew his face and his name and his pass and the specific institutional status the pass represented.

The checkpoint was unattended.

The officers were not at their station. The building was in the specific state of institutional disruption that Zeyad recognized from thirty-four years of diplomatic service — the brief interval between an event the institution could not process and the moment the institution began processing it. In the interval, the procedures lapsed. In the interval, the checkpoints emptied. In the interval, a man with a grey unlaminated access pass could walk from the gallery to the session floor because the institution that had suspended his credentials was itself suspended.

He walked through the checkpoint. Down the stairs. Through the corridor that smelled of the same institutional air conditioning, the same filtered atmosphere, the same temperature-regulated neutral air that every UGC building in New Geneva shared. Past the eastern alcove. Khalil's portrait — the 2047 photograph, his great-grandfather in the formal posture of a man who believed in the institution he was helping to build, the diplomatic credentials visible on the desk before him, the pen in his hand. The pen was the same model. The pen had been the same model for four generations.

He did not stop at the portrait. He had stopped at the portrait four times a week for ten months. Today he walked past it the way a man walks past a thing he has already seen.

The session floor. The chamber was in the state he had observed from the gallery — delegates standing, some seated, the procedural machinery stalled, the session clerk at the recording station typing furiously into the official transcript, the institutional act of documenting the interruption. The clerk was documenting the Voice the way the clerk documented every event — as an item, a timestamp, a procedural notation. The institution's reflex. The first response was always the record.

Zeyad walked to the session clerk's station. He placed the recorder on the table beside the clerk's terminal.

The clerk looked up. The clerk knew him — Ambassador Al-Fahim, suspended, tribunal pending, grey unlaminated pass. The clerk's expression carried the specific institutional awareness of a person who recognized that the man standing before them should not be on the session floor and that the procedures that would normally prevent it were not currently operational.

"Let the record show," Zeyad said.

His voice was the diplomatic register. The practiced composure. The thirty-four years of choosing words for their load-bearing capacity, of constructing sentences that would survive translation into twelve languages and transcription into the official record and the specific institutional scrutiny that converted spoken testimony into permanent documentation.

"Let the record show that the void spoke at 14:37 UTC through this building's infrastructure systems. Let the record show that among the voices was Mirelle Dubois, journalist, absorbed during BLACKWEIR operations in Neo-Shanghai, who was at the time of absorption filing a report on NitroCore infrastructure pre-positioning in the absorption corridors." He placed his hand on the recorder. "Let the record show that Mirelle Dubois is still filing her report. The recording on this device contains her continued transmission. Let the record show that the Accords have no framework for this."

The clerk typed. The clerk's hands moved across the terminal with the specific institutional reflex of a person who had been trained to transcribe what was spoken into the record regardless of whether the person speaking had the credentials to speak. The record did not check credentials. The record received what was said. The record was the one institutional mechanism that did not differentiate between a credentialed delegate and a suspended ambassador.

The clerk typed every word.

"Noted," the clerk said. "Timestamp 14:41 UTC. Statement entered into the emergency session record, Item Four supplemental."

"Thank you," Zeyad said.

He picked up the recorder. He walked back to the gallery. Through the corridor. Past the checkpoint — still unattended, the institutional disruption not yet resolved, the procedures not yet restored. Up the stairs. Through the gallery entrance. To his seat. Third row, eastern section.

He sat down.

The session resumed at 14:58 UTC.

Seventeen minutes. The institutional machinery required seventeen minutes to restore itself — to convert the event into a procedural item, to return the delegates to their seats, to advance the agenda past the supplemental notation on Item Four, to resume the vote on the seventy-two-hour mandate extension. Seventeen minutes between the voices of the dead and the next motion on the floor. The efficiency was not callous. The efficiency was structural. The institution processed events at the speed the institution processed events, and the speed did not change because the event was unprecedented, and the processing did not stop because the content was ontologically impossible.

Item Four passed. The mandate was extended. Item Five began — a report from the Scientific Advisory Board on the synchronization data that had preceded the event. The report was technical. The language was institutional. The data was presented in the format the institution required — tables, confidence intervals, the vocabulary of measured uncertainty applied to phenomena that exceeded measurement.

Zeyad sat in the gallery and did not take notes.

His pen was in his breast pocket. The notebook was in his jacket's inner pocket. His hands were on the gallery railing — the same position, the same posture, the composure that had held through thirty-four years of diplomatic service and the tribunal and Lagos and the breach zone and the news of Mumbai and the hum in his chest. The composure held. The composure had always held. The composure was the architecture and the architecture was the inheritance and the inheritance was what Khalil had given him along with the pen and the portrait and the specific belief that if the record was complete, the institution would act.

The record was complete.

The record contained the tribunal proceedings. The record contained seventeen stages of procedural processing. The record contained Mirelle's dead-hand transmission — the NitroCore infrastructure documentation, the evidence of deliberate pre-positioning, the proof that the absorption corridors were engineered. The record contained his margin notes. The record contained the Lagos field observations. The record contained the FAS alternative model analysis. The record contained his framework document — the document that had been tabled without discussion, the document that proposed an alternative that was sound and that would not be adopted. The record contained 2.3 million dead in Mumbai. The record contained the hum in his chest.

And now the record contained Mirelle. Still reporting. Still filing. Still doing the work from inside the thing the institution had failed to prevent. The record contained her voice at 14:37 UTC, speaking through the institution's own infrastructure, continuing the sentence that had been interrupted during BLACKWEIR, providing the evidence that the institution had not acted on when she was alive and would not act on now that she was — whatever she was. Not dead. Not alive. Still reporting.

The belief did not break.

He had expected it to break — the way structures break, catastrophically, the load-bearing element failing and the architecture collapsing. He had expected that hearing Mirelle's voice from inside the void would produce the rupture that thirty-four years of institutional service had been building toward — the moment when the belief that the record mattered, that the institution could act, that the process was not futile, would fail under its own accumulated weight.

The belief did not break. It dissolved. The way the Jordanian flag dissolved — not a rupture but a loss of coherence, the signal degrading, the display cycling through its failure pattern, the image present and not-present simultaneously. The belief was still there. He could still see it. He could see the institution below him processing the agenda. He could see the delegates voting. He could see the clerk typing the supplemental notation. He could see the holographic flags reconstituting after their systemic interrupt. The institution was functioning. The belief was functioning. The record was being kept.

The record did not produce justice. The record did not produce action. The record produced a record. The record was the institution's terminal function — not the means to an end but the end itself. The institution documented. The institution transcribed. The institution filed. The institution extended its mandate by seventy-two hours. The institution had no framework for the voices of the dead speaking through its own infrastructure, and the institutional response to having no framework was to note the absence of a framework in the record and proceed to Item Five.

His pen was in his breast pocket. He did not reach for it.

The pen had written questions in the tribunal. The pen had written the framework document in Lagos. The pen had gone still during Mumbai. The pen had resumed — always resumed, because the pen was the inheritance and the inheritance was the function and the function was documentation. The pen wrote. That was what the pen did.

He reached for the notebook. He opened it to a new page — the first blank page after three-quarters of margin notes and procedural observations and the shorthand of a man who had documented everything because documentation was the only act the institution permitted him. The page was blank. The pen was in his hand.

He did not write a margin note. He did not write a procedural observation. He did not write the institutional shorthand.

He wrote:

*What follows is an account of what the Accords were designed to do, what they did instead, and what must replace them.*

The sentence sat on the page. The ink was from the pen that had been in his family for four generations — the pen that had signed the original Accords, that had written Khalil's margin notes in the founding sessions, that had documented his father's postings in Cairo and Geneva and New York, that had written Zeyad's tribunal notes for ten months. The pen had written institutional language for four generations. The sentence on the page was not institutional language. The sentence on the page was the first line of a document that the Accords had no framework for, because the Accords had no framework for their own replacement.

Below, the session continued. Item Five. The Scientific Advisory Board's report. The institutional machinery processing the data at the speed the institutional machinery processed data. The holographic flags stable — except the Jordanian flag, still dissolving, still reconstituting, the maintenance request still unfiled, the building still unable to repair a holographic emitter while transmitting the voices of the dead through its own speaker system.

The hum was in the foundation. New Geneva's register. Ambient. Institutional. The furniture of the building that had just broadcast Mirelle Dubois continuing her report from inside the void.

He thought of Mirelle. Not the voice — the voice was on the recorder, the voice was in the record, the voice was documented. He thought of the person. The journalist who had called him *Ambassador* with the inflection that meant the question was coming. The source who had built the NitroCore documentation package that had arrived as a dead-hand transmission months after her absorption. The professional who was still professional, still reporting, still filing, still doing the work from wherever the absorbed existed — because the work was what she was, the way the documentation was what he was, the way the institution was what the institution was. Mirelle was still Mirelle. The void had not changed her function. The void had changed her location.

He looked at the sentence on the page. The first line. The pen was in his hand. The pen moved.

He began to write.
