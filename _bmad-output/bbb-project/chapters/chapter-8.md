## Draft v1

Nuwa was cooking when he came through the door.

He smelled it before he saw her — soy paste and chili oil and the sharp mineral note of reconstituted protein heating in a pan that was older than both of them. The smell cut through the corridor's chemical sweetness like a blade. Real food. Not the subsidized ration packs that came vacuum-sealed with the Provision logo and a calorie count that assumed you spent your shift sitting down. Nuwa had found something at the market — actual vegetables, or close enough. She did this sometimes. Came home from the textile plant and went straight to the Hum Market with the credits she'd set aside from the household fund, the ones Fuxi pretended not to notice were missing because the argument about it was more expensive than the food.

She was standing at the heating element with her back to him. Her hair was shorter than last month — she'd cut it herself, the way she always did, with the shears from the plant. The cut was uneven at the back. She didn't care. Nuwa's relationship with her appearance was purely functional — anything that could get caught in machinery got cut, and everything else was irrelevant. She was wearing the grey coverall from the plant, sleeves pushed to her elbows. Her forearms were stained. The textile recycling process used a chemical solvent that turned skin a faint violet where it made contact, and Nuwa's hands and wrists were mapped with it — a topography of eight-hour shifts and inadequate protective equipment and a plant manager who had filed the requisition for new gloves six months ago and received, in response, a revised safety protocol that reclassified the solvent exposure threshold.

"Boots," she said, without turning around.

Fuxi looked down at his boots. Orange dust. Deep Sump residue. The mat by the door was right there.

"I'm inside."

"You're on my floor."

He stepped onto the mat. Pulled his boots off. The apartment floor was cold through his socks — the concrete transmitted the temperature the way it transmitted the hum, evenly, without preference, a medium through which everything traveled. He set the boots on the mat, side by side, the way Nuwa had been asking him to do for three years and the way he'd been doing for approximately one.

She turned around. Her face was the same shape as his — their mother's jaw, their father's forehead, the Lin geometry that marked them as siblings before they opened their mouths. But where Fuxi's face had been compressed by the Deep Sump into an expression that defaulted to neutral, Nuwa's face was in constant negotiation with itself. She was twenty-four and she looked twenty-four and she looked older than twenty-four and she looked like someone who had decided that looking tired was a waste of energy she could spend on something else.

"You look terrible," she said.

"Standard."

"Worse than standard. Sit."

He sat. The table was a slab of composite bolted to the wall — everything in the apartment was bolted to something, a habit of construction from when the Sump residential blocks had been built over active conduit lines and the vibration had been strong enough to move furniture. The vibration was weaker now, at this depth. Barely perceptible through the table's surface. Fuxi rested his hands flat on it anyway and felt the hum — faint, constant, the building's pulse carried through concrete and steel and composite and into the bones of his wrists. Home frequency. Different from the Deep Sump's bass drone. Thinner. Quicker. A domestic hum.

Nuwa set a bowl in front of him. Reconstituted protein with chili oil, soy paste, and something green that might have been bok choy or might have been a convincing imitation. Steam rose from the surface and the smell was close and real and human in a way that the monitoring bay never was.

"Eat," she said. "Before it gets cold and becomes evidence."

"Evidence of what?"

"Of the fact that I spent twenty creds on vegetables instead of putting it toward the frame fund. Eat fast. Destroy the evidence."

He ate. The food was good. Better than good — it was specific. It tasted like something someone had chosen to make, not something extruded from a processing line and sealed in a packet with a barcode. Nuwa's cooking was like her conversation: layered, deliberate, assembled from whatever was available into something that exceeded the sum of its components. She sat across from him with her own bowl and ate the way she always ate — quickly, efficiently, one hand holding the bowl close, the other working the chopsticks with a precision that came from the same muscle memory she used at the plant, sorting textiles by weight and grade, her hands reading material the way Fuxi's hands read pipe casings.

The hum was in the walls. In the floor. In the table between them. It was the sound of the conduit network running through the building's infrastructure — the smaller distribution lines that branched off the trunk system and fed the Sump residential blocks with the nitro that powered the lights, the heating elements, the frame relay stations, the water recyclers. Everything. The hum was the sound of everything working. It was their white noise. They had grown up inside it, slept inside it, eaten ten thousand meals inside it. Fuxi did not hear it the way he did not hear his own breathing — it was present and constant and below the threshold of attention, and the only time he noticed it was when it changed.

It had not changed.

"So," Nuwa said. She set her bowl down and leaned back and looked at him with the expression that meant she was about to build a case. "The P-3."

"The P-3."

"Stall 14 guy says one more week on the hold. After that it goes back on the table."

"We're two months into the savings plan."

"Two months into *your* savings plan. I have a different plan."

Fuxi set down his chopsticks. "What plan."

"Yuen's plant is doing a round of transfers. Mid-Level textile operations. Quality-control positions. They need people with experience on the sorting line who can interface with the QC monitoring system, which requires" — she held up one finger — "a functional frame with AR overlay that doesn't render my supervisor as a floating torso."

"The P-3."

"The P-3. With the sensory filter. Which also means I stop getting the headaches at work, which means I stop burning two credits a week on the suppressants, which means the math actually works if you run it forward six months instead of looking at it as a lump expense."

Fuxi picked up his chopsticks again. The food was getting cold. He ate and listened to her build the case, brick by brick, the same way she'd built it over frame messages in the Deep Sump — but hearing it in her voice, in person, with her hands moving as she talked, was different. She used her hands the way he used monitoring data. Each gesture was a data point. The finger counting savings. The flat palm pressing down on the table as she laid out the transfer requirements. The quick sweep through her hair — the uneven cut, the shears she wasn't supposed to have — when she hit the part about transit permits.

"Transit permits are locked for the quarter," Fuxi said.

"Transit permits are locked for Sump-to-Spires. Sump-to-Mid-Level transfers on employer sponsorship are processed separately. Yuen's plant has a standing arrangement with GridLine for employee mobility within the industrial classification. It's a different queue."

"You've already checked."

"I've already checked."

He looked at her. She looked back. The conduit glow came through the frosted polymer window and cast its orange light across the table, across her face, across the violet staining on her hands. In the Deep Sump, the orange was absolute — it saturated everything, turned the world monochrome. Up here, in the apartment, it was diluted by the overhead fixture that Nuwa had repaired herself three weeks ago when the building maintenance had failed to respond to the fourth request. The light in the apartment was orange and white and something between the two. It turned Nuwa's face into a study in warmth that was not entirely the conduit's doing.

"Mid-Level," he said.

"Mid-Level."

"That's up."

"That's the point, Fuxi."

He ate another bite. Chewed. The protein had gone cold and the texture had changed — the reconstitution process was heat-dependent, and cooled reconstituted protein had a grain to it that reminded him of gasket material. He ate it anyway.

"The seniority I have with the service—"

"—keeps you in the Deep Sump maintaining trunk-lines for ten hours a day while the RCI readings climb and the suppressants your coworkers take make their hands slow and the overtime they offer for the Resonance District section comes at double rate because everyone who works there for more than six months files medical claims that the company settles out of the emergency discretionary budget that technically doesn't exist." She said all of this without raising her voice. The words came fast and flat and organized, the way sorted textiles came off her line — graded by weight, by urgency, by the order in which they needed to land. "Your seniority is a leash, and the leash is attached to a pipe, and the pipe is getting warmer."

"The pipe is fine."

"The pipe is always fine. Until it isn't."

The hum filled the silence between them. It was a good silence — not empty, not hostile. A silence that held the shape of an argument they'd had before and would have again, an argument whose contours they both knew the way Fuxi knew the trunk-line's resonance. Familiar. Navigable. Survivable.

Nuwa picked up her bowl again. Ate. Her jaw worked with the same efficient rhythm as her hands at the sorting line. Then:

"I'm not asking you to leave. I'm asking you to let me try."

"I'm not stopping you."

"You're calculating. I can see it. You're running the math on what happens if I get the transfer and you're still down there, and the math says the rent goes up because the subsidy is tied to your service address and my contribution drops during the transition period and the net is negative for four months."

The math did say that. He had been running it since she first mentioned Yuen's transfers three days ago, the numbers cycling in the back of his mind the way monitoring data cycled across his screens — automatically, continuously, a background process that never stopped.

"Four months is manageable," he said.

"Four months is manageable." She put her bowl down. "Wait. Is that a yes?"

"That's a the-math-is-manageable."

"Coming from you that's practically a parade."

She smiled. Not large — Nuwa's smiles were compressed, the way everything in the Sump was compressed, occupying the minimum necessary space. But present. It changed the geometry of her face in a way that reminded him of their mother, and the reminder was a blade that he turned aside the way he turned aside all the blades that came at him in the space between work and sleep — not by force but by redirection, by sending the thought down a channel where it could run without cutting.

He gathered the bowls. Took them to the basin. The water came out warm — heated by the conduit lines that ran behind the wall, one of the few advantages of living inside the infrastructure. He washed the bowls and felt the hum through the water and through the basin and through his hands, which were still calibrated to the Deep Sump's frequency and which registered the apartment's hum as a high, thin cousin of the sound that lived in his bones for ten hours every shift.

"The market was busy today," Nuwa said behind him.

"Mmhm."

"I went past stall 14. The P-3 is still there. The guy remembers me."

"He remembers your creds."

"Same thing, down here." A pause. The kind of pause that had texture. "The route back was crowded. Everyone going the same way, toward the residential blocks. Normal crowd."

He heard the pause. Heard what she wasn't saying underneath it. The route from the Hum Market to their block ran past two conduit access points — heavy steel doors set into the corridor walls, marked with the NitroCore logo and the universal hazard symbol that everyone in the Sump had learned to ignore the way they'd learned to ignore the hum. The access points were where the shimmers appeared. Not often. Not predictably. But the municipal advisory had been updated twice in the last quarter — *Ambient hum levels within certified tolerance. If you experience headaches, contact NitroCore health station.* — and the advisory only updated when the readings moved.

"Normal crowd," he said.

"Normal crowd."

He set the clean bowl in the rack. Turned off the water. The basin's vibration ceased but the hum continued — walls, floor, ceiling, the constant architecture of sound that was the Sump's version of quiet.

He did not ask her what she'd seen near the conduit access point on the route back from stall 14. She did not offer. The not-asking and the not-offering were the same shape — a mutual agreement, enacted without negotiation, to keep certain observations inside the body where they could not become problems. Fuxi knew this shape. He practiced it professionally. Every shift at station 3, watching monitors 4 through 6, logging what the instruments showed and not logging what his body felt. The domestic version was gentler. Smaller. Built from love rather than protocol.

But it was the same architecture.

Nuwa went to bed at 22:00. She had the early shift tomorrow — textiles ran 06:00 to 14:00 on this rotation, and Nuwa's rotation started at the sorting line and ended at chemical processing, which meant she needed her hands steady and her headache manageable and her frame functional enough to read the material grade codes that the QC system projected in AR across each batch. The current frame — salvaged, second-hand, its overlay glitching near conduit junctions — was costing her accuracy. Accuracy was costing her the transfer.

The P-3 was not a luxury. It was infrastructure. Fuxi understood this the way he understood gasket tolerances — not as an abstraction but as a physical fact with physical consequences. The frame was the difference between Nuwa staying at the Sump plant and Nuwa moving to Mid-Level. Mid-Level was not the Spires. It was not escape. It was forty meters higher and marginally less orange and the hum was quieter and the transit permits were easier and the medical coverage included the solvent-exposure treatment that the Sump plant classified as elective.

Forty meters. In a city that measured status by altitude, forty meters was a life.

He sat on the bed in the dark. The blue textile was beneath him — the deep blue fabric with the interlocking circles that Nuwa had salvaged from the recycling plant because it was the bluest thing the plant had ever processed and because everything in the Sump was orange and because she wanted one thing that wasn't. He ran his hand across it. The texture was smooth, worn soft by use, and in the dark it was just a fabric, but in the conduit glow it was the only thing in the apartment that wasn't the color of nitro.

His hand found the multitool in his jacket pocket. His father's. Jun Lin's. Stainless steel, heavy for its size, the handle worn to a polish by decades of use — first in his father's hands, maintaining the same trunk-lines that Fuxi maintained now, and then in Fuxi's, carried every shift but rarely used for its intended purpose. The service-issue toolkit was better. Newer. Calibrated. The multitool was obsolete. He carried it anyway. The weight of it in his pocket was a specific thing — not comfort, not memory, not sentiment. *Presence.* His father's hands had held this tool in the same corridors where Fuxi's hands worked now, and his father had died of the cumulative exposure that the company medical officer called "chronic resonance fatigue syndrome" and that the Deep Sump crews called "the hum got him," and the multitool was what remained.

He turned it in his fingers. The hum came through the walls and the floor and the building's bones and settled behind his eyes the way it always did — a pressure that was not quite a headache and not quite nothing. Occupational tinnitus. Certified tolerance.

Monitor 6 had read 27.1 at the end of his shift today. Baseline. Normal. The spike from three weeks ago — 31.2, hold, linear decay — had not recurred. The junction housing 4E-9 inspection he'd recommended had been scheduled, conducted, and closed with a finding of "no anomaly detected." The system had self-corrected. The log had cycled through the active queue and was aging toward the ninety-day archive. In fourteen more shifts, it would be gone.

But the shape.

He kept coming back to the shape. The linear decline. The clean, precise descent from peak to baseline as if the reading had known where it was going and had gone there deliberately. Not a system decaying. Not a fluctuation self-correcting through the accumulated friction of redundancy margins and flow regulators. A line. Drawn from here to there. Straight.

He had not told Nuwa about the spike. He had not told Nuwa about the shape. This was not deception — Nuwa did not ask about his readings the way he did not ask about her solvent exposure, and the mutual not-asking was how they lived inside a system that generated information faster than it generated the capacity to act on it. You learned what to hold and what to set down. You learned which data points were actionable and which were the ambient noise of living inside infrastructure that was a hundred and twenty years old and running at three times its design specification. Most of the noise was just noise. The hum was just the hum. The readings self-corrected.

Fuxi sat on the blue textile in the dark apartment in the Sump of Neo-Shanghai and held his father's multitool and listened to the hum that had been his white noise since the day he was born and did not think about the shape of the decline on monitor 6 and did not think about what Nuwa had not said about the route back from stall 14 and did not think about the municipal advisory that had been updated twice in the last quarter for a hum that was within certified tolerance.

He thought about the P-3. Four more months. The math was manageable. Nuwa would get the frame. Nuwa would get the transfer. Nuwa would move up — forty meters, a different plant, better medical, a frame that worked. He would stay in the Deep Sump because the Deep Sump was where the work was and the work was what kept them fed and housed and saving. This was the arrangement. This was the architecture of their lives — Fuxi below, maintaining the system that maintained the city, and Nuwa above, or soon above, rising through the narrow pathways that existed for people who were precise and fast and willing to cut their own hair with stolen shears.

He would stay. She would rise. He would keep the pipes running and the hum constant and the monitors green and his hands steady and his reports filed and his questions unasked, because the questions were not actionable and the answers were not survivable and the system self-corrected and his sister needed four more months.

Outside, the conduit glow pulsed through the frosted polymer window. Orange. Constant. The color of the world that worked. The hum carried through the building and into the bones of a man sitting in the dark with a dead man's tool in his hands, and the system was running, and the system was fine, and the readings were within tolerance, and the advisory had been updated only twice, and somewhere two hundred meters below, the trunk-lines carried what they carried at volumes that were classified and temperatures that were rising and in a pattern that — if you looked at it from exactly the right angle, and if you had spent six years learning what the instruments couldn't show you — was not quite noise.
