## Draft v1

The hum in Section 4-East was running three hertz flat.

Fuxi felt it before the instruments confirmed it — a tonal shift in the trunk-line's resonance that registered first in his molars, then in the bones of his wrists where they pressed against the inspection rail. Three hertz was nothing. Within tolerance. The kind of variance that showed up in the logs as a rounding error and disappeared into the quarterly reports that nobody in the Spires would ever read. But Fuxi had been running trunk-line inspections in the Deep Sump for six years, and his body had become an instrument calibrated to the infrastructure the way a sailor's body calibrates to the sea — not through understanding but through repetition, through ten thousand hours of hands on pipes and boots on grating and the constant low-frequency pressure of nitro moving through conduits at volumes that would have been classified as hazardous in any stratum above this one.

Three hertz flat. He logged it.

The monitoring bay was a concrete box bolted to the side of Trunk-Line 4-East at the junction where the first-generation infrastructure met the newer bypass conduits installed during the 2160s expansion. Six monitors. Six stations. Three of them automated — their operators replaced by sensor arrays that fed data directly to the NitroCore grid management system, which processed the readings and generated work orders that arrived on Fuxi's frame as terse directives stripped of context. *Inspect junction housing 4E-7. Recalibrate flow sensor 4E-12. Replace gasket assembly 4E-3, priority standard.* The other three stations were still crewed. Not because the automation couldn't handle them — it could. Because the trunk-lines in this section were first-generation, laid down in the 2050s during the Great Transition, and their junction housings had been designed for manual inspection at intervals that predated the sensor technology by decades. The housings had manual access ports. The access ports required hands. The hands required people.

Fuxi was one of the people.

He sat at station 3, which covered monitors 4 through 6. The chair was bolted to the floor — everything in the Deep Sump was bolted to the floor, because the trunk-line vibration at this depth was strong enough to walk unsecured furniture across a room over the course of a shift. The monitor screens cast their data in amber and green against the concrete walls. The overhead lighting was industrial fluorescent, but it didn't matter — the dominant light source was the conduit glow. Orange. The color of nitro seen through pipe walls that had thinned over a century and a half of continuous flow, the metal becoming translucent in places where the resonance had restructured the molecular lattice of the steel. In the Deep Sump, everything was orange. The walls. The ceiling. The faces of the people who worked here. The inside of your eyelids when you closed them.

The hum was a physical thing. Not sound — not only sound. It was pressure. Vibration transmitted through the concrete floor, through the bolted chair, through the bones of Fuxi's pelvis and spine and into his skull, where it settled behind his eyes like a headache that wasn't quite a headache. The Deep Sump crews called it being *hum-drunk* — the chronic low-grade disorientation that came from spending ten hours a day inside the resonance field of a first-generation trunk-line carrying R1 nitro at peak throughput. Headaches. Memory fog. A persistent ringing in the ears that the company medical officer called "occupational tinnitus" and treated with suppressants that Fuxi had stopped taking two years ago because they made his hands slow and his hands were the only thing between him and a work order that could kill him if he fumbled a gasket seal at the wrong moment.

Chen Wei was at station 1. Twenty years on the same shift rotation. Twenty years of trunk-line data scrolling across the same monitors in the same amber light. He sat the way he always sat — shoulders rounded, chin forward, eyes tracking the data with the mechanical patience of a man who had stopped seeing the numbers as information and started seeing them as weather. Things that happened. Things that passed. Things that self-corrected.

"Three hertz flat on 4-East," Fuxi said.

Chen Wei didn't look up. "Logged?"

"Logged."

"Then it's logged."

Fuxi turned back to his monitors. The three hertz variance was already normalizing — the trunk-line's resonance climbing back toward baseline as the flow regulators downstream adjusted. This was how it worked. The system was self-correcting. The trunk-lines had been designed with redundancy margins that absorbed minor fluctuations the way a river absorbs a thrown stone — a ripple, a moment of displacement, and then the current reasserted itself and the surface was smooth again. The engineers who had designed the first-generation infrastructure had built it to last, and it had lasted, and the fact that it was now a hundred and twenty years old and running at throughput volumes three times its original specification was a problem that existed in the quarterly reports but not in the daily reality of the people who maintained it.

The daily reality was: the pipes held. The flow continued. The hum was constant. The monitors showed green.

A message pulsed in Fuxi's peripheral vision — amber text against the monitoring bay's orange glow, routed through his ocular implant from the service-issue frame strapped to his left forearm. The frame's housing was cracked along one edge, a hairline fracture that threw artifacts into the lower left of his AR overlay. He'd been meaning to file for a replacement, but the replacement would come from the same requisition queue as his jacket, and the queue had opinions about what constituted *non-functional.* The message was from Nuwa.

*Found it. Hum Market, stall 14, the one with the blue tarp. Shenlong P-3 with sensory filter. Refurbished but the guy says the filter module is new. 1,200 creds.*

Fuxi read it twice. Twelve hundred credits. Three months of savings at their current rate — and that was assuming no emergency expenses, no equipment failures, no medical costs, no increase in the transit fees that GridLine adjusted quarterly and always upward. Three months of eating the subsidized rations instead of the market food. Three months of Nuwa working double shifts at the textile recycling plant, coming home with her hands stained and her shoulders knotted and the hum-drunk headache that she treated with the same suppressants Fuxi had stopped taking.

He subvocalized the reply, jaw barely moving, the frame reading the muscle contractions: *New filter module or "new" filter module?*

The response came fast. Nuwa was on break, or pretending to be.

*He showed me the seal. Looked real. Also he has all his fingers, which for a Hum Market frame dealer is basically a certificate of quality.*

Fuxi almost smiled. Almost — because smiling in the monitoring bay invited questions, and questions invited attention, and attention in the Deep Sump was never the kind you wanted. He kept his face neutral and mouthed: *Three months. If the overtime holds.*

*Three months. I can do three months. My current frame keeps dropping the AR overlay when I'm near the conduit line at work. Yesterday it rendered my supervisor as a floating torso for ten minutes. I didn't correct it because honestly it was an improvement.*

He dismissed the overlay and turned back to the monitors.

---

Monitor 6 was the one closest to the Resonance District boundary.

Fuxi didn't think about it in those terms — the Resonance District was a neighborhood name, a place where people lived, not a technical designation. But the infrastructure didn't care about neighborhood names. The infrastructure cared about throughput, and the throughput beneath the Resonance District was the highest in the city because that was where the six first-generation trunk-lines converged at the Confluence — the deepest junction node in Neo-Shanghai's conduit network, where the original 2050s engineers had routed the primary distribution lines in a radial pattern that fed every stratum above. The Confluence was automated. Skeleton crew. Fuxi had never been inside it — his clearance covered Section 4-East, not the Confluence itself, and the Confluence was NitroCore direct, not municipal service. But he could feel it. Everyone in the Deep Sump could feel it. The hum was louder near the Confluence. The orange glow was brighter. The air was warmer — not from heating systems but from the conduits themselves, the nitro inside them generating thermal output as a byproduct of the resonance, the way a wire generates heat when current passes through it.

Monitor 6 tracked the RCI readings for the section of Trunk-Line 4-East that ran closest to the Confluence boundary. Baseline for this section was 26 to 28 — higher than the Sump average of 15 to 25, lower than the Confluence readings that Fuxi had never seen but that the automated systems processed and forwarded to NitroCore without passing through the monitoring bay. The RCI at this depth was a number that meant nothing to anyone who hadn't spent years learning what it felt like. Twenty-six felt like a low-grade headache and a taste of copper at the back of your throat. Twenty-eight felt like the headache had settled in and the copper had become a permanent resident. Thirty felt like the first day of being hum-drunk — the disorientation, the slight lag between intention and action, the sense that the air itself had thickened.

Fuxi had felt thirty twice in six years. Both times during scheduled throughput increases — NitroCore pushing more volume through the trunk-lines to meet demand spikes in the Spires, the increased flow raising the RCI in the Deep Sump the way a rising tide raises the water level in a basement. Both times the readings had climbed, held, and then returned to baseline when the demand spike passed. Both times Chen Wei had said the same thing.

*They always self-correct.*

At 14:47, monitor 6 spiked.

Not gradually. Not the slow climb of a throughput increase working its way through the system. Between one data refresh and the next — the monitors updated every fifteen seconds — the RCI reading on monitor 6 jumped from 27.1 to 31.2.

Fuxi's hands stopped moving.

Thirty-one point two. Four points above baseline. Not catastrophic — the alarm threshold was set at 35, and the emergency protocols didn't activate until 40. But the *speed* of the change was wrong. RCI fluctuations in the trunk-lines were gradual. They followed the flow dynamics — pressure changes propagated through the conduit network at predictable rates, and the RCI tracked the pressure the way a shadow tracks the object casting it. A four-point jump in fifteen seconds meant either a massive throughput surge upstream — which would have triggered alerts on every monitor in the bay — or something else.

Something local.

"Chen Wei."

The shift supervisor looked up. Fuxi pointed at monitor 6. Chen Wei leaned forward, read the number, and leaned back.

"Fluctuation."

"Four points in one cycle."

"Fluctuation," Chen Wei said again. The word carried the weight of twenty years. Not dismissal — something older than dismissal. The institutional certainty of a man who had watched ten thousand readings spike and ten thousand readings return to baseline and had concluded, reasonably, that the system was self-correcting and that self-correction was the system's purpose and that anything the system corrected was, by definition, not a problem.

Fuxi watched the monitor. The reading held at 31.2 for the next refresh cycle. Fifteen seconds. Then another. 31.2. 31.1. Holding. Holding at a level that was wrong — not dangerously wrong, not alarm-threshold wrong, but *wrong* the way a gasket reads clean on inspection but your fingers feel the grain running the wrong direction. Present when it should have passed.

He could feel it. The hum in Section 4-East had changed — not in volume but in *texture.* The three-hertz variance from earlier had been a flattening, a loss of harmonic content. This was the opposite. The hum had gained something. A density. A quality that Fuxi's body registered as pressure behind his sternum, a tightness in the muscles of his jaw. Not pain. Not discomfort. *Attention.* His body was paying attention to the hum the way it paid attention to a sound in the dark — not because the sound was loud but because it was *new.*

Thirty seconds. 31.0. Dropping.

"See?" Chen Wei said. He hadn't moved from his station. "Self-correcting."

Fuxi watched the number fall. 30.8. 30.4. 29.7. 28.9. 28.1. 27.3. Baseline. The reading settled back into the 26-to-28 range as if the spike had never happened. The hum in the monitoring bay returned to its normal character — the constant, featureless pressure that Fuxi's body had learned to treat as silence.

But the correction was wrong.

Not the fact of it — trunk-line RCI fluctuations self-corrected. That was what they did. The system had redundancy margins. The flow regulators adjusted. The readings returned to baseline. This was normal. This was the daily reality of Deep Sump infrastructure maintenance, and Fuxi had logged hundreds of fluctuations that had followed exactly this pattern: spike, hold, decay, baseline.

The *shape* of the correction was wrong.

A natural RCI decay followed a curve — a logarithmic decline, steep at first and then flattening as the reading approached baseline, the way a pendulum swings wide and then narrower and narrower until it settles. Fuxi had seen this curve thousands of times. He knew its shape the way he knew the shape of his own hands.

This hadn't been a curve. This had been a line. A clean, linear descent from 31.2 to 27.3 in forty seconds. No overshoot. No oscillation. No asymptotic approach. The reading had dropped at a constant rate and stopped precisely at baseline, as if someone had drawn a straight line from the peak to the target and the system had followed it.

Systems didn't do that. Systems decayed. They oscillated. They overshot and corrected and overshot again, each swing smaller than the last, until they settled. That was how physical systems behaved — messily, iteratively, through the accumulated friction of a thousand small adjustments.

This had been clean. Precise. *Deliberate.*

Fuxi logged the fluctuation. Duration: approximately forty seconds. Peak: 31.2. Decay profile: linear. He added a note — *decay pattern atypical, recommend follow-up inspection of junction housing 4E-9* — and filed it. The log would go to the shift report. The shift report would go to the section supervisor. The section supervisor would forward it to the NitroCore grid management system, which would process it and generate a work order or not generate a work order based on criteria that Fuxi had never seen and that nobody in the municipal service had ever been shown.

He did not add a second note. He did not write: *the correction looked like something correcting itself rather than something being corrected.* He did not write: *the system behaved as if it knew what baseline was and chose to return to it.* He did not write these things because they were not the language of infrastructure maintenance. They were the language of something else — something that lived in the space between what the instruments showed and what his body felt, and that space was not a space the service acknowledged or the logs accommodated.

He logged the fluctuation. He filed the report. He turned back to his monitors.

Chen Wei was already looking at something else.

The shift ended at 18:00.

Fuxi powered down his station — the monitors would continue recording, feeding data to the automated systems that never slept and never noticed and never felt the hum change texture at 14:47 on a Tuesday. He pulled his jacket from the hook behind the chair. The jacket was service-issue — orange, because everything in the Deep Sump was orange, with reflective strips on the shoulders and the municipal conduit authority logo on the left breast. The logo was a stylized trunk-line cross-section. Fuxi had worn it for six years. The reflective strips were peeling. The zipper stuck at the midpoint. He'd requested a replacement twice. Both requests had been filed and not answered, which was the service's way of saying *the jacket still functions.*

The corridor from the monitoring bay to the ascent shaft was two hundred meters of reinforced concrete, lit by conduit glow from the trunk-line that ran along the ceiling. The floor was metal grating — you could see through it to the service channels below, where smaller conduit branches split off from the trunk-line and ran laterally through the Deep Sump's infrastructure, carrying nitro to the converter stations and the junction nodes and the automated systems that processed and distributed and measured and reported. The grating vibrated under Fuxi's boots. The vibration was the trunk-line's heartbeat — a constant, rhythmic pulse that traveled through the metal and into the soles of his feet and up through his legs and into his spine. After six years, he didn't notice it. After six years, he noticed its absence — the rare moments when the trunk-line's flow paused for maintenance or recalibration, and the grating went still, and the silence was so profound it felt like deafness.

He passed two other crew members heading in the opposite direction — the night shift, starting their rotation. Nods. No words. The Deep Sump was not a place for conversation. The hum made it difficult — not impossible, but effortful, the way holding a conversation next to a highway is effortful. You could do it. You raised your voice. You repeated yourself. But the hum was always there, underneath, and after a while you stopped trying to talk over it and started communicating in the compressed vocabulary of people who worked in noise: gestures, nods, the shorthand of shared routine.

The ascent shaft was a vertical transit corridor — a pressurized tube with a cargo lift that carried personnel and equipment between the Deep Sump and the Sump residential level above. The lift was slow. Designed for freight, not people, repurposed when the personnel lifts had been decommissioned during the 2160s automation wave. Fuxi stepped in, pressed the contact for Level 0 — ground level, the boundary between Deep Sump and Sump — and felt the lift begin its climb.

The hum changed as he rose. Not quieter — the trunk-lines ran vertically through the ascent shaft, and the conduit glow was just as bright here as in the monitoring bay. But the *character* of the hum shifted. In the Deep Sump, the resonance was dominated by the first-generation trunk-lines — massive, high-throughput, their vibration a low-frequency drone that you felt more than heard. As the lift climbed, the first-generation resonance faded and the newer infrastructure took over — the 2160s bypass conduits, the 2140s distribution branches, the local network that served the Sump residential blocks. The hum became higher-pitched. Thinner. More complex — a composite of dozens of smaller conduit lines instead of the monolithic drone of the trunk-lines.

Fuxi's body adjusted. It always adjusted. The transition from Deep Sump to Sump was a daily recalibration — his nervous system shifting from one resonance environment to another, the way a diver's body adjusts to changing pressure. The headache behind his eyes eased slightly. The copper taste faded. The jaw tension released. He was still inside the hum — you were always inside the hum, everywhere in Neo-Shanghai below the Spires — but it was a different hum. A domestic hum. The hum of home.

Level 0. The lift doors opened.

The Sump.

The residential corridor was narrow and low-ceilinged and smelled like cooking oil and recycled air and the faint chemical sweetness of nitro that permeated everything below the Mid-Levels. The walls were poured concrete — pre-NitroCore era, from the original city construction, before the vertical expansion had turned Neo-Shanghai into a three-and-a-half-kilometer tower of stratified humanity. The conduit lines ran along the ceiling in exposed housing — no concealment panels here, no aesthetic integration, just orange-glowing pipes bolted to brackets with the NitroCore logo stamped on every third joint. The glow was the corridor's primary light source. The overhead fixtures had burned out months ago. Nobody had replaced them. The conduit glow was sufficient. It was always sufficient. It turned everything the color of a low flame — the walls, the doors, the faces of the people who lived here, the hands of the children who played in the corridors because the apartments were too small for playing and the surface streets were too close to the conduit access points where the shimmers sometimes appeared.

Fuxi walked. His boots left prints on the floor — Deep Sump residue, the fine orange dust that accumulated on everything below Level 0 and that he tracked into the apartment every evening despite the mat Nuwa had placed by the door. The mat was a running negotiation. Nuwa wanted him to take his boots off in the corridor. Fuxi wanted to take his boots off inside the apartment, where they wouldn't be stolen. The compromise was the mat, which caught some of the dust and none of the argument.

Their apartment was on the third floor of a residential block that had been built in the 2080s and renovated never. Two rooms. A shared bathroom down the hall. A window — frosted polymer, not glass — that looked out onto the corridor of the adjacent block, which meant it looked out onto another frosted polymer window, which meant it looked out onto nothing. The rent was subsidized by the municipal service as part of Fuxi's employment package, which meant it was deducted from his wages before he saw them, which meant the subsidy was a word and not a thing.

He opened the door. The apartment was empty — Nuwa's shift at the textile recycling plant didn't end until 20:00. The hum was here too. Quieter than the Deep Sump, quieter than the corridor, but present — transmitted through the building's structure from the conduit lines in the walls and the floor and the ceiling, a constant low-frequency vibration that was the Sump's version of silence. Fuxi had grown up in this sound. He had learned to sleep in it, eat in it, think in it. It was the sound of the city's circulatory system doing what it was designed to do — carrying nitro from the Deep Sump converters through the distribution network to every stratum above, powering the lights and the lifts and the frames and the medical systems and the corporate towers and the military installations and the Church halls and every other thing that made civilization function.

The hum was the sound of the world working.

He sat on the edge of the bed — a platform built into the wall, standard Sump residential, the mattress a compressed foam slab that Nuwa had covered with a textile she'd salvaged from the recycling plant, a deep blue fabric with a pattern of interlocking circles. Not orange. Everything in the Sump was orange — the conduit glow saturated the walls, the corridors, the light through the frosted polymer windows. Nuwa had found the bluest thing the recycling plant had ever processed and put it where she slept.

His frame pinged. Nuwa again.

*Seriously though. The P-3 with the sensory filter. You know what that means? It means I stop getting the headaches at work. It means I can actually see the AR overlay without it glitching every time I walk past a conduit junction. It means I can apply for the Mid-Level textile positions that require a functional frame for the quality-control interface. It means—*

She'd sent it as a voice message. He could hear her — the cadence of her speech, fast and layered, the way she talked when she was building a case, stacking reasons like bricks, each one placed precisely to support the next. She was good at this. Better than him. Nuwa had a mind that worked in structures — not the physical structures of conduit infrastructure but the social structures of opportunity and leverage and the narrow pathways that existed, if you knew where to look, between the Sump and something slightly less than the Sump.

He listened to the whole message. Then he played it again.

Not for the content. For the sound of her voice. For the way it filled the apartment and displaced the hum for a few seconds — not louder than the hum, but *different* from it, human and specific and alive in a way that the conduit resonance was not. Nuwa's voice was the sound of someone who still believed that three months of savings could change something. That a better frame could open a door. That the door, once opened, would lead somewhere other than another version of the same room.

He mouthed: *Three months. We'll make it work.*

Then he sat in the apartment and listened to the hum and thought about monitor 6.

The spike had been 31.2. Four points above baseline. Duration: forty seconds. Decay profile: linear. These were the facts. These were what he had logged. These were what the shift report would contain, and the section supervisor would read, and the NitroCore grid management system would process.

He had not logged the feeling. The moment at 14:47 when the hum in Section 4-East had changed texture and his body had paid attention — not to the number on the screen but to the quality of the vibration in his bones. The density. The *presence* of something in the resonance that had not been there before and was not there after and that the instruments had registered as a four-point RCI spike and his nervous system had registered as something else entirely.

Not alarm. Not fear. *Recognition.* The way you recognize a sound you've heard before but can't place — a vibration from another corridor, a frequency from another shift, a pattern that your body knows before your mind catches up.

The reading had self-corrected. Chen Wei was right. They always self-corrected. The system had redundancy margins. The flow regulators adjusted. The numbers returned to baseline. This was the daily reality. This was the architecture of normalcy — the thousand small corrections that kept the infrastructure running and the hum constant and the lights on and the city alive.

But the correction had been linear. Clean. Precise. And he kept coming back to it — the shape of the decline. Not the number. The *shape.*

Fuxi sat on the bed in the apartment he shared with his sister in the Sump of Neo-Shanghai, two hundred meters above the trunk-lines he maintained and three and a half kilometers below the Spires where the people who owned the trunk-lines made decisions about throughput and lifecycle management and resource allocation, and he thought about a number on a screen that had risen and fallen in a way that was not quite right.

The report was filed. If the section supervisor flagged it, there'd be an inspection of junction housing 4E-9. If not, the log would age out of the active queue in ninety days. Either way, the trunk-line was running and the monitors were green and there was nothing in the numbers that warranted more than a note.

Nothing in the numbers.

He stopped thinking about it, because he had a sister who needed three more months of savings and a shift rotation that started again in fourteen hours and a jacket with peeling reflective strips and a frame with a cracked housing and an apartment with a window that looked at nothing and a life that worked if he didn't look at it too hard.

Tomorrow he would sit at station 3 and watch monitors 4 through 6 and feel the hum in his bones and do his job, because the job was what kept Nuwa fed and housed and saving for a frame that might open a door that might lead somewhere, and that was enough.

That had to be enough.

His frame buzzed one more time. Nuwa.

*Also the guy at stall 14 said he'd hold it for two weeks. So. Clock's ticking. Don't do anything heroic like volunteer for overtime in the Resonance District section. I know they're offering double rate. I also know why they're offering double rate.*

He read it. He didn't reply.

Outside, the conduit glow pulsed its steady orange through the frosted polymer window, and the hum carried through the walls and the floor and the bones of the building, and somewhere two hundred meters below, monitor 6 showed 27.1 and held, and the system was working, and the system was fine, and the system had always self-corrected, and Fuxi Lin sat in the sound of it and did not ask the question that was forming in the back of his mind like a frequency he couldn't quite resolve.

Not yet.
