## Draft v1

The pharmacist didn't look up.

That was the thing about the pharmacy — the one transaction in her life where the face didn't matter. The pharmacist saw the prescription. The pharmacist saw the insurance routing. The pharmacist saw a patient number and a medication list and dosages that told a story she would never ask about, because the story was none of her business and the medications were ready and the interaction was thirty seconds of professional silence in which Kira Calloway was not a streamer, not a brand, not a face that two hundred thousand people could identify from a clip thumbnail. She was a prescription history. She was upper-end dosages. She was antidepressants, antipsychotics, anxiolytics — the full architecture of keeping a brain running when the brain had opinions about whether it wanted to run — and the pharmacist handed her the bag and said "Have a good one" without looking up and Kira said "Thanks" and meant it more than anything she'd said on camera in months.

The bag was heavy the way it was always heavy. A month's worth of keeping the machinery below the threshold where it became unmanageable. She tucked it into her carry-on in the transit station and checked the time in her HUD — the pink scroll of the left eye, the ocular implant that never turned off — and the flight was in four hours and she had nothing to do with the four hours except not think about where she was going.

Neo-Shanghai. Academy promotional tour. Three days of panels and interviews and being the face of something she hadn't signed up for but couldn't refuse because the Academy had paid for the implant and the implant had made her career and the career had made the platform and the platform had made her famous and the fame had made her useful and the usefulness was the leash she'd agreed to when she was ten and didn't understand what she was agreeing to. The cycle. The system. The thing that kept turning whether you wanted it to or not.

Her HUD scrolled. Messages from the Academy liaison — schedule confirmations, media guidelines, wardrobe recommendations she would ignore. Beneath those, the feed: Neo-Shanghai RCI readings in the headlines again. Elevated. Sustained. Something about infrastructure assessments and public safety reassurances that used words like *nominal* and *within parameters* and *no cause for concern*, which she'd learned to read as *we are concerned but the sentence structure is designed to make you stop reading before you get to the part where we explain why.*

She didn't read them. She closed the feed. The hum sat in her spine — the R3 interface at the base of her fifth vertebra, the chord that had been tightening for weeks, aligning, each harmonic clarifying against the others — and it was doing what it always did, which was sitting there being a fact she couldn't turn off. In the Bay Area, the hum was background. Dense, layered, always present, but *background* — the way traffic noise becomes background when you've lived with it long enough. You stop hearing it. You start hearing through it.

She'd been hearing through it for ten years. She was very good at hearing through it.

The transit station hummed. The conduits beneath the platform hummed. She leaned against her carry-on and pressed two fingers to the back of her neck — the implant housing, the bone — and the hum was there the way it was always there, tighter than it had been a month ago, the intervals between harmonics narrowing, the chord resolving toward something she couldn't name. She dropped her hand. Filed it. Filed everything.

Four hours.

---

The plane landed and the hum changed.

Not gradually. Not the slow adjustment of walking between rooms with different acoustics. The moment the doors opened and the conduit network beneath Neo-Shanghai's tarmac connected to the ambient field her R3 had been carrying across the Pacific, the chord in her spine shifted — a half-step, maybe less, the kind of interval a trained ear catches and an untrained ear files as unease without knowing why. The harmonics didn't just tighten. They *intensified.* As if someone had taken the same chord she'd been carrying and doubled the gain, tripled it, layered it with overtones that hadn't existed in the Bay Area frequency space, and the R3 was parsing it all — every new harmonic, every additional density — and feeding it into her skeleton the way it had been feeding her the hum since she was ten.

Except this was not the Bay Area hum.

She stepped onto the jetway and her jaw tightened. Involuntary. The muscles responding to a signal her conscious mind hadn't caught up with yet — the body's early warning system, the flinch before the burn. The chord was *denser.* More resolved. The intervals she'd been feeling tighten over weeks had snapped into a precision that bordered on painful, each harmonic sitting in its exact frequency slot like instruments tuned to a standard she'd never heard before.

She walked through the terminal and smiled at the Academy liaison waiting at arrivals — a man in a pressed suit who was already talking, already routing her toward the car, already performing the institutional choreography of managing a mage who was also a celebrity who was also a promotional asset — and her face did the thing it always did: the reflex, the autopilot, the performer's smile that cost nothing except the constant expenditure of pretending the inside matched the outside.

"— and we've moved the evening panel to the smaller venue, capacity issues with the original space, but the morning session is confirmed, the media slots are at eleven and two, and we've got you in the Spire Meridian which is close to the convention center so transit shouldn't be—"

"Great," she said. "Sounds good." Three words. Enough. The liaison kept talking. She stopped listening.

The car moved through Neo-Shanghai and the hum moved with her.

The city was vertical in a way the Bay Area wasn't — Spires climbing into a skyline that made San Francisco's mid-rise spread look like it was lying down. The conduit infrastructure here was dense. She could feel it through the car floor, through the seat, through the architecture of her skeleton. The R3 was working harder than it had ever worked — parsing, filtering, the interface designed for Bay Area density now processing a signal environment that exceeded its calibration by an order she didn't have numbers for. She touched the back of her neck. The implant housing was warm. Not hot — warm. The way a machine runs warm when it's working at capacity.

Her left hand went flat on the seat.

Ring finger. Little. The dropout — two seconds, the nerve endings ceasing, the ulnar side of her hand going quiet. The absence she'd been filing for weeks as a data point she didn't want to think about. It came back. She made a fist. Opened it. The fingers worked.

She looked out the window and the city looked back and the hum held its new density and she was managing. She was always managing.

---

The panel was called *Mage Perspectives: Cultural Engagement in a Connected World.* She'd read the brief on the flight. She hadn't prepared remarks because she never prepared remarks because the performance was better unrehearsed — looser, more natural, the kind of authenticity that audiences trusted because it *was* authentic, at least on the surface, at least in the way a professional improviser's spontaneity is authentic even though the spontaneity is itself a practiced skill.

Six mages on stage. Moderator. Cameras. An audience of maybe four hundred — industry people, media, Academy brass in the front row. Kira in the second seat from the left, spine straight, shoulders open, the performer's posture she'd built over six years of being on camera. Split neon hair catching the stage lights. One blue eye, one pink. The face that two hundred thousand people thought they knew.

"Kira, you've been vocal about mage representation in streaming — can you talk about what that visibility means for the next generation of Academy graduates?"

She leaned into the mic. "I mean, honestly? It means they get to see someone who looks like them doing a job that doesn't involve standing in a field and hoping the void doesn't eat them." Laughter. The right amount. "I stream. I play games. I talk to chat. And yeah, I'm a mage, and the implant is part of my life, and I don't hide that — but I also don't make it the whole thing, because it's not the whole thing. It's one part. The job is being interesting for ten hours."

The audience liked that. They always liked that. The casual, the self-deprecating, the performer who seemed to be saying the thing she actually thought. She was good at it. She'd been good at it since she was fourteen. The skill was not saying the thing she actually thought — the skill was making the curated version feel indistinguishable from the raw one.

The hum spiked.

Not a scream. Not the full episode — not the bone-deep shriek that had been getting worse since the Bay Area. A spike. A sudden tightening of the chord, the harmonics squeezing into a register that sat between pain and pressure, and her jaw locked for half a second — maybe less, maybe a quarter-second, the kind of micro-expression that a camera might catch if the camera operator knew what to look for and the audience would miss because the audience always missed it.

Her right hand gripped the armrest. Her smile held. Her voice held.

"— and I think the Academy does a lot of good work in terms of outreach, but there's always more to do, right? There's always more to do."

The spike passed. The chord settled back to its new Neo-Shanghai density. The moderator moved to the next panelist. Kira released the armrest and put both hands in her lap where the camera couldn't see them and her left index finger was doing the thing it did when the hum was loud — tracing chord shapes against her thigh. D minor. A diminished. E-flat something. The shapes were evolving. She noticed that. She filed it.

The panel ran ninety minutes. She was on for maybe twenty of those minutes — the rest was other mages talking about cultural exchange programs and Academy initiatives and the kind of institutional language that made her want to check her HUD and see how long until she could leave. She didn't check. She sat in the performer's posture and smiled when appropriate and laughed when appropriate and the audience saw a young woman who was comfortable and sharp and exactly what the Academy's promotional material promised: a mage who had made it, who was thriving, who was proof that the system worked.

The system was the hum in her spine and the system was the smile on her face and neither system was working the way it was supposed to.

Backstage. The performer left.

It took longer than usual — the smile fading in stages, the posture collapsing, the shoulders curling forward, the spine releasing into the curve of a body that had been holding itself in a shape that wasn't its own. She stood in the green room with a bottle of water she wasn't drinking and her hands were shaking.

Her hands were shaking.

That was new. The fingertip dropouts — those she'd filed. The jaw tightening — filed. The chord shapes — filed. But the shaking was not a filing-compatible data point. The shaking was her hands, both of them, vibrating at a frequency she could feel in the small muscles of her forearms, a tremor that was subtle enough to miss from a distance and obvious enough at arm's length to require explanation. She put the water bottle down because she didn't trust herself to hold it without the tremor transferring to the surface of the water where it would become visible and visibility was the thing she managed and managed and managed.

She pressed both palms flat against the green room table. The surface was cold. The tremor didn't stop.

She stood there. Palms down. Head down. The split neon catching the fluorescent lights, blue and pink, the color palette she'd chosen at thirteen that had become the cage she performed inside. The hum held its Neo-Shanghai chord. The chord held its intervals. The intervals held something she could almost name.

A handler knocked. "Car's ready, Ms. Calloway."

"One minute."

She gave it thirty seconds. Straightened her spine. Opened her shoulders. Put the performer back on — not all the way, not the full broadcast persona, just enough to walk through a lobby and into a car without anyone seeing what the backstage had looked like.

---

The hotel room was on the thirty-second floor. She felt every floor on the way up.

She felt it in the hum. The R3 tracked altitude the way it tracked everything: passively, constantly, feeding her data she hadn't asked for about the density of the conduit network beneath the building and the ambient coherence field and the distance between her spine and the infrastructure that the city was built on top of. In the Bay Area, thirty-two floors would have been unremarkable. Here, thirty-two floors put her closer to the Spire-level conduit grid — the high-throughput trunk-lines that ran through the upper architecture of Neo-Shanghai like arteries through a body — and the hum responded. Tightened. Clarified. Each floor a half-step up on a scale she couldn't read but couldn't stop hearing.

The room was large and impersonal and smelled like climate control. She dropped her bag, kicked off her shoes, and stood in the entryway with her eyes closed the way she stood in every new room: assessing. Not the décor. Not the layout. The hum. What the hum was doing in this particular cubic volume of air at this particular altitude in this particular city.

It was doing something she hadn't felt before.

The chord — the same chord she'd been carrying since the Bay Area, the one that had been tightening and aligning and clarifying for weeks — was no longer *tight.* It was *articulate.* Each harmonic sat in its interval with a definition that felt less like resonance and more like — she didn't have the word yet. Less like the ambient vibration of infrastructure and more like something that had been *organized.* Not random. Not noise. Not the background hum of a city running on nitro. Something that had structure the way language has structure: not because someone built it but because it was *trying to mean something.*

She opened her eyes. Crossed to the bed. Sat on the edge.

Pulled the pharmacy bag from her carry-on.

The pills. The ritual. The thing her hands needed to do when her mind was running too fast to hold. She unzipped the matte black case — not the Lumina kit, the other one, the one that didn't live behind a bathroom mirror. The 30-day holder. The bottles. She lined them up on the hotel bedspread with the precision of a person performing a ceremony she'd performed a hundred times: antidepressants first, the big capsules, one per compartment, thirty compartments, click click click. Then the antipsychotics — smaller, round, a different sound against the plastic. Then the anxiolytics. Then the rest. The full daily stack. Upper-end dosages, every one of them, because her brain had been running hot since before the implant and the implant had not helped and the dosages had climbed the way dosages climb when the machinery keeps finding new ways to malfunction.

Click. Click. Click.

Her hands steadied. That was the point. The hands needed to work. The sorting was repetitive and precise and demanded just enough attention to keep the pattern-seeking machinery from eating itself — the racing thoughts, the branching what-ifs, the cognitive engine that ran at redline whether she wanted it to or not. The Lumina turned it off. The pills kept it from destroying her. The sorting gave it something to do that wasn't catastrophizing.

She worked through the bottles. The hum held. Neo-Shanghai's chord sat in her spine like a guest who'd arrived uninvited and was making itself comfortable. She could feel it more clearly now — sitting still, in silence, without the panel noise and the backstage adrenaline and the performer's architecture consuming her attention. The chord was doing something she'd never felt it do in the Bay Area.

It was *phrasing.*

The way a musical phrase resolves: tension, direction, the sense that the notes are going *somewhere.* The harmonics in her spine were not just sitting in their intervals. They were moving. Slow, almost imperceptible, the kind of motion you'd miss if you weren't sitting still on a hotel bed clicking pills into compartments with your hands barely steady and your mind running the analysis it had been trying to run since the plane landed.

She pressed two fingers to the back of her neck. The implant housing was warmer than it had been at the transit station. The R3 was working. Working hard. Parsing a signal environment that was denser and more structured than anything it had been calibrated for.

*Structured.*

There it was. The word she'd been circling. The hum in Neo-Shanghai was not just louder or denser or more intense. It was *structured.* It had architecture. It had the quality of something that had been organized — not by the conduit network, not by the infrastructure, not by the engineering that kept the nitro flowing, but by something *in* the signal that was using the infrastructure the way sound uses air: as a medium. Not the source. The medium.

She finished the pill sorting. Closed the 30-day holder. Sat with it in her lap.

The tremor was still there. Fainter now — the sorting had helped, the rhythm had helped, the ritual of putting the medications in their compartments had given her body a shape to hold that wasn't the performer's shape and wasn't the collapsed shape and was just her hands doing a thing they knew how to do. But the tremor was there. In the fingertips. In the small muscles. A vibration that was hers and wasn't hers and she couldn't tell which part was the shaking and which part was the hum and the inability to tell the difference was a data point she could not file.

She put the holder on the nightstand. Put the bottles back in the bag. Sat on the bed in the impersonal room in a city she'd never been to with a chord in her spine that was doing something it had never done and her hands were shaking and the shaking wouldn't stop and the hum was structured and she was twenty years old and she was sitting on a hotel bed in Neo-Shanghai with a month of psychiatric medication on the nightstand and a sound in her bones that felt like it was trying to say something.

She pulled up the mage group on her HUD and opened the gesture board — the projected keys hovering at waist height, visible only through the pink overlay of her left eye.

The channel scrolled. The private channel she'd been part of since she'd replied to those four messages weeks ago and become a node in a network she hadn't meant to join. Ren. Lena. Yuki. Priya. Dex and Tomás and Suki in the broader group. Others since — mages who'd heard she might understand, who'd reached out the way the first four had, who'd found their way to a channel that the Academy's Section 14.3 said they shouldn't be using to discuss the thing they were all discussing.

The last message was three hours old. Lena, from Lagos: "Anyone getting the new readings? Infrastructure upgrades they said. Sure."

Kira typed. Deleted. Typed again.

She'd done this before — the typing and deleting, the hovering, the moment between having the thought and releasing it into a space where other people could see it. She'd done it weeks ago when she'd replied "I felt it." Three words that had changed her from an audience to a participant. This was different. This was not confirmation. This was a claim.

She looked at her hands. The tremor had settled into something she could almost ignore if she kept them flat on the bed. The pill holder sat on the nightstand. The bag from the pharmacy sat on the floor. Outside, Neo-Shanghai's skyline pulsed with the conduit grid's light — faint, pervasive, the glow of a city that ran on the same infrastructure her implant was tuned to.

She typed it.

*It's louder here. Much louder. Does anyone else feel like it's words?*

She looked at the message. Twelve words. The cursor blinking at the end. The word *words* sitting there in the text field like a grenade she hadn't thrown yet.

It was not a scientific observation. It was not data. It was not the kind of careful, hedged, Section-14.3-compliant language the Academy would approve of. It was Kira — impulsive, direct, the pattern-seeking machinery finally producing a pattern she couldn't not report. The hum in Neo-Shanghai was structured. The structure felt like it was trying to communicate. And the word for things that try to communicate is *words* and she knew this would change things and she knew she should delete it and sit on it the way she sat on everything and file it and manage it and be the performer who didn't say the thing she actually thought.

She sent it.

The message appeared in the channel. Her words on other people's screens. Ren in Kyoto. Lena in Lagos. Yuki in Osaka. Priya wherever Priya was. The network that the Academy said shouldn't exist receiving a message that the Academy said shouldn't be sent from a mage who was supposed to be doing promotional work and was instead sitting on a hotel bed in the city where the hum was loudest with her medication on the nightstand and her hands not quite steady and a question in a group chat that she could not take back.

Three dots appeared. Someone was typing.

She closed her eyes. The hum held. Neo-Shanghai's chord carried its new structure through her spine, through the bone, through the R3 interface that the Academy had fitted when she was ten and that was now receiving a signal that no one had built it to receive and no one had trained her to interpret and no one at the Academy was willing to acknowledge existed.

*Words.*

She'd said it. It was out there. The pattern-seeking machinery had done what pattern-seeking machinery does — it had found the pattern, and the pattern was not noise and was not feedback and was not the "sensory adjustment period" the Academy's travel advisory had promised would resolve in 48 to 72 hours. The pattern was structured. The structure was directional. And the direction felt, in the bone, like something reaching.

She didn't know toward what. She didn't need to know yet.

She opened her eyes. The three dots were still there. Someone was composing a reply. The question was in the world now, occupying space in a network that was growing because the thing it was growing around was real, and the realness was the thing the Academy couldn't manage and Kira couldn't file and neither system — the institutional one or the personal one — had a protocol for what happened when a twenty-year-old mage sat in a hotel room and felt the infrastructure of a city trying to speak.

She pulled her knees up. Made herself small. The performer was gone. The filer was gone. She was just Kira, on a bed, in a room, in a city that hummed at a frequency she could almost understand, waiting for someone to tell her she wasn't the only one who heard it.

The three dots resolved into a message.

She read it.

She wasn't.
