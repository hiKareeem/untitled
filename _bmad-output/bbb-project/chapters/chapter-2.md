## Draft v1

The apartment smelled like solder flux and boiled greens. Mirelle Dubois sat on a stool that had been designed for a child and held a cup of tea she would not drink.

The woman across from her — Lien Suen, thirty-one, textile recycling, second shift — hadn't sat down. She stood at the kitchen counter with her back to the window, her hands working a piece of scrap fabric into a knot and then smoothing it flat and then knotting it again. The window behind her was frosted polymer, one corner patched with packing tape. Through it, the orange glow of the nearest conduit line cast everything in the room the color of old copper.

"He left for his shift," Lien said. "Same time. Same route."

Mirelle didn't write anything down. Not yet. The recorder in her jacket pocket was running — audio only, no visual, nothing that pinged the mesh. She'd learned this in her first weeks on the Sump beat: you write in front of people, they edit themselves. You record and they forget. And if you're recording on hardware that doesn't touch NitroCore's network, what they forget stays forgotten by anyone who might care.

"What time?"

"Fifth hour. Always fifth hour. The shift starts at sixth but he walks slow — his knee, the cold." Lien smoothed the fabric flat. "He takes the corridor past the trunk-line access on Level 3. Fifteen minutes. He's done it every day for nine years."

The hum was in the walls. Not loud — it was never loud in a residential block this far from the main conduit arteries. But it was there, the way pressure was there. A low, sustained vibration that Mirelle could feel in her molars if she clenched her jaw. She'd spent five months in the Sump on and off, and the hum had stopped being something she noticed and started being something she noticed *not noticing.* The residents didn't hear it at all. The residents heard silence when the hum stopped, the way you hear silence when a refrigerator cycles off — the absence louder than the presence.

"And the morning he didn't come home," Mirelle said. "Walk me through it."

Lien's hands stopped. The fabric lay flat between them, smoothed to nothing.

"I didn't know until eighth hour. Mei was — my daughter was sick, so I kept her from school, and I was late for my own shift, and I didn't check until—" She pressed her lips together. "He didn't ping. He always pings when he clocks in. So I checked the corridor log. It shows him entering the Level 3 access corridor at fifth hour, eleven minutes. It doesn't show him leaving."

"The log is NitroCore's system?"

"Municipal. But NitroCore runs it."

Mirelle nodded. She knew this. She knew more about the Sump's surveillance architecture than most of the people who lived inside it — which corridors had live feeds, which had been downgraded to motion-detect, which junction access points had been reclassified from "public maintenance" to "restricted" in the last fiscal quarter without public notice. She'd mapped it. Not for publication. For the private file, the one that lived on a burner drive in the lining of her coat, encrypted with a key she changed weekly.

"You went to the corridor," she said.

"Not until ninth hour. I had Mei. I couldn't — I asked Zhou, from downstairs, to check. He works sanitation on Level 2. He walked the corridor."

"And?"

Lien picked up the fabric again. Twisted it.

"He said the corridor was empty. The trunk-line access hatch was closed. Sealed. Standard. No sign of — nothing wrong." She paused. "But he said the air smelled wrong. Not the usual. Not the flux-and-copper you get near the junction. He said it smelled like — cold. Like the air itself had a temperature that wasn't the same as the walls."

Mirelle's chest tightened. Not visibly — she'd trained the reaction out of her face years ago, the way you train a dog not to flinch at thunder. But inside, the pattern recognition was firing. *Cold air near conduit infrastructure.* She'd heard this before. Three times in five months, from three different sources, in three different Sump sectors. None of them connected. None of them published. None of them provable.

"Did he see anything?"

"He said—" Lien looked at the window. The orange light caught the moisture in her eyes. "He said there was a shimmer. Near the hatch. Not like light — like the air was *wrong.* Like looking through water that wasn't there. He said it was small. Fist-sized. He said it was gone by the time he blinked."

*Shimmer.* The Sump word. Everyone in the Sump had seen one, or said they had, or knew someone who had. Minor void anomalies — the official classification was VPI-0, sub-threshold, no intervention required. The VEC logged them when they were reported, which they rarely were, because reporting meant paperwork and paperwork meant attention and attention in the Sump was never the kind you wanted.

"Did Zhou report it?"

"He doesn't report things. Nobody reports things." Lien's voice flattened. The fabric in her hands had gone still again. "I went to NitroCore's local office. I filed a missing-worker claim. They told me to check the Feed."

"The Feed."

"The Feed says relocated. It says Dao was reassigned to a maintenance crew in Sector 12 and that his transfer was — what's the word—"

"Authorized."

"Authorized. Effective immediately. No disruption expected." She looked at Mirelle for the first time since she'd started speaking. Her eyes were dry now. Steady. The grief underneath was the kind that had gone past tears into something harder and more permanent, like scar tissue forming over a wound that hadn't been cleaned. "He didn't transfer. He didn't pack a bag. His tools are in the closet. His boots are by the door. He walked to work and the corridor ate him and the Feed says *relocated.*"

Mirelle let the silence hold. Three seconds. Five. Long enough for the hum to fill the space between them — the conduit vibration through the walls, the low-frequency pulse of infrastructure that never slept because infrastructure didn't have that luxury.

"Has anyone else gone missing from this block?"

Lien's jaw tightened. A tell — not the question she'd expected. Not the *how are you holding up* or *is there family you can stay with* that the social workers asked, the ones who came once and never came back.

"Chen Yue. Fourth floor. Three weeks before Dao."

"The Feed?"

"Relocated."

---

The corridor on Level 3 was narrow, low-ceilinged, and lit by a strip of amber conduit-glow that ran along the left wall like a vein. The hum was louder here — not painful, not yet, but present in a way that made Mirelle's teeth ache. She walked with her hands in her pockets, the left one curled around the borrowed RCI sensor — a palm-sized disc, industrial surplus, calibrated by a contact at the Chén Academy who owed her a favor and didn't ask what it was for.

The trunk-line access hatch was thirty meters from the residential corridor junction. Standard NitroCore hardware — reinforced steel, biometric lock, the corporate logo stamped into the upper-left corner in that particular shade of orange that was either branding or a side effect of the nitro that flowed behind it. The hatch was closed. Sealed. No visible damage, no residue, no sign that anything had happened here nine days ago that was different from what happened here every other day for the last nine years of Dao Suen's shifts.

She held the RCI sensor against the wall beside the hatch. The disc hummed in her palm — sympathetic vibration, the sensor's element resonating with the conduit field. The reading climbed. Fourteen. Fifteen. Seventeen.

She frowned. Baseline for a residential Sump stratum at this distance from a trunk-line should be fourteen to sixteen. Seventeen was elevated but within variance.

She moved closer to the hatch. Eighteen. Eighteen point six.

She pressed the sensor against the hatch itself.

Nineteen point four.

She held it there for ten seconds, watching the decimal hold steady. Not a spike — a sustained elevation. The kind that would show up in a quarterly infrastructure report as "within tolerance" because the tolerance bands had been widened three times in the last decade to accommodate readings that kept climbing.

She pulled the sensor back and slipped it into her pocket.

The wall to the right of the hatch was poured concrete — old, pre-NitroCore, from the era when the Sump was the city's ground level and not its basement. Someone had written on it in chalk. White, blocky, the letters uneven but deliberate:

**DAO SUEN**

Below it, older, half-faded:

**CHEN YUE**

Below that, older still, barely legible:

A name she couldn't read. Three characters, maybe four. The chalk had been rained on, or wept on, or simply left to the patient erasure of humidity and time.

She photographed the wall with the camera she kept in her breast pocket — dedicated hardware, no mesh connection, no metadata, no frame integration. The image would live on the burner drive with everything else. She photographed the hatch, the corridor, the ambient glow. She did not photograph the small bundle of dried flowers someone had left at the base of the wall beneath the names. She wanted to. She didn't. Some things were evidence and some things were grief and she was not yet certain she knew which was which.

She stood in the corridor and listened. The hum. The distant rattle of a ventilation unit. The sound of the city's plumbing doing what plumbing did — moving fluid, maintaining pressure, carrying the substance that kept the lights on in the Spires three kilometers above her head. The Spires, where the hum was scrubbed to nothing. Where the air was filtered and the temperature was perfect and the closest anyone came to a conduit was the ambient glow of the AR display on their frame.

She'd been up there once. Press pool for some corporate function — controlled access, sanitized air. She'd felt lighter. Not because of the altitude but because of the absence. No hum. No vibration in her jaw. No orange glow. Three hundred people in formalwear who had never heard the sound that followed Dao Suen to work every morning. *They don't know. They don't know because they can't hear it.*

She turned from the wall and walked back toward the residential junction.

---

Gauthier picked up on the third ping.

"Mirelle." His voice was Paris-clean — no hum bleed, no background rattle, eight time zones and a continent away from the sound in her teeth. She could hear the soft pulse of his environmental system in the background. Temperature-controlled air. The Wire's Paris bureau had been chosen for exactly this kind of distance — close enough to the European Accords infrastructure to matter, far enough from any Sump to forget.

"I have another one."

"Another what?"

"Missing worker. Sump, Sector 4. Conduit maintenance tech, NitroCore municipal contract. Nine days ago. The Feed says relocated."

A pause. She could hear him breathing. Gauthier was a careful man — not cautious, careful. The distinction mattered. Cautious people avoided risk. Careful people evaluated it. Gauthier evaluated it the way a surgeon evaluated an incision: not whether to cut, but where, how deep, and what you were willing to lose if the blade slipped.

"The family?"

"Wife. Lien Suen. He left for his shift, walked into the trunk-line access corridor on Level 3, corridor log shows entry, no exit. NitroCore says transfer authorized."

"And the wife says?"

"He didn't pack. His tools are in the closet. She mentioned a shimmer near the hatch."

"A shimmer."

"Her neighbor saw it. Sub-threshold, probably VPI-0. Gone before he could confirm."

"Mirelle." The tone shifted. Not dismissal — something gentler, which was worse. "One missing worker and a shimmer a neighbor might have seen. That's—"

"There's a second. Same building. Three weeks earlier. Chen Yue. Also relocated per the Feed."

Silence. She heard him shift in his chair — the creak of good furniture, the kind the Paris bureau provided, the kind that adjusted to your posture and logged your stress levels and sent the data to HR.

"Two," he said.

"Two from the same block, same proximity to the same junction. And there are names on the wall, Gauthier. Chalk. Dao Suen, Chen Yue, and at least one more I can't read. Workers write the names of the disappeared near where they vanished. It's—"

"Folklore."

"It's *documentation.* It's the only documentation these people have because the Feed says relocated and NitroCore says authorized and nobody is counting."

"We count. That's what we do. But we count with evidence, not chalk." He wasn't wrong. That was the worst part. He was applying the standard — The Wire's standard, the one that separated them from the Feed's algorithmic noise, from the Lens rumor mills, from the conspiracy theorists on the underground mesh. Verification. Confirmation. A second source that wasn't a chalk name on a wall. "What do you actually have?"

She closed her eyes. Translated from instinct to inventory — the shift from Mirelle-the-hunter to Mirelle-the-professional, the one who filed clean copy and sourced every claim and never published a word she couldn't stand behind in front of a libel tribunal.

"One confirmed missing worker, corridor log entry with no exit, NitroCore press release contradicting the family's account. One corroborating disappearance from the same block, same Feed classification. Chalk names near the site — at least three. Elevated RCI reading near the access hatch: nineteen point four, baseline should be fourteen to sixteen."

"You have an RCI sensor?"

"Borrowed."

"Calibrated?"

"Yes."

"By whom?"

She didn't answer. Source protection was reflex, not strategy.

"Mirelle." His voice was even. Patient. The voice of a man who had edited conflict journalism for twenty years and understood that the distance between a hunch and a story was measured in dead sources and retracted headlines. "You have one family's testimony, one anecdotal shimmer report, and an RCI reading from an uncertified instrument operated by a journalist, not a technician. The NitroCore statement says reassigned. We have no body, no breach report, no VEC confirmation. What am I supposed to do with this?"

"Run it."

"As what? 'One Sump Worker Missing, Corporate Records Say Otherwise'? On what desk does that land? Who picks it up? Who follows up?"

"I follow up."

"You've been following up for five months. You have six missing workers across three sectors. No two are connected by anything except geography and your conviction that they should be."

*Seven,* she thought. *Dao makes seven.* But she didn't correct him. She didn't need to win the argument. She needed to end the call and go back to the corridor and stand near the chalk names and think about what she was actually looking at.

"Give me two more weeks."

"For what?"

"A pattern. Two more weeks of cross-referencing the disappearances with NitroCore's infrastructure reports. If the missing workers cluster around the same trunk-line junctions, that's not anecdotal. That's geographic correlation."

She heard him exhale. Not a sigh — an assessment. Gauthier didn't sigh. He calculated.

"Two weeks. And Mirelle — don't go below Level 4 without checking in. The Sump beat doesn't pay enough for me to replace you."

"It doesn't pay enough for me to stay, either."

"And yet."

"And yet."

She cut the connection.

---

The Sump at shift change was a river. Not metaphorically — the flow of bodies through the narrow transit corridors had the same hydraulic logic as the conduit network beneath it. Pressure differentials. Bottlenecks at junction stairs where the infrastructure narrowed. Eddies in the wider spaces where market stalls and food vendors and prayer niches created obstacles the current had to route around. The orange glow of the conduit lines striped everything in bands of amber and shadow, and the hum was louder here, out in the open, where the insulation between residential walls and the main arteries was thinner and the sound bled through like heat through old glass.

Mirelle moved with the current. She was good at this — at being a body in a flow, unremarkable, her frame running the same firmware as everyone else's visible layer while the private partition underneath recorded, cataloged, geotagged. Her clothes were Sump-appropriate: work jacket, secondhand boots, a scarf that was textile recycling from a vendor two blocks from Lien's building. She did not look like a journalist. She did not look like anything. That was the skill — not disguise but *legibility.* She read as Sump because she'd learned Sump the way she'd learned every other register in her career: by listening until the grammar was instinct.

A feed-screen on the transit wall scrolled updates. Weather — controlled, always controlled, the atmospheric processors above the brightline keeping the temperature at civic-mandated levels that had nothing to do with comfort and everything to do with conduit efficiency. Transit advisories — Sector 6 interchange running at 80% capacity, expect delays. Employment listings — NitroCore Municipal, conduit maintenance, Level 2–4, immediate start, credential tier Basic, housing not included.

And there, between the transit advisory and the employment listing, in the same font, at the same scroll speed, with the same algorithmic indifference:

**PERSONNEL UPDATE: Junction 7 maintenance crew restructured. All personnel reassigned. No disruption to service expected. — NitroCore Sump Division**

She stopped walking. The current split around her — bodies routing past the obstacle she'd become, the way the current routes past a stone in a river. Nobody else stopped. Nobody else read the update as anything other than what it was: administrative noise, the background hum of institutional language that the Feed produced the way the conduits produced heat. Constant. Ignorable. Designed to be.

*All personnel reassigned.*

Not *one* worker. *All.* The entire Junction 7 maintenance crew. She hadn't known that. Lien hadn't mentioned it — why would she? Lien knew about Dao. Lien knew about Chen Yue. She didn't know about the rest of Junction 7's crew because the Feed had told her what happened to them and the Feed's answer was *reassigned* and in the Sump, when the Feed said something, you either believed it or you didn't, but either way you went to work because the alternative was losing your credential and losing your credential meant losing your housing and losing your housing in the Sump meant the only thing below you was the Deep Sump and nobody went to the Deep Sump by choice.

Mirelle started walking again. The current absorbed her.

She thought about the chalk names on the wall. Three names, maybe more. Dao Suen. Chen Yue. The one she couldn't read. How many names would fit on that wall? How long before the rain and the humidity dissolved them into the concrete and someone wrote new ones?

She thought about the RCI reading. Nineteen point four. Not alarming. Not reportable. But she had readings from the other sectors too — the other six missing workers, the other corridors, the other trunk-line access points. None of them alarming individually. All of them above baseline. A pattern that existed only if you looked at all of them together, which nobody did, because the data lived in six different municipal databases managed by three different NitroCore subcontractors and the only person who had cross-referenced them was Mirelle Dubois, freelance, The Wire, Sump beat, working out of a rented room in Mid-Levels District 5 with a borrowed sensor and a burner drive and a conviction that Gauthier would call a hunch.

She thought about the NitroCore press release. *All Junction 7 personnel reassigned.* Not missing. Not absorbed. Not investigated. *Reassigned.* The word did the work — it moved the people from one category to another without passing through the space between, the space where questions lived, where a wife might say *his boots are by the door* and a neighbor might say *the air smelled cold* and a chalk name might say *I was here and then I wasn't.*

There was a phrase for what was happening here. She didn't have it yet. But she could feel its shape — the outline of a system that had already categorized these people, these disappearances, these chalk names as *within tolerance.* The tolerance bands had been widened. The baseline had been raised. The Feed said relocated.

She took the transit stairs to Level 1 and emerged into the narrow canyon of the Sump's main commercial street. The brightline was visible from here — the sharp division between Sump-orange and Mid-Level-white, the boundary where the conduit glow gave way to filtered light, where the hum dropped below the threshold of casual perception and the people above it could pretend the sound had never existed.

She looked up at the brightline. Three kilometers above that, the Spires. The towers where the air tasted like nothing and the hum was someone else's problem.

The hum was in her teeth.

She didn't file the story. She didn't have the story yet. She had seven missing workers, three sectors, elevated RCI readings, one shimmer report, one NitroCore press release that contradicted a widow's testimony, and chalk names on a wall that nobody was counting.

She would count them.

She turned from the brightline and walked back into the Sump, and the current took her, and the hum followed, and the Feed scrolled on above her head — *no disruption to service expected* — and Dao Suen's name was still written in chalk on a wall near Junction 7, where the air smelled like cold and the reading was nineteen point four and the tolerance band was wide enough to hold a man's disappearance without anyone having to call it what it was.
