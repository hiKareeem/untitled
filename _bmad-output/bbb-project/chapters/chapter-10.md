## Draft v1

The Bay was loud at sundown.

Not the drone of deep infrastructure — not the geological hum that cities were built on top of and most people never noticed. This was surface noise. Human noise. The clatter of a transit hub at shift change, voices in four languages, the ambient wash of a city that ran on nitro and never stopped running. The conduits here were buried deep enough that you couldn't feel them through the sidewalk — not unless you had an R3 spinal implant tuned to the resonance, in which case you felt them always, through the concrete, through your shoes, through the architecture of your skeleton into the place where the interface sat against the bone and hummed.

Kira felt them always.

The fog was rolling in through the Gate — the gap where the old bridge stood preserved against the sky, a monument to something that no longer existed beneath it. The bay was wider now than the old maps showed. The seawall curved south past the transit hub toward where San Jose used to be, before the water came. The skyline was low and wide. No Spires. Just the mid-rise arc of a city built to spread rather than climb.

She walked the six blocks from the transit station to her building with her hood up and her head down, which didn't work, because she was 5'4" with neon hair that shimmered even under a hood and a face that two hundred thousand people could identify from a clip thumbnail. A kid at the crosswalk looked at her, looked at his frame, looked back at her. She smiled — the reflex, the autopilot, the performer's muscle memory — and he grinned and she kept walking and the interaction was over in three seconds and cost her nothing except the reminder that she could not disappear.

She used to try. The first year after the rebranding, when the numbers started climbing and the recognition started happening outside of streams, she'd tried hats, she'd tried different routes, she'd tried timing her errands for hours when the streets were empty. None of it worked. The hair was the brand and the brand was the hair and the audience had parasocially bonded with a color palette she'd chosen at thirteen. She'd stopped trying. Smiled when recognized. Kept walking. The cost was so small it didn't register as a cost until you added up every three-second interaction across every day and realized the sum was a life lived in permanent soft performance.

Her building was mid-rise, unremarkable — the kind of housing that Bay Area professionals occupied when they made good money but not Spire money. Fourteenth floor. The elevator hummed. Everything hummed. She leaned against the wall and felt the building's vibration merge with the vibration in her spine and for a moment couldn't tell where the infrastructure ended and she began, which was either a metaphor or a diagnosis, and the elevator arrived before she had to decide.

The apartment door opened to biometric. She stepped inside, dropped her bag on the floor next to yesterday's bag, and stood in the entryway for a moment with her eyes closed. The performer hadn't switched on yet. The stream was in — she checked the time in her HUD — forty-three minutes. Forty-three minutes of being nobody.

She crossed to the bathroom. Washed her face. Changed into the stream outfit — the one that looked effortless, because she'd spent two hours in a store making sure it looked effortless. Looked at herself in the mirror — split neon hair, one blue eye, one pink, the face that two hundred thousand people thought they knew — and the face looked back and it was fine. It was always fine.

Behind the mirror, in the cabinet, the kit was there. Matte black case, zippered. She didn't open it. Not before a stream. That was the line — the one line she'd drawn when the Lumina started working well enough to become a habit, the hard bright line between *professional* and *dependent*: never at work. She could feel the noise in her head — the racing thoughts, the branching what-ifs, the pattern-seeking machinery that never stopped — and she let it run. She'd stream through it the way she always streamed through it. Raw. The audience wouldn't know the difference. They never did.

Thirty-eight minutes.

---

The apartment was a composition in two keys.

Camera-left: ring light, acoustic panels in matte grey, the streaming chair with its ergonomic armrests and lumbar support that she'd paid real money for because her spine needed it, though she told her audience she just liked the brand. Three monitors arranged in a shallow arc — the center for gameplay, the left for chat, the right for her production overlay. LED strip along the desk edge, set to a soft gradient that matched her hair. Blue fading into pink. The color temperature was calibrated. The framing was calibrated. The throw pillows on the background shelf were calibrated — she'd spent forty minutes arranging them three years ago and hadn't touched them since except to dust.

Camera-right: everything else.

Pizza box from two days ago, lid open, one slice fossilized. A glass of something she'd poured yesterday and forgotten. Laundry — not dirty exactly, not clean exactly, occupying the liminal space between the hamper and the couch where clothes went to await a decision she kept deferring. The kitchen was around the corner and she hadn't looked at it today because looking at it meant dealing with it and dealing with it meant the ants and the ants meant she'd have to clean and cleaning meant she wasn't streaming and not streaming meant —

She touched the back of her neck. The hum was there. Always there. A low sustained note that had lived in her spine since she was ten years old, so constant and so old that she'd stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. Except lately. Lately the note had changed — not louder, not different in pitch, but *thicker*. As if a single tone had begun to split into harmonics she couldn't quite resolve. Like a chord played on a piano with the sustain pedal down, the overtones bleeding into each other until the original note was buried somewhere underneath.

She dropped her hand. Rolled her shoulders. Pulled her hair forward over her left eye — the pink one, the implant, the HUD that was already scrolling pre-stream notifications across her peripheral vision — and then swept it back again. Habit. The hair shimmered under the ring light. Neon blue on the left, neon pink on the right. Synthetic, augmented, chosen at thirteen by a girl who didn't understand she was building a cage. She'd change it if she could. She couldn't. The audience had opinions about the hair.

The audience had opinions about everything.

She straightened in the chair. Spine aligned. Shoulders open. Chin level. The ballerina posture that was built into her muscle memory from years of choreography that had nothing to do with dance and everything to do with looking like a person who was fine. She was fine. She was always fine. She'd been fine on camera since she was fourteen and the fineness was so deeply rehearsed that she sometimes forgot it was a performance, which was either healthy adaptation or complete dissolution of self, and she didn't have time to think about which because the stream was starting in two minutes.

*Going live in 120.*

She posted it. Watched the notification ripple through the feed — the subscriber pings, the repost bots, the preview-clip generators that would pull the first thirty seconds of her stream and distribute them across platforms she didn't control. Two hundred thousand followers. Give or take. The number fluctuated. She'd stopped checking the exact count six months ago because the checking had become compulsive and the compulsion had become the kind of thing you notice about yourself at three in the morning and then can't stop noticing.

Her left eye — the implant — pulsed a soft pink. Chat was populating. The early arrivals. The ones who set alerts.

*kiracalloway is live!*

She smiled. The smile was real. That was the dangerous part — it was *real*, every time, the hit of going live, the rush of the room filling with voices, the sense that she existed because they were watching. She knew what that meant. She knew what that said about her. She smiled anyway.

"Hey. Hi. Hello. Welcome. Chat's moving — I see you, I see you. Hi. Okay." She leaned into the mic. "We're doing a chill one tonight. I've got — what do I have. I have leftover pizza that I'm not going to eat on camera because last time I did that someone clipped it and it became a meme and I am *still* getting tagged in that. I have a game I've been meaning to finish for three weeks. And I have —" she glanced at chat "— yeah, I have *opinions*, apparently. Chat's already fighting. Love that for us. We're seventeen seconds in and chat's already fighting."

The room filled. Viewer count climbed. She watched it in her peripheral vision — the implant overlaying the number in soft pink text at the edge of her HUD, ticking upward like a pulse monitor. She didn't look at it directly. Looking at it directly was acknowledging that it mattered. It mattered.

She loaded the game. Something open-world, half-explored, the kind of thing she picked up and put down between projects — a vast procedurally generated landscape that she navigated with the same distracted competence she applied to everything. The game was not the point. The game was the scaffolding. The game filled the microseconds between chat messages, gave her hands something to do while her mouth did the work and her left eye tracked the feed and her right eye — the real one, the blue one — watched the gameplay without really seeing it.

"Okay so I went left last time and died immediately, so tonight we're going — chat, if you say left I'm banning all of you. I'm not even kidding. Left is *dead* to me. Left is where dreams go to — okay three people already said left. Three of you are going to jail."

She played. She talked. She read chat and responded to the messages that caught her attention — the funny ones, the sharp ones, the ones that gave her something to riff on. This was the rhythm. This was what she was good at. The stream was a live performance and the performance was proximity — the audience felt close to her because she let them in, she responded, she reacted, she was *present* in a way that felt unscripted because it was unscripted, mostly, except for the parts that were so deeply practiced they'd become invisible.

Forty minutes in. Viewer count steady. Chat flowing. The game was ambient — she'd found a quiet stretch of landscape and was running through it at half-attention, the procedural terrain scrolling by like scenery outside a train window.

"— and I'm *not* saying the Academy is wrong about the deployment schedules, I'm saying they could maybe — maybe — communicate like they're talking to people instead of equipment. That's all. That's literally all I'm saying." She dodged a rock formation in-game without looking. "One of you said 'aren't mages equipment though' and I want you to know that I saw that and I'm choosing peace today. I'm choosing *peace*. But I screenshotted it."

She grinned. Chat exploded. She let it run, reading the scroll, picking out the threads — the jokes, the arguments, the mage veterans who showed up in her chat specifically for these moments, the non-mages who watched because she made the experience legible. That was the brand. That was the thing she'd built when she'd torn down the pop-star costumes and the choreographed shows and the managed appearances — she'd rebuilt herself as someone who translated. Mage life for the non-mages. The implant, the hum, the Academy, the deployments, the politics — she talked about it the way she talked about everything else, casually, mid-game, between bites of food she wasn't eating on camera anymore. She made it normal. She made it *hers*.

The hum shifted.

Not in her ears. In her spine. Below the fifth vertebra, where the implant housing sat against the bone — the R3 interface that had been fitted when she was ten and had been part of her body so long that she sometimes forgot it was technology and not anatomy. The hum shifted the way a note shifts when the sustain gives way and the harmonics begin to collapse, the overtones folding inward, and for a moment — a held breath, a beat of silence in a song that had no silence — the single sustained note that had lived in her spine for a decade became something else.

Not louder. Not a sound at all, exactly. A *pulling*. As if something had reached into the frequency she carried and *tugged*.

Her fingers stopped on the controller. One beat. Two.

The scream rose.

It came from the base of her skull — not from outside, not from the room, but from the place where the implant met her nervous system, where the R3 nitro cycling through the interface translated energy into sensation that she had been trained to manage since she was a child. The scream was not a scream. It was the hum accelerated — the sustained tone ripping upward through registers she didn't know she could feel, the harmonics no longer splitting but *screaming*, every overtone firing simultaneously, the sound of a chord struck with every key at once and held past the point where the strings should have broken.

She breathed.

Not a gasp. Not a flinch. A controlled exhale through her nose, jaw set, the muscles of her face held in the exact configuration of mild amusement that she had practiced in mirrors at fourteen when the first episodes had started and her Academy instructor had told her *control is silence — if they can't hear it, it isn't happening*. She was twenty. She'd been doing this for six years. She was a professional.

"— and I keep telling people, if you're going to critique the deployment schedule, at least *read* the deployment schedule, because half the takes I see are just — chat, are you guys seeing this terrain? This is gorgeous. Hold on. Let me — hold on."

She panned the in-game camera. Bought herself three seconds. The scream peaked — a white-hot frequency that ran the length of her spine from the implant housing to the top of her skull, a note so high and so thin that it bypassed her ears entirely and existed only in the nerve pathways that the R3 had colonized over the past ten years. Her left hand, the one not on the controller, pressed flat against her thigh under the desk. Hidden. Her fingers splayed, rigid — the hand of a pianist slamming a chord she couldn't release.

Then it passed.

The way it always passed. The scream folded back into the hum. The harmonics resolved. The sustained tone returned to its usual pitch — the same pitch, the same thickness, the same omnipresent background that she carried the way other people carried their heartbeat. Her fingers relaxed. She exhaled again, quieter this time, and felt the muscles in her jaw unlock one by one.

Chat hadn't noticed. Chat was arguing about terrain generation. Someone had clipped the landscape pan and was posting it with thinking-face emojis. Someone else was asking if she'd tried the eastern canyon. Three people were still fighting about deployment schedules.

*She* noticed. She noticed the way she always noticed — a beat behind the event, after the performance had already covered it, when the body sent its belated report: *that happened, that was bad, that was worse than the last one*. She pressed her fingertips together under the desk. The tips were numb. That was new.

"Okay we're going east. Chat has spoken. East it is. If I die, it's on all of you, and I *will* be reading the names."

The stream continued. The game continued. The performance continued — the warmth, the riffing, the casual authority of a woman who had been on camera for a third of her life and could maintain the show through anything short of unconsciousness. She was good at this. She had always been good at this. The gap between what she felt and what they saw was the space she lived in, and it was wide enough to hold the scream and still look like a person having fun.

Ten hours. She felt every one of them. Not the way the audience felt it — not as entertainment, not as community, not as the parasocial comfort that two hundred thousand people drew from watching a woman play a game and talk about her life. She felt it the way a performer feels it: as labor, as calibration, as the constant low-grade expenditure of making the real look effortless. Her spine ached. The hum was back to baseline, but baseline was louder than it had been six months ago, and six months ago was louder than the year before that, and the year before that was louder than the year before that, and at some point the word *baseline* stopped meaning *normal* and started meaning *the current rate of deterioration*.

She didn't think about that. She thought about the eastern canyon, which was beautiful, and about chat, which was warm, and about the fact that she needed to pee and hadn't eaten anything real since this morning and the pizza on the counter was two days old and she was twenty years old and this was her life and it was fine. It was fine.

"Alright. *Alright.* We're wrapping. I know, I know — I love you too, chat. Same time Thursday unless I die in my sleep, which based on my eating habits is statistically possible. Raid — let me find someone to raid. Is Mako live? Mako's live. We're raiding Mako. Be nice. Be *nice*. I'll know if you're not nice." She paused, smiled, and the smile was real again, the dangerous kind, the kind that meant she still needed this even though it was eating her. "Thank you. Genuinely. I mean it. Okay. Bye. Go. Get out. Love you. Bye."

She ended the stream.

The silence landed like a held note finally released.

She sat in the chair for eleven seconds after the broadcast cut. She counted them. Not deliberately — the counting was automatic, a habit she'd developed in her first year of streaming when the post-stream silence had felt so violent that she'd needed something structural to bridge the gap between *on* and *off*. Eleven seconds. Then the performer left.

The posture went first. Her spine — the spine that held the implant, that carried the hum, that she kept straight and open because that was what performers did — curved forward. Her shoulders rounded. She pulled her knees up into the chair and wrapped her arms around them and became the smallest version of herself, the 5'4" frame that had commanded a room for ten hours folding into a shape that took up almost no space at all. The ring light was still on. The LED strip was still glowing its blue-to-pink gradient. The monitors still showed the end-of-stream analytics — peak viewers, average watchtime, new subscribers, raid success — and she did not look at them.

She looked at nothing. Her right eye — the real one — stared at the wall behind the monitors. Her left eye — the implant — was still scrolling. Always scrolling. Post-stream mentions, clips going up, a message from her mod team about a chatter they'd banned during the deployment-schedule argument. The pink eye never turned off. That was the deal. That was the architecture of the implant — spinal interface connected to ocular HUD connected to the neural pathways that processed both, a closed circuit that ran on R3 nitro and ran *constantly*, because the engineers who designed the system hadn't built an off switch and the Academy instructors who trained her hadn't taught her to want one.

She unfolded enough to stand. Crossed the apartment to the bathroom. The kit was where it always was — behind the mirror, matte black case, zippered. She opened it the way she opened it every night after a stream: deliberately, without urgency, the way you take a prescribed medication because that's what it was, functionally, regardless of what the law said about it. Inside: a milligram scale, a glass vial half-full of blue powder, a metal straw she cleaned after every use.

Lumina. The name was marketing — someone in a lab had decided that a drug promising silence should sound like light, which was the kind of contradictory branding that Kira, as a professional brand architect, could appreciate even as she recognized it as bullshit. The packaging claimed void-residue. The forums claimed void-residue. Whether it was actually void-residue or just another synthetic neuromodulator dressed up in mystique didn't matter. What mattered was this: it worked.

She tapped the vial. Set the scale on the countertop. Weighed out forty milligrams — her dose, calibrated over fourteen months of careful titration, the exact amount that quieted the noise enough for sleep. Forty milligrams. Not thirty, which left edges. Not fifty, which knocked her out so hard the alarm couldn't reach her. Forty.

She cut the line on the countertop with the edge of a card. Leaned down. Inhaled through the metal straw — left nostril, quick, practiced, the powder hitting the back of her sinuses with a cold burn that lasted two seconds and then was gone.

The onset was immediate. Not euphoria — Lumina didn't do euphoria. Not numbness — not at forty. What Lumina did was *subtract*. The noise in her head — the racing thoughts, the branching what-ifs, the constant parallel processing of every person and every scenario and every possible outcome — went from a crowd to a room. The hum in her spine didn't get quieter. The hum never got quieter. But the part of her brain that kept *reaching* for it, the pattern-seeking machinery that wanted to resolve the harmonics into meaning — that part went still. Not silent. Still. The difference between a room full of people talking and a room full of people breathing.

She cleaned the straw. Zipped the case. Put it back behind the mirror.

The apartment reasserted itself around her. The pizza box. The glass of forgotten something. The laundry on the couch. The kitchen she wasn't going to look at. The space she lived in when the camera was off — the space that proved she was a person, or proved she wasn't, depending on how you read it. The streaming corner was immaculate. The rest was entropy. She'd read once that the state of a person's home reflected the state of their mind, and she'd thought about that for exactly as long as it took to close the article and open chat.

She touched the back of her neck. The hum was there. It was always there. But the thickness — the new thing, the splitting, the harmonics she couldn't resolve — that was still present at the edges. Like a choir holding a note after the conductor had lowered the baton. Like something waiting.

Her left eye pulsed. A message — not from the mod team, not from the feed. The group chat.

She opened it. Five names: Dex, Priya, Tomás, Suki, and her. Academy friends, mostly. Dex and Priya from her years at Varma. Tomás from the São Paulo program, connected through the streamer circuit. Suki from Kyoto — not a streamer, a field mage, active deployment, the only one in the group who used her implant the way the Academy intended. They talked most days. Not about anything. About everything. The chat was the one space that was neither performance nor silence — it was just people who understood what the hum felt like, talking the way people talk when they don't have to explain the baseline.

The message was from Priya.

*Weird question. Did any of you feel that?*

Kira stared at it. Her right eye on the text. Her left eye already scanning the timestamp — forty-seven minutes ago. Sent during the stream. During the —

*Feel what?*

That was Tomás.

*Idk. Hard to describe. Like a pull? In the implant. Around 21:30 UTC. Lasted maybe ten seconds.*

Dex:

*bro's glitching again 💀💀*

Tomás:

*😭😭 priya what are you on and where can I get some*

Priya:

*I'm serious. It was different from the usual stuff. Not louder. Just... directional? Like it came from somewhere.*

Suki:

*I was on deployment. Didn't notice anything unusual.*

Dex:

*"Didn't notice anything unusual" most suki response ever*

*Certified field mage moment™*

The chat moved on. Someone posted a clip of a cat. Tomás sent a meme she'd seen three times already. Dex and Priya started arguing about a game neither of them had finished. Normal. Casual. The texture of people who'd known each other since they were children with fresh implants and bad haircuts, before the hum had become loud enough to talk about and the talking had become complicated enough to avoid.

Kira read Priya's message again. *Like a pull. In the implant. Around 21:30 UTC.*

21:30 UTC. That was — she checked, though she didn't need to — the landscape pan. Hour nine. The three seconds she'd bought herself. The scream.

She started typing. Stopped. Her fingers hovered — not over the keyboard but over the edge of the desk, pressing into the surface, finding shapes. Silent chord progressions. C minor. F diminished. The patterns her hands made when her mind was working faster than her willingness to commit to a thought.

She could say it. *Yeah, I felt it too.* Four words. The chat would take it seriously because she was Kira and Kira didn't exaggerate and Kira had a platform and Kira's word had weight. The four words would become a thread and the thread would become a conversation and the conversation would become something she'd have to manage, something she'd have to *think* about, and thinking about it meant thinking about the hum and thinking about the hum meant thinking about why it was getting thicker and thinking about why it was getting thicker meant —

She closed the chat.

She sat with it. Thirty seconds. The Lumina should have been enough — forty milligrams was the dose, the *right* dose, the number she'd spent fourteen months calibrating — but the quiet room in her head was filling back up and this time the voices had something specific to say. Priya's timestamp. Her scream. The pull. A line between two points that she did not want to follow to wherever it went.

She went back to the bathroom. Opened the kit. Scale. Vial. Straw.

Fifteen milligrams. Not a dose. A nudge. Just enough to push the edges back for another hour, to keep the room quiet long enough to fall asleep before the pattern-seeking machinery assembled Priya's question and her scream into something she'd have to think about. She weighed it precisely — because she was always precise, because precision was control and control was the line between *using* and *needing* — and leaned down and inhaled and the cold burn was brief and shallow and the quiet that followed was not silence but close enough.

The forty was supposed to be enough. The forty was always enough.

She cleaned the straw. Put the kit away. Didn't look in the mirror this time.

The ring light was still on. She should turn it off. She didn't.

She sat in the streaming chair, in the curated corner of her uncurated life, and the hum held its note in her spine like a chord no one had asked her to play. Outside the window the Bay was dark. The city ran on nitro and the nitro ran through conduits beneath the streets and the conduits hummed at frequencies that most people couldn't hear and Kira could hear all of them, had always been able to hear all of them, and the thing she'd spent six years learning to hide on camera was not the scream itself but the fact that she was listening.

She was always listening.

The gap between what she was and what they needed her to be was exactly wide enough for a held breath, a three-second camera pan, and a smile that was real. That was the professional margin. That was the space she lived in. It had always been enough.

She touched the back of her neck, where the implant sat against the bone. The hum was there. Thicker now. Waiting.

It had always been enough.
