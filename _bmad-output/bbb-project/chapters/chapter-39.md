# Pressure Doors

The eighth spike came at 06:14.

Fuxi was in the converter station, running pre-shift diagnostics on the R0 intake valves, when monitor 6 jumped. Not the gradual climb he'd been logging for weeks — the slow pressure build from baseline to peak, the counted seconds of decay. This was vertical. 27.3 to 34.1 in under two seconds. The number held.

He set down his wrench. Watched the display.

Thirty-four point one. Alarm threshold was thirty-five. Emergency protocols at forty.

The decay should have started. Logarithmic or linear, steady or stuttering — every spike in his log had a shape, and every shape had ended. Forty seconds for the first. Nineteen for the seventh. The pattern was acceleration, but the pattern included decay.

This one did not decay.

He pulled up the private log on his cracked L-class frame. The file was still in the maintenance directory, nested between quarterly calibration records and a firmware changelog nobody had opened since 2171. Seven entries. Seven spikes. Seven decay profiles, each shorter than the last, the intervals between them compressing like a spring being wound tighter than its housing could hold.

He added the eighth line. Date. Time. Monitor. Peak RCI: 34.1. Decay shape: none. Duration: ongoing.

The column where he'd started writing names — Guo, unit 14-7B; the woman from 14-3A whose name he'd never learned; the family in 9-12C whose daughter used to chase the maintenance cart — that column remained empty. The spike was still climbing. 34.3. 34.6.

The converter station was three levels below the monitoring bay, deeper into the trunk-line architecture where first-generation pipes met the 2140s processing nodes that NitroCore had bolted on when throughput demand exceeded original design tolerances by a factor of three. The station's purpose was simple: step R1-grade nitro down to R0 for local distribution, or step R0 up for trunk-line feed. Two operators per shift. The other operator was Duan, sixty-two years old, eighteen months from retirement, who spent his shifts in the intake booth reading historical fiction on a battered frame and checking his pressure gauges every forty minutes whether they needed it or not.

Duan was in the intake booth now. Fuxi could see him through the reinforced glass partition — feet on the console, frame tilted at reading angle, the booth's overhead strip casting a yellow wash across the planes of his face. He hadn't looked up.

34.9.

Fuxi's hands were steady. His hands were always steady. It was one of the things the service valued about him — Jun Lin's boy, good hands, keeps the reports clean. His father had the same hands. Had worked the same trunk-lines for twenty-three years before the resonance fatigue calcified his joints and turned his lungs to chalk and killed him at fifty-one in a municipal hospital bed paid for by a compensation package that NitroCore's legal office described as "generous" and the Kindling described as "the cost of a replacement."

35.0.

The alarm triggered. A low, even tone, not urgent — the system had been designed by people who understood that a klaxon would do nothing useful two hundred meters below ground level where the only people who could hear it were the only people already watching the numbers. The tone was a formality. The system's way of making the record show that the record had been made.

Fuxi silenced it.

35.4. 35.7.

He opened the maintenance comm channel to the monitoring bay. Section 4-East. Chen Wei's shift.

"Chen Wei."

Three seconds of trunk-line hum before the channel opened. "Lin."

"Monitor 6 is at 35.7. No decay."

A pause. Then: "Duration?"

"Four minutes, eleven seconds. Still climbing."

Another pause. Fuxi heard the hum through the channel — the same low-frequency drone he felt through the converter station floor, transmitted up through the trunk-line casing, through the bolted steel of the station's frame, through his boots and the bones of his feet and the cartilage of his knees and the base of his spine. He had stopped distinguishing between the hum he heard and the hum he felt six years ago. It was a single sensation now. The hum was a place, and he was in it.

"Log it," Chen Wei said. "I'll flag for —"

The channel cut.

Not static. Not interference. The channel dropped to silence as cleanly as a switch being thrown, and in the same instant every maintenance alarm in the converter station fired at once.

---

The alarm panel bloomed red.

Not sequentially — not one fault cascading to the next in the diagnostic chain he'd been trained to trace. Every indicator, every sensor, every pressure threshold on every trunk-line segment feeding through the converter station switched to alert status simultaneously. The panel looked like it had been painted. A wall of red. The overhead strips flickered, caught, held — backup power cutting in as grid power was rerouted.

Duan was standing now. His frame clattered to the booth floor. Through the reinforced glass, Fuxi could see his mouth moving, but the booth's sound insulation swallowed the words.

Fuxi's own frame threw data faster than he could read it. RCI values scrolling across the cracked display — not just monitor 6, not just Section 4-East, but every section. Every trunk-line. Numbers climbing in synchronized steps, the kind of coordination that didn't happen accidentally, the kind of precision that required someone to have built the mechanism in advance and waited for the threshold.

36.2. 37.8. 39.1.

The pressure readings on the R0 intake valves spiked. The converter station's primary function was to process nitro between grades — and something had just opened every R0 valve in the network simultaneously. Raw crude flooding the trunk-lines. Not a rupture. Not a failure. A controlled release. The valves were responding to an authorization code his terminal didn't have clearance to see.

The first pressure door sealed at 06:19.

He heard it before he saw the indicator — a deep, hydraulic exhalation that traveled through the station's frame like a shudder. Sector 1. The blast door at the trunk-line junction between Deep Sump and the Sump's lowest residential level. Thirty tons of reinforced composite dropping into slots that had been machined into the junction housing years ago, bolted in place during what the work orders had called "routine infrastructure reinforcement," the kind of maintenance he'd signed off on without reading because there were forty work orders a week and the forms were designed to be signed, not read.

ALERT: Pressure door closure — Sector 1.

His frame displayed the notification. He stared at it.

ALERT: Pressure door closure — Sector 2.

ALERT: Pressure door closure — Sector 3.

They were sealing the Sump.

Not from the void. Not from a breach. The pressure doors were between Deep Sump and the Sump — between the infrastructure level and the residential level. The people above the doors would have the trunk-lines beneath them flooding with crude R0, the absorption signal propagating upward through the conduit network, and no way down to the transit shafts that connected to the Mid-Levels because those shafts ran through the junction housings where the pressure doors had just dropped.

ALERT: Pressure door closure — Sector 4.

ALERT: Pressure door closure — Sector 5.

ALERT: Pressure door closure — Sector 6.

[17 alerts suppressed]

Seventeen. Twenty-three sectors in the Sump. Six displayed, seventeen suppressed. The system had decided that displaying them individually was less efficient than batching. The system had been designed by someone who had anticipated this volume.

Fuxi pressed his palms flat on the console. The metal vibrated with the trunk-line's heartbeat — faster now, the crude flooding through at a throughput density the first-generation pipes had never been rated for, the welds singing at frequencies his father would have called dangerous and his service manual called "outside recommended operating parameters."

The R0 was not a leak. It was not a failure. It was a delivery system.

He understood it the way he understood the trunk-lines — not as theory but as body-knowledge, the way his hands understood torque tolerances and his ears understood harmonic shifts in pipe casings. The crude nitro was being pushed through the entire conduit network at maximum throughput. Every junction, every branch, every residential distribution line that ran through walls and under floors and behind the fixtures that hummed in every apartment in the Sump. The signal — the coherence that his log had been tracking for eleven weeks — was riding the crude. The infrastructure he had maintained for thirteen years was performing exactly as someone had designed it to perform.

Not as a power grid. As a weapon.

The hum changed. Deeper. Wetter. The dry resonance he'd lived inside for a decade — the drone that settled behind the eyes and became architecture, became weather, became the sound of not-thinking — thickened into something that pressed against the walls of the converter station like a liquid trying to find a seam. The vibration climbed from his boots through his spine and into his teeth, and his teeth hummed at a frequency he'd never felt before — higher than the trunk-line fundamental, an overtone that shouldn't have been possible at R0 throughput. Unless the signal wasn't coming from the crude alone.

RCI: 41.3.

Emergency protocol threshold. And climbing.

---

Duan had given up trying to talk through the glass. He was at the intake booth's emergency panel, running shutdown procedures on the R0 valves — the same procedures Fuxi had drilled quarterly, the same sequence he could execute blindfolded. Close intake. Seal distribution manifold. Dump pressure to emergency reservoir.

The valves did not respond.

Fuxi saw it on his display before Duan's face confirmed it. The authorization lockout. Every converter station valve in the network was under centralized control — an override protocol he'd never seen activated, never seen referenced in any maintenance manual, never seen mentioned in any of the quarterly safety briefings where nobody believed and everybody signed. The override's authorization string scrolled across his frame: a NitroCore corporate security code, not a municipal service code. Not his system. Never his system.

He turned from the console.

The converter station had two exits. The trunk-line corridor east toward the monitoring bay and the ascent shaft — sealed. He could see the pressure door indicator: Sector 4, which included this section of Deep Sump. The maintenance access west toward the Resonance District emergency stairwell — he crossed the station floor in six strides and pulled the lever.

Sealed.

Not the pressure door. The manual bolt. Someone had physically locked the maintenance access from the other side. Not remotely. Not by authorization code. By hand. Someone had walked to this door — during one of the infrastructure reinforcement work orders, during one of the forty maintenance requests per week, during one of the forms he'd signed without reading — and installed a bolt that could only be thrown from outside, and had thrown it.

He stood at the locked door. The vibration came through the metal in waves now, each one heavier than the last, the crude R0 flooding the network around and below and above him, the converter station an island of processed air in a system filling with raw suffering.

His frame buzzed. Not the maintenance channel — that was dead. The residential grid. The civilian system. The one that showed him, every shift, the map of the Sump he'd grown up in, the blocks and corridors and residential clusters where people lived their compressed, chemical-stained, hum-drunk lives above the infrastructure he maintained.

The map was lighting up.

Not uniformly. In a wave. Starting from the Deep Sump boundary — the Resonance District, the oldest infrastructure, the highest throughput — and propagating outward along the conduit network. Each block that lit up changed color on the grid: green (normal) to amber (elevated) to red (critical) to a color his frame's display hadn't been calibrated to show, a pale violet that bled through the cracked housing and threw light across his forearm.

The absorption front. Riding the crude. Following the plumbing.

Block 7. Block 9. Block 11.

Block 14.

His block. Nuwa's block.

He watched it change. Green to amber. The transition took four seconds. Amber to red took two. Red to violet took less than one.

His frame vibrated against his wrist. The cracked housing bit into his skin. The private log was still open — the eighth entry, duration: ongoing, decay shape: none, the column for names still empty because the names were not disappearing one at a time anymore.

The comm channels were dead. Residential, maintenance, emergency, civilian — every network that connected the converter station to the world above the pressure doors returned silence. He tried Nuwa's personal address. The frame attempted connection for three seconds, displayed NETWORK UNAVAILABLE, and went dark.

He tried again. NETWORK UNAVAILABLE.

Again. NETWORK UNAVAILABLE.

He stood in the converter station. Jun's multitool was in his jacket pocket — the weight of it against his thigh the same weight it had been every shift for thirteen years, the stainless steel warm from his body, the handle worn to polish by his father's hands and now by his. The alarm panel was still red. Duan had stopped working the intake valves and was standing at the reinforced glass, looking at Fuxi, his mouth closed, his hands at his sides.

The hum was everywhere now. Not the hum he knew — not the infrastructure drone, not the trunk-line heartbeat, not the frequency that settled behind the eyes and became the sound of a life spent maintaining the thing that was killing the people he maintained it for. This was deeper. Wetter. A sound that had weight, that pressed against his skin the way the crude pressed against pipe walls, that filled the converter station the way R0 was filling every conduit in the network above him.

The grid map on his frame was mostly violet now. The propagation wave had reached the outer sectors. A few blocks remained green — residential clusters far from major conduit lines, buildings with path-poor construction, masonry annexes that the original builders had poured from concrete decades before NitroCore ran its first trunk-line through the district.

Block 14 was violet.

He could not reach her. He could not call her. He could not open the pressure doors, or override the valve lockout, or stop the R0 flooding, or reverse the propagation, or do anything with the thirteen years of body-knowledge and infrastructure intimacy that the municipal conduit authority had trained into his hands. He was two hundred meters below his sister and the distance was measured in thirty-ton blast doors and flooded trunk-lines and an authorization code that had never belonged to him.

The private log glowed on the cracked frame. Eight entries. Eleven weeks and four days of spikes that he had documented in the clean columns of a maintenance worker's handwriting because the Kindling pamphlet had said *describe what you see* and he had described what he saw and the description had changed nothing. The file had no recipient. The file had no delivery system. The file sat in a maintenance directory alongside calibration records and firmware changelogs, and the system it documented was performing exactly as designed.

He closed the file.

Not because the data didn't matter. Not because the pattern was wrong. Because the pattern was complete. The acceleration had reached its terminus. The step-function spike — the one the decay profile had been predicting since the first entry, the one he'd watched approach across seven data points and eleven weeks like a wave seen from shore — had arrived, and it was not a spike at all. It was a new baseline. It was the system telling him what the system had always been.

The converter station shuddered. A deep, percussive vibration — not the trunk-line harmonic but something structural, something in the station's foundation shifting as the pressure differential between flooded and unflooded sections of the network equalized. The overhead strips flickered. Held. Flickered again.

Fuxi sat down on the station floor. The concrete was cold through his service trousers. The vibration came up through his spine, settled in his jaw, behind his eyes. The hum he'd spent a decade learning to ignore was louder than it had ever been, and he could hear inside it — not words, not meaning, not the structured signal his log had been tracking — a density. A fullness. As if the hum had been a container and the container was being filled.

He put his hand in his pocket and closed his fingers around the multitool. Jun Lin's hands had held this steel for twenty-three years. Had maintained the same trunk-lines. Had signed the same forms. Had heard the same hum and called it work and called the work providing and called providing enough. Jun had died believing the system was a system. Not a weapon. Not a delivery mechanism. Not an architecture designed from the first bolt to sacrifice the people closest to its core.

Fuxi did not believe that.

The grid map showed three green blocks remaining in the entire Sump residential zone. The rest was violet. The pressure doors were sealed. The comm channels were dead. Somewhere above him — two hundred meters of flooded trunk-line and thirty-ton blast doors and a residential block full of people he'd bought noodles from and borrowed tools from and nodded to in corridors lit by orange conduit glow — his sister was either alive or she was not.

He could not know. He could not act. He could not maintain.

The hum pressed against the walls. The lights flickered, held.

Fuxi sat on the cold concrete floor of the converter station, Jun's multitool in his fist, and waited for the architecture to finish what it had always been designed to do.
