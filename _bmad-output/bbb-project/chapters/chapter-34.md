> *"A dead-hand is not a weapon. It is the architecture of a conversation you expect to be interrupted."*
> — Black Babel, Operational Protocols (undated)

---

The dead-hand timer said fourteen hours.

Mirelle sat at the terminal in the transit box and read the number twice. Fourteen hours — the residue of a seventy-two-hour window she'd reset thirty-one hours ago in a stairwell between the Deep Sump sub-levels and the Resonance District service corridor, standing on a landing that smelled of conduit sealant and cable grease, her hands shaking from the descent and the readings and the seventh valve.

Seven valves now. Six from the flood-valve expedition three weeks ago — NitroCore PMU-7 series, NCS-4400 hydraulic gates, hardwired control conduits, installation dates 2171 through 2173. And the seventh, documented five days ago through a different route — a ventilation service corridor in Sector 6, credentials borrowed from a retired pressure-systems inspector whose name she'd encrypted and sealed before she'd finished climbing the access ladder. The seventh valve was identical to the other six. Same make. Same series. Same installation period. Same hardwired control architecture that connected to nothing she could trace from the outside.

Seven valves across four sectors. She'd extrapolated in the annotation: if the installation pattern held across all twenty-three Sump sectors, the total was in the dozens. Infrastructure on that scale was not a contingency. It was a system. Pre-installed, pre-authorized, waiting for a trigger that the architecture's designers had anticipated before the architecture was built.

She saved the annotation. Added it to the deposit file.

The ringing in her ears was louder this morning. Both ears now — the right had been first, eleven weeks ago, a high constant tone that sat inside the ambient hum like a wire threaded through cable housing. The left had joined at week nine, lower, wider, a tone that didn't compete with the right but converged with it in a frequency she could feel in her jaw when the building's conduit branch cycled to its nighttime throughput. Eleven weeks. The audiologist at Zhongshan Medical had said twelve was the threshold — twelve weeks of sustained exposure and the sensorineural damage stopped being progressive and started being permanent. She was one week from permanent.

She'd stopped pressing her thumb against the tragus. The cartilage deformed. The ringing did not. The test had become redundant.

She powered the terminal and opened the evidence file. The complete dataset — fourteen months of field investigation compiled into an architecture that would survive the investigator. She read through it the way she read through it every morning now, not to verify the contents but to verify the shape. The shape was the argument. The individual files were data. The shape they made when assembled was the story.

Item: flood valve documentation. Seven trunk-line junctions. Photographs, serial numbers, installation dates, procurement classifications. NitroCore PMU-7 series. Hardwired control conduits. Secondary drainage outlets directional toward the Confluence.

Item: RCI field readings. Fifty-three measurements across six sectors. Baseline Sump 8–19. Deep Sump 22–34. Resonance District 28–38. Below Level 12 junctions 34.6–44.7. Trunk-line adjacent disappearance sites averaging 31. The gradient was geographic, consistent, and pointed at the conduit network.

Item: disappearances. Twenty-eight confirmed across six sectors over fourteen months. All within two hundred meters of trunk-line infrastructure. All reclassified by the Feed. Names, dates, coordinates, Feed language. The vocabulary of bureaucratic erasure, cataloged.

Item: infrastructure maps. Sub-Level 12 corridor network. Four junctions documented in detail. Shaft access points. Pressure door locations. The parallel conduit network — modern composite, Spire-grade material, concealed by decommission classification.

Item: Arctic-7 co-origin data. Throughput density maps. Breach event overlays. The handwritten annotation: *Co-origin. Not proximity. Not correlation.*

Item: matched-district analysis. Four layers. Infrastructure density. Disappearance density. RCI gradient. Junction reclassifications. The correlation that was not subtle and not publishable and not coincidence.

Item: the diplomat's fragments. Classification anomalies. Annex B/C routing. Observer seat empty fourteen months. The institutional architecture that contained the evidence by distributing it across jurisdictions that would never compare notes.

Item: her analysis. The raw register. The angry voice. *The infrastructure was built to flood. The flood valves predate the crisis. The sacrifice was engineered before the justification.*

She closed the file. Checked the dead-hand timer. Thirteen hours, forty-two minutes.

She needed to see Talia.

---

Node RD-14 had moved. Not far — two buildings east, a sub-level unit accessed through a textile wholesaler's loading dock in the Resonance District's commercial strip. The rotation was Talia's operational rhythm: never the same address for more than three months, the node migrating through the district's commercial infrastructure the way water migrated through fractured concrete. The access protocol was the same — six words in Cantonese, monthly rotation, physical presence required.

The loading dock was narrow and cold. Mirelle gave the phrase to a man sorting packing crates who did not look up and did not respond except that a freight door behind the crates slid open with the efficiency she remembered from the repair shop and the cable yard before it.

She walked through.

The new space was smaller than the old one. The insulation was better — the hum attenuated to a pressure rather than a sound, the vibration present in the floor but absent from the walls, the kind of careful acoustic engineering that cost more than most Sump residents' annual income. The hardware was the same: storage racks, encrypted relays, displays running data streams she couldn't read. Talia's operational footprint was portable. The architecture traveled.

Talia was standing. That was different. Every previous meeting, she'd been seated — on a cable spool, at a workstation, in the posture of someone who owned the room's geometry. Today she stood at the central display, her back to the door, both hands flat on the interface panel.

"You're on time," Talia said without turning. "That's new."

"I was early to two of our last three meetings."

"You were early to one. Late to one. On time to none." She turned. The processing ring in her eyes caught the display's light — amber cycling to green, the optical hardware running whatever passive assessment Talia performed on everyone who entered her space. "You look like shit, Dubois."

"I brought a gift."

Mirelle set the drive on the interface panel. The same kind of drive she'd deposited nine months ago — thumb-sized, solid-state, commercial hardware wiped to factory spec. But the encryption key was different. Heavier. The dataset it carried had taken fourteen months to compile and would take longer than that to break.

Talia looked at the drive. Didn't touch it.

"That's bigger than your usual deposits."

"That's everything."

The word sat in the insulated room. Talia's processing ring pulsed once. Slow.

"Everything," she repeated. Not a question. The register of someone who understood the weight of the word before it finished arriving.

"Flood valve documentation — seven junctions now. RCI field readings from fifty-three sites. Twenty-eight confirmed disappearances. Infrastructure maps. Sub-Level 12 corridor network. Matched-district analysis. Arctic-7 co-origin data. Diplomatic fragments. My analysis. Fourteen months."

"Your analysis." The same eyebrow, the same millimeter shift she'd given nine months ago when Mirelle had first started annotating her deposits. "You're still editorializing."

"I'm still contextualizing."

"Your clients won't know the difference."

"My clients are the dead-hand recipients. They'll know."

Talia picked up the drive. Inserted it into the interface. The file tree populated — denser than anything Mirelle had previously deposited, the encrypted contents unpacking in layers that took the system eleven seconds to render. Talia's eyes moved with the luminescent pulse of her optical hardware, processing faster than conscious attention.

She stopped on the flood valve photographs. The PMU-7 series. The installation dates.

"These are pre-installed."

"2171 through 2173."

"Before the current crisis threshold."

"Before the threshold was published. Before the tolerance bands were set. The valves were installed during what NitroCore classified as routine infrastructure reinforcement. The work orders are in the file."

Talia scrolled. The RCI gradient. The disappearance overlay. The matched-district analysis — four layers converging on the same geography, the same architecture, the same conclusion. She scrolled through the entire dataset without speaking. Three minutes. The longest silence Mirelle had experienced in this room.

"You're not depositing evidence," Talia said. "You're writing a will."

Mirelle turned her stylus between her fingers. Once. The rhythm that meant she was processing — or that she didn't want to answer.

"I'm upgrading the dead-hand."

"You're finalizing it." Talia turned from the display. Her expression was the same professional assessment it had always been — the appraiser evaluating the market, not the object. But her voice carried something Mirelle hadn't heard before. Not concern. *Calculation.* The kind of arithmetic Talia performed on assets and liabilities, applied now to the woman standing across from her. "When was the last time you slept more than four hours?"

"That's not in the scope of our transaction."

"Your transaction requires you to check in every seventy-two hours. If you're dead, you can't check in. Your sleep schedule is an operational variable."

"I sleep enough."

"You have bilateral nitro-ear and you've lost weight since the last deposit and your hands haven't stopped moving since you walked in. How many weeks?"

"Eleven."

Talia held her gaze. Two seconds. Three. The ring was steady — not processing. Measuring.

"One week," she said.

Mirelle didn't answer. Talia didn't need her to. The number was the same number the Zhongshan Medical audiologist had provided — twelve weeks, permanent threshold. One week was not a professional assessment. It was a countdown.

"The dead-hand architecture," Mirelle said. "I want to review the distribution chain."

Talia turned back to the interface. Professional again. The personal calculus filed where she filed everything that wasn't transactional.

"Three sealed addresses. Encryption keys distributed at registration. Auto-distribute triggers on two missed seventy-two-hour windows — one hundred forty-four hours total. The distribution is simultaneous — all three recipients receive the complete package at the same time. No staggering, no priority order. The architecture doesn't know who they are. I don't know who they are."

"I know who they are."

"That's your business." Talia pulled up the dead-hand protocol interface. "Current deposit: the dataset you've been building for nine months. Twenty-three prior updates. You want to replace the entire deposit with this new package?"

"Replace. The new package contains everything from the prior deposits plus the seventh junction, the updated analysis, and the latest disappearances. The prior deposits are redundant."

Talia's fingers moved. The interface responded — file trees collapsing, new structures populating, the dead-hand deposit rebuilding itself around the comprehensive dataset Mirelle had just delivered.

"There's something else crossing my node," Talia said. She didn't look up from the interface. "Atmospheric data. Scientific. Convergence modeling with a variable I've never seen in an atmospheric paper — consciousness-dependent, if you can believe that. Encrypted, routed through a protocol I didn't build."

Mirelle's stylus stopped. "Source?"

"Anonymous. The routing is institutional — someone inside a research organization with access to atmospheric monitoring infrastructure. The data arrived two days ago. Sitting in my general intake."

"Can I see it?"

"No." Talia finished the dead-hand rebuild and turned. "The data wasn't sent to you. It was sent to the network. Different client stream. My architecture compartmentalizes — you know that."

"Atmospheric convergence data with a consciousness variable and you're sitting on it."

"I'm *holding* it. For a client stream that isn't yours. Different channel, different encryption, different purpose." She paused. "Whoever sent it already has what they need from it. They're not looking for a journalist. They're looking for a distribution architecture."

"That's what I'm looking for."

"You already have one." Talia gestured at the display where the dead-hand protocol was rebuilding. "Yours fires when you stop checking in. Theirs fires when they decide. Different mechanisms. Different timelines. I don't cross the streams."

Mirelle turned the stylus between her fingers. The atmospheric data was a thread she could pull — convergence modeling, consciousness-dependent variables, the kind of scientific framework that could give her evidence an institutional vocabulary that Gauthier's editorial board might accept. But Talia's architecture was compartmentalized for reasons Mirelle understood professionally even as she resented them personally. The streams didn't cross. The dead-hand didn't know who the recipients were. The atmospheric data didn't know who the journalist was. The architecture protected everyone by keeping everyone ignorant of everyone else.

"Fine," she said.

"Fine." Talia turned back to the interface. "Dead-hand rebuild complete. New deposit registered. Client: M. Expiry: seventy-two hours rolling. The timer resets from your last check-in — you have..." She consulted the display. "Forty-one hours remaining on the current window."

"The recipients."

"Three sealed addresses. Same as registration. Do you want to modify?"

Mirelle thought about the three addresses. São Paulo — the Wire journalist who had covered the Mediterranean refugee corridor with her, who understood sourcing and evidence chains and would know what to do with the dataset without being told. Singapore — the freelance investigator who had published the VEC circuit-purge exposé when no institutional outlet would touch it. And the third.

"The third address," she said. "The institutional contact. Does the protocol still route through the encrypted back-channel?"

"The protocol routes to the address you provided at registration. If the address is an encrypted channel, the package arrives through that channel. The dead-hand doesn't know the difference between a direct address and a relay."

"Good."

Talia set down the interface stylus. Picked up her mug — different mug, same tea. The mint crossed the insulated space.

"Your diplomat checks that channel every six hours," she said. "I track traffic patterns on the protocols I host. Not content — frequency. He's been checking every six hours. Weeks."

"He's not my diplomat."

"He checks every six hours, Dubois. That's somebody's diplomat."

The room held the observation the way Talia's architecture held data — without judgment, without interpretation, stored at the register it arrived in. Mirelle didn't answer. The stylus turned between her fingers — faster now, the rhythm she couldn't control, the tell that experienced interviewees learned to watch for and that Talia had undoubtedly catalogued months ago.

"Is there anything else?" Mirelle said.

Talia drank her tea. Set the mug down. The ring in her eyes pulsed once — the slow cycle that meant she was deciding what to say, or what not to say, or whether the distinction mattered.

"The Babel price," she said.

"Name it."

"Nothing."

Mirelle's stylus stopped.

"That's not how you work."

"It's how I work today." Talia's voice was level. The same professional register, the same transactional cadence. But the content was wrong. Talia charged for everything. Talia's economy was information, and information was what Mirelle had, and the exchange was the architecture of their entire relationship. "The dataset you just deposited is the most comprehensive independent investigation of NitroCore's Sump infrastructure that's crossed my node. When it distributes — *if* it distributes — the markets it moves will be worth more than anything you could pay me. I'm investing in your dead-hand, Dubois. Not your wallet."

"You're investing in my death."

"I'm investing in the architecture you built around it. There's a difference." She picked up the mug again. The tea was cold. She drank it anyway. "Same as last time."

Mirelle stood. The meeting was over — she could feel the shape of it, the way Talia's posture shifted toward the display, the way the professional space between them contracted toward the exit. But she didn't move toward the door.

"Talia."

"Mm."

"If the dead-hand fires. If the package distributes. You'll know."

"I'll know because you'll have missed two check-in windows. That's all I'll know. I don't investigate. I don't follow up. I don't —"

"Mourn. I remember."

"— speculate about what happened. The architecture fires. The data moves. My involvement ends." She held Mirelle's gaze. The ring was steady. "That was the service you purchased. No sentiment. No follow-up."

"No sentiment."

"None." Talia set down the mug. The sound it made against the interface panel was the only noise in the insulated room besides the attenuated hum and the ringing that never stopped. "Go check in. You have forty hours."

The Resonance District hit her like a wall.

The transition from Talia's insulated space to the district's full-spectrum hum was physical — a pressure that settled into her jaw, her sternum, the bilateral ringing that spiked and converged with the infrastructure's voice before settling into the constant dual tone that had become the soundtrack of her weeks.

She walked north. Variation D — the fourth route she'd mapped for this particular node location, calibrated to the evening shift's foot traffic and the reduced surveillance coverage that came with the commercial strip's transition to nighttime operations. The streets were narrower here. Fewer feed-screens. The hum deeper than the sections closer to the Mid-Levels boundary, the conduit infrastructure older, the pipes carrying throughput densities that the tolerance bands measured and the institutions adjusted and nobody who lived above the Sump could feel.

Twenty-eight disappearances. Two more since the flood valves — a trunk-line welder in Sector 5 and a conduit inspector in Sector 2. The Feed had said *reassigned* for both. The Feed always said *reassigned* or *relocated* or *credential transferred.* The vocabulary didn't change because the vocabulary was the mechanism. Not a failure of language. A function of it.

She reached the Sector 3 commercial strip. The crowd was thinner at this hour — third shift beginning, the corridors less dense, her silhouette more visible in the transit corridor's orange light. She adjusted the implant's recording gain. The iris shifted. She wasn't recording — not deliberately — but the hardware was always cycling, always storing, the data accumulating in the local partition behind her left orbit with the patient consistency of equipment that didn't distinguish between evidence and routine.

The transit box was warm. The conduit branch humming through the floor at its nighttime register — lower, wider, the building's reduced power draw changing the frequency the way it always did, the way her body had learned to track without conscious attention. She locked the door. Checked the corridor monitor. Nothing.

She sat at the terminal. Opened the dead-hand interface. Thirty-nine hours. She typed the reset code. The timer climbed back to seventy-two.

Seventy-two hours. Then seventy-two more. The architecture holding.

She opened a new file. The notation she added after every deposit — the private record, the layer beneath the evidence, the voice that existed between the clean sentences of her evidentiary analysis and the angry register of her unpublished conclusions.

*Dead-hand finalized. Complete dataset deposited — RD-14. Seven flood valves. Fifty-three RCI readings. Twenty-eight disappearances. Infrastructure maps. Matched-district analysis. Co-origin data. Diplomatic fragments. My analysis. Fourteen months in one package. The architecture survives the architect.*

*Talia charged nothing. That's the price you pay when the broker calculates that your data is worth more as a posthumous market event than a living transaction. She's not wrong. She's never wrong about markets. She's wrong about me — I'm not planning to die. I'm planning for the architecture to survive the possibility.*

*The atmospheric data is interesting. Consciousness-dependent convergence modeling, routed through Talia's node, compartmentalized from my stream. Someone in the scientific apparatus has found the same pattern from a different angle and sent it to the same network I did. The streams don't cross. That's the architecture. Maybe it's better that way — my evidence and their science arriving through separate channels, to separate recipients, building the same case from different materials. The story doesn't need a single source. The story needs to exist in enough places that removing one doesn't remove the pattern.*

*Twenty-eight names. The twenty-ninth had not been mine. Not today.*

She saved the file. Added it to the local backup — not the dead-hand deposit, which was finalized, but the personal archive that lived on the drive in her boot and the paper in her coat lining and the local partition behind her left eye. Three copies. Three media. The redundancy that Djamel had taught her in Abidjan, that the cameraman in Caracas had died proving insufficient, that she maintained not because it guaranteed survival but because it guaranteed the survival of the data.

She closed the terminal. Lay on the cot. The frame vibrated — the conduit branch's nighttime frequency transmitted through the building's concrete and the cot's metal legs and the thin mattress that separated her body from the infrastructure's voice.

The ringing in her right ear found the ringing in her left. The two tones converged. One week.

She thought about the three addresses. São Paulo. Singapore. The encrypted back-channel where a diplomat she refused to call hers checked every six hours.

She thought about Talia's observation — *That's somebody's diplomat* — and the way the ring had pulsed when she said it. Not processing. *Observing.* The distinction that Talia made between data and interpretation, between what crossed her node and what she chose to name, applied to people the same way it applied to information. Talia had observed a traffic pattern. She had named it. The naming changed nothing about the pattern and everything about how Mirelle heard it.

Every six hours. Weeks.

She pressed her thumb against the tragus of her right ear. Force of habit. The cartilage deformed. The ringing continued — steady, bilateral, the sound her body had manufactured from eleven weeks of proximity to infrastructure that had been carrying nitro since before the Sump was the Sump and would be carrying it after the Sump was whatever the architecture was being redesigned to make it.

The dead-hand timer said seventy-two hours. The architecture held. The data was outside her now — distributed, redundant, housed in a network she had built with the same precision that had built the flood valves and the pressure doors and the tolerance bands that measured what the institutions were willing to acknowledge.

The difference was that her architecture was designed to open.

Seventy-two hours. Then seventy-two more.

She closed her eyes. The hum carried her toward something that was not sleep and not wakefulness but the state between them where the ringing and the vibration merged into a single frequency her body produced and the infrastructure echoed and neither source could be distinguished from the other.
