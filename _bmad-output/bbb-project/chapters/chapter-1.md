## Draft v1

The Celestial Ballroom occupied the 114th floor of NitroCore Tower, and Aurielle Vasquez had been smiling for two hours.

Not the smile she used in private — the quick, involuntary thing that surfaced when she read something clever or caught her reflection doing something undignified. This was the other one. The institutional smile. Warm enough to register on camera, controlled enough to mean nothing. Her father had taught her this smile when she was fourteen, standing in front of a mirror in the Singapore compound while he adjusted her posture from behind.

*Chin level. Shoulders back. The smile is for the room, not for the person. If they think it's for them, they'll want more of it.*

She'd hated the lesson. She'd learned it anyway.

The ballroom was full. Three hundred and twelve guests — she'd reviewed the list twice, memorized the names she needed, flagged the ones her father had flagged. The NitroCore Annual Innovation Gala, which was not about innovation and never had been. It was a census. Who showed up. Who sat where. Who spoke to whom during the first reception hour, when the conversations were still strategic and the alcohol hadn't softened anyone into honesty.

Aurielle moved through it the way she'd been taught. A hand on a forearm here. A question about someone's daughter there. *Ask about their children. People reveal their ambitions through their children.* Eduardo's rule. She'd never decided whether it was cynical or observant. Both, probably. The distinction mattered less than the result.

"Aurielle, you look stunning tonight." Director Okafor, Helix Energy, leaning in with his third glass of something amber. His smile was broader than hers and meant even less.

"Director. How's the Mombasa expansion?"

"On schedule. Your father's licensing terms are — well." He lifted the glass. "Robust."

"He prefers 'thorough.'"

"I'm sure he does."

She held eye contact one beat longer than comfortable — Eduardo's technique, not hers, borrowed the way you borrow a coat that fits well enough — and excused herself. Okafor would remember the conversation as pleasant. He would not remember what she'd learned from it: the Mombasa expansion was not on schedule. People who said *on schedule* without being asked were managing expectations.

She cataloged it. Filed it beside the twelve other small readings she'd taken tonight — the PCC trade liaison who wouldn't make eye contact with the NitroCore general counsel, the Chimera Collective delegation that had arrived as a group and hadn't separated once, the empty chair at Table 6 where the GCTA observer was supposed to be sitting. Data. Her father would want the debrief tomorrow. He always wanted the debrief.

The podium was being prepared at the far end of the ballroom. Eduardo's speech — the keynote, the reason for the evening's real attendance. He'd been working on it for weeks, which meant he'd written it in an hour and spent the remaining weeks deciding which version of himself to perform. The visionary. The steward. The patriarch. He had versions the way other men had suits, and he selected them with the same deliberate precision.

She checked the time on her frame — a gesture so habitual she barely registered the AR overlay. 21:47. The speech was at 22:00. She was supposed to join him on stage for the introduction. The dutiful daughter. The heir apparent. The visual confirmation that NitroCore's succession was secure, generational, inevitable.

She didn't resent it. That was the troubling part. She'd expected to resent it — the choreography, the performance, the implication that her primary value was continuity. But standing in this room full of people whose names she'd memorized and whose motives she could read, she felt something closer to fluency. She was good at this. She understood the grammar of it. The smiles, the silences, the calibrated pauses. The way a room reorganized itself around power.

She moved toward the stage.

Eduardo was already there, adjusting his cuffs. He wore the same suit he always wore to galas — charcoal, bespoke, cut to minimize his shoulders and maximize his height. The polished oxfords. She noticed them every time. They were the one vanity he permitted himself — hand-made, actual leather, maintained by a craftsman in the Mid-Levels who'd been resoling the same pair for eleven years. In a world of nanite-fabricated everything, Eduardo Vasquez polished his own shoes. He said it was discipline. She suspected it was theater. The CEO who still uses his hands.

He saw her approaching and did the thing he always did — the quick assessment. Eyes up, posture check, a micro-nod that meant *acceptable*. He'd yelled at her this morning about her shoes. *Those lack authority. Change them.* She'd changed them. The new ones pinched at the heel, and she'd spent the evening shifting her weight to compensate, and he hadn't noticed because the point was never comfort. The point was that weakness was a scent.

"Ready?" he said.

"I reviewed the remarks. The line about the Jakarta facility—"

"Stays." He adjusted his cuffs again. A tell she'd never pointed out to him. When Eduardo adjusted his cuffs twice, he was nervous. Not about the speech. About something he hadn't told her.

"Noted."

She took her seat on the stage. A row of high-backed chairs arranged behind the podium — the visual architecture of institutional support. Thorne was already seated two chairs to her left, his hands folded in his lap, his expression arranged into that same respectful attention he'd held all evening. It hadn't shifted once. Most people couldn't maintain a single expression for two hours without a crack. Thorne could maintain one for years. Beyond him, the rest of the executive delegation — Director Chen-Nakamura, the CFO, two board liaisons whose names she'd memorized but whose faces had already begun to blur into the ambient corporate backdrop.

Eduardo stepped to the podium. The room settled. He began to speak.

She didn't listen. She'd heard him rehearse it in his study, pacing between the desk and the window, his reflection doubling him in the glass. The steward voice tonight — warm, measured, the version that made shareholders feel safe. She knew the cadence so well it had become architecture, something she lived inside rather than listened to.

Instead she was thinking about the empty chair at Table 6. The GCTA observer who hadn't come. She was thinking about the Chimera delegation and their coordinated arrival. About Okafor's *on schedule* and the insurance implications of the Mombasa expansion's real timeline. Her mind did this — collected the evening's data points and sorted them while the rest of her performed presence, posture correct, expression attentive, eyes on her father's back while her thoughts were three moves ahead in a game she hadn't yet identified.

Eduardo's voice was a frequency she'd stopped parsing. The words arrived as rhythm without content — stress patterns, pauses, the slight drop in register he used before a key phrase. She'd heard it a thousand times. She could feel the audience responding, the collective lean forward, the held breath before the applause line. She felt nothing. She was thinking about the quarterly projections and whether—

---

Salt.

That was the first thing. The taste of salt — not sweat, not the residue of the evening's hors d'oeuvres, but something thicker, warmer, coating her lips and the inside of her mouth. She swallowed before she understood what she was swallowing.

Then the dress.

Not blood. Not at first. At first it was: wrong color, wrong texture, wrong temperature. The shimmer-silk had been pearl white. Now it was drenched across the bodice in thick, dark crimson, and there were things caught in the delicate lace — pieces of — soft, grey, warm —

She looked up.

Eduardo was still at the podium. Still standing. His hands still gripped the edges of the lectern with white-knuckled force, as if he were simply pausing for dramatic effect. But where his face had been — above the jaw, above the collar of the charcoal suit she'd watched him adjust twenty minutes ago — there was a smoking ruin. The thermal round hadn't just killed him. It had erased him from the nose up.

Behind him, the holographic display still scrolled: *EDUARDO VASQUEZ — VISIONARY OF THE YEAR.*

The room was — she couldn't parse the room. A sea of tuxedos and gowns surging toward the exits. Drone cameras swiveling from the podium to the stampede and back. She couldn't locate the report that must have preceded this — the shot, the crack, whatever sound a thermal round makes when it crosses a ballroom. It wasn't in her memory. She reached for it and found nothing — not a gap but an absence, as if the event had happened in a frequency she didn't process.

*Not a sound she missed. A sound that didn't exist for her.*

She was sitting. She was still sitting in the high-backed chair, and she had pieces of her father on her dress, and the holographic display was still scrolling his name, and he was still standing because the lectern was holding him up. She looked at her hands. Blood. Not hers. She lifted her right hand and watched a drop fall from her fingertip to the stage floor. It hit without sound.

She smelled copper and burnt hair.

A hand on her arm.

Thorne. Already standing, already beside her — two chairs, he'd crossed two chairs — his grip on her upper arm firm enough to anchor without bruising. His suit was clean. She registered this from a distance, the way you register a data point before you understand its implications. Two chairs away from a thermal round and his suit was *clean.*

He was holding a handkerchief. White. Pristine. Folded into a precise square, offered with the hand that wasn't holding her arm. The fabric was bright against his dark sleeve, impossibly clean in a room that smelled like copper and burnt hair.

"Wipe your face," he said.

His voice was level. Not calm — calm implied an alternative. Level. As if the volume and register had been selected in advance and nothing that had occurred required adjustment.

She didn't move.

"Aurielle." Closer now, his mouth near her ear, the grip tightening by a fraction. "The cameras are recording. Every feed in this room is live. You are sitting on a stage covered in your father and three hundred people are watching you decide what to do next."

She took the handkerchief. The fabric was silk — actual silk, not synthetic, the kind that cost more than most people in the Sump earned in a month. It was soft against her face. She wiped her mouth first. Then her cheeks. Then the left side of her neck where the blood had started to dry and the handkerchief caught against her skin.

"Stand up," he said. "Walk. Don't run."

She looked at the podium. Eduardo was — the lectern was still holding him. The polished oxfords braced against the stage floor, the only part of him that looked the way it had five minutes ago. Blood was pooling beneath them, dripping onto the lip of the stage. Then gravity took over.

"Don't look. Walk."

She walked.

---

The corridor outside the Celestial Ballroom was ninety meters long and lined with commissioned art that NitroCore rotated quarterly. Aurielle had reviewed the current installation three weeks ago — a series of light sculptures by a PCC-funded artist who worked exclusively with nitro-infused glass. The pieces cast amber refractions across the walls, warm and clean, and she walked through them with Thorne's hand on her arm and her father's blood drying on her dress.

Security materialized around them. Four, then six. The head of the executive protection detail — Vasquez-assigned, she'd known his name this morning but it was gone now, filed somewhere her mind couldn't access — spoke into a comm unit in clipped syllables. Words reached her in fragments. *Perimeter. Contained. Airspace.* Protocol language. The institution was responding the way institutions respond: with procedure.

"Board Resolution 2175-003," Thorne said. He wasn't speaking to her. He was speaking to the comm channel through his frame, his voice the same level register, as if reading a contract clause. "Emergency succession. No quorum required. Effective immediately upon confirmation of—" A pause. Not hesitation. Selection. "Upon confirmation of the event."

*The event.* Her father's face erased by a thermal round was *the event.* The blood cooling on her collarbone was a consequence of *the event.* Already, the language was building a container for it — classification, resolution numbers, protocol chains. The institution was metabolizing Eduardo Vasquez the way a system metabolizes an input: efficiently, without sentiment, toward output.

"Where are we going?" Her voice sounded wrong. Too steady. The words had formed without her deciding to speak them, assembled by a part of her brain that was still following Eduardo's rules: *When you don't know what to say, ask a question. It buys time and sounds like leadership.*

"Private suite. 116th floor. Medical is meeting us there."

"I'm not hurt."

"Medical is meeting us there," Thorne repeated. His hand hadn't moved from her arm.

They turned a corner. More art. More amber light. She was leaving a trail — not visible on the dark corridor carpet, but she could feel the blood on her shoes, the slight adhesion with each step, the way the shimmer-silk clung to her skin where it was wettest. The dress was ruined. An absurd thought. The most expensive garment she owned, bespoke, fitted in a session that had taken four hours and involved a designer who spoke exclusively in fabric weights and drape angles. Ruined. She would never wear it again. She would never be able to look at shimmer-silk without—

"There will be theories," Thorne said. "A rival. A fanatic. A disgruntled employee. It doesn't matter yet." He spoke the way he always spoke — declarative, sequential, each sentence a brick laid precisely atop the last. "What matters is the next twelve hours. The board will want a statement. I'll draft options. You'll choose one. We'll schedule a private session with each board member before anything goes public."

"My father is dead."

"Yes."

She waited for more. The qualification, the condolence, the human thing that should follow that statement. Thorne walked beside her, his hand on her arm, his stride matched to hers, his expression unchanged.

"The King is dead," he said. Not to her. To the corridor, to the institution, to the cameras that were certainly still recording because the cameras were always recording. "That means you are the Queen." He looked at her then — the first time his eyes had met hers since the stage. "And Queens do not die in the crossfire, Aurielle. They survive to settle the score."

The words landed in her chest like stones dropped into still water. Not because they were comforting. Because they were *ready.* He'd had them prepared. The handkerchief, the corridor, the board resolution, the draft statement, the phrase — all of it assembled before the thermal round had finished its trajectory. As if he'd been carrying these responses the way a surgeon carries instruments: sterilized, ordered, waiting for the incision.

The thought surfaced — *He knew* — and she sealed it. Pressed it into a compartment she didn't have a name for yet, a partition that would hold the suspicion alongside the dependence, the grief alongside the calculation, the blood alongside the white silk of his handkerchief. She sealed it because she needed him. Because the institution required continuity and Thorne was continuity's architect. Because her father was dead and the man who might have arranged it was the only person in the building who had told her what to do next instead of screaming.

"Welcome to the throne, my dear." His voice was quiet now. Almost gentle. Almost. "Try not to slip."

---

The private suite on the 116th floor had been Eduardo's. She could tell because the air smelled like sandalwood — his cologne, absorbed into the upholstery over years of late-night briefings and early-morning departures. NitroCore's atmospheric systems scrubbed everything — allergens, particulates, the faintest trace of the hum that permeated every other altitude of the city — but they couldn't scrub scent from leather.

She was alone. Thorne had left with the security detail. Medical had come and gone — a quick scan, vitals within range, no injuries detected. *No injuries detected.* She had her father's blood on her body and the system found nothing wrong.

The room was quiet. Not silent — silence didn't exist in Neo-Shanghai, even at Spire altitude. There was always the faint hum of the environmental systems, the subliminal pulse of the conduit network far below, the sound of a building that was itself a living instrument of the grid. But the quiet was a different quality than the ballroom's noise. Thicker. More private. The kind of quiet that allowed the sounds you'd been suppressing to surface.

She stood in the center of the room and rotated her left wrist. Slowly. A phantom ache — the ghost of a fracture she'd sustained at eight, falling from a climbing structure in the compound gardens. Healed perfectly. The nanites had seen to that. But the habit remained: when the world exceeded her capacity to process it, her left wrist reminded her that she'd been broken before and the break had been repaired so completely that only she knew it had happened.

The handkerchief was in her right hand. She hadn't let go of it. White silk, now streaked with rust and copper where her face had been. She looked at it the way she'd been trained to look at financial statements — reading the data, not the story. Blood. Silk. A gift from a man whose suit had been clean when everyone else's world was ending.

How had his suit been clean?

Three hundred and twelve people in a ballroom. A thermal round — military grade, that much was obvious from the — from what it did. From *the event.* High angle, based on the splatter pattern, which she was analyzing now because her mind had decided that analysis was preferable to the alternative, which was the shoe, the oxford, the sole facing partially upward, and the foot still—

She stopped.

She looked at the handkerchief. She folded it. Carefully, deliberately, matching the creases to the original folds, pressing the silk flat with her thumbs until it was a precise square again. Smaller. Stained, but contained. She put it in the pocket of her ruined dress.

She did not look at the mirror on the far wall. She knew what she would see. A woman in a ruined gown, blood in her hair, blood on her hands, standing in a room that smelled like her dead father's cologne. A woman who had walked when she was told to walk. Who had wiped her face when she was told to wipe her face. Who had not cried, not screamed, not run — because the cameras were recording, and the cameras were always recording, and the lesson her father had taught her in front of a mirror when she was fourteen was the last lesson he would ever teach her.

*The smile is for the room, not for the person.*

She wasn't smiling. She wasn't anything. She was standing in a room that smelled like sandalwood, holding the shape of herself together the way you hold water in cupped hands — carefully, provisionally, aware that the first tremor would lose it.

The small voice in the back of her mind — the one that calculated, that optimized, that her father would have recognized as his own — was already asking the question she would spend the rest of her life answering.

*What happens now?*

She knew what happened now. Board Resolution 2175-003. Emergency succession. No quorum required. By morning she would be the youngest CEO in NitroCore's history, appointed by blood on shimmer-silk and the quiet architecture of a man who had handed her a white handkerchief at the precise moment she needed one.

Her wrist had stopped rotating. Her hands were still.

She left the handkerchief in her pocket. She would have it cleaned. She would fold it. She would place it in a drawer in the office that still smelled like sandalwood cologne. She would never use it again. She would never throw it away.

Outside the window, 114 floors above the city that hummed beneath her, the Spires caught the last of the evening light and held it — amber and clean, the color of refined nitro, the color of the grid her father had built and her father's father had designed and she had just inherited in the time it took for a thermal round to cross a ballroom.

She didn't look down. She couldn't see the Sump from here. Nobody could.
