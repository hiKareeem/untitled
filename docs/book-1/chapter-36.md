# Chapter 36
## Projected Casualties

> "In the event that projected casualties exceed the threshold for standard institutional review, the authorizing officer shall execute the relevant authorization instrument in private session. The document shall bear a single signature. No countersignature is required."
>
> — *NitroCore Crisis Management Protocol*, Rev. 8, §14.3

---

The alternatives analysis was eleven pages.

Aurielle had read it from the last page first, as she read everything Thorne prepared — the conclusion before the construction, the answer before the argument, the institutional architecture in reverse so that by the time she reached the first page she understood not just what the document said but why it had been built in the sequence it had been built. Eduardo's method. The only inheritance that did not require a board resolution.

The conclusion was on page eleven: *No viable alternative to Protocol BLACKWEIR has been identified within the operational parameters established by the Confluence contingency assessment. Recommendation: proceed to formal authorization.*

She had known this before Thursday. She had known it in Sub-level 7, when Thorne had said the word and the small voice had completed the arithmetic before he finished explaining the flood valve specifications. She had known it when she typed the formal request for alternatives — *Request: Alternatives Analysis — BLACKWEIR Protocol, Confluence Contingency* — in the institutional language, on the secured terminal, with the pen parallel to the outbox tray and the sandalwood already gone from the office that smelled like a room. The request had been genuine. The alternatives would be inadequate. She had known both things at once, the way the institution held both classifications at once, the way the Chair held both the cause and the cost in the same mind because the mind was the only place where the complete picture existed.

Eleven pages. Four alternatives. Each one modeled with the precision Thorne applied to everything — engineering specifications, projected timelines, resource requirements, casualty estimates formatted in the tabular language the institution used for numbers that were also people. Each one arriving at the same conclusion through a different institutional dialect. Partial evacuation: triggers mass transit disruption, accelerates the Confluence, estimated Spire casualties exceed BLACKWEIR's Sump projections by a factor of three. Full rehabilitation: thirty-six months minimum, the grid cannot sustain the throughput reduction, economic cascade across the Pacific Corporate Coalition. Enhanced containment: delays the Confluence by eight to fourteen weeks, does not prevent it, doubles the eventual casualty projection. Managed attrition: the original Scenario C, now inoperative — the step-function had collapsed the timeline from months to weeks.

Four alternatives. One conclusion. The document proved she had considered every option. The document was designed to prove she had considered every option. That was its function — not analysis but record, not exploration but documentation of the exploration that produced the predetermined result. The alternatives analysis was the institutional artifact that would exist in the LP9 archive beside the authorization, evidence that the Chair had exercised due diligence before exercising the authority the Chair existed to exercise.

She set the document on the conference table. Aligned the edges with the authorization form that waited beside it — Form CMP-7, the crisis management instrument she had never seen before Thorne placed it in the evening brief three days ago. Single page. Single signature line. The classification header read LP9. The casualty projection field was blank — a formatted space, three centimeters wide, labeled *Projected Impact (Designated Containment Perimeter)* in the institutional typeface that made every field on every form look the same whether the field contained a maintenance budget or a death count.

The field was blank because the number had not yet been entered. The number would not be entered.

---

The conference room was on the 108th floor. Not the boardroom — smaller, adjacent, the room Eduardo had used for classified briefings that required more than two chairs but fewer than twelve. Dark wood paneling. Recessed lighting. The carpet that absorbed footsteps the way the institution absorbed everything: efficiently, without record. A rectangular table, eight chairs, a holographic display surface recessed into the table's center. The room was designed to absorb rather than transmit — sound dampened by the paneling, light managed by the recessed fixtures, the temperature held at 21.2 degrees because the building still ran on Eduardo's settings and no one had changed them because no one changed what the dead man's architecture had established.

Five people. Aurielle at the head. Thorne to her left — standing, the presenter's position, the same posture he held in Sub-level 7 and would hold in any room where the real numbers were being delivered to the person who held both classifications. Harding from NitroCore Legal sat at the midpoint of the table — a man she had met twice, whose function was to confirm that the authorization instrument met the classification protocol's requirements and whose presence in this room meant that the institution's legal architecture had reviewed the document and found it structurally sound. The grid management liaison — the same woman whose name Aurielle had read on a briefing roster and processed as a title rather than a person. And Okoro, the security director — a man whose job was to confirm that the perimeter enforcement protocols were staged and operational.

Five people in a room designed for eight. The empty chairs were not symbolic. They were institutional — the room was this size because this was the room, and the room did not adjust for the number of people the briefing required.

"The alternatives analysis has been distributed," Thorne said.

He did not activate the holographic display. The briefing was not a presentation — not the Sub-level 7 model with its volumetric reconstruction and its color-coded maintenance overlays and its absorption scatter plots that made the Sump visible as geography. This was an authorization meeting. The data had been presented in previous sessions. The analysis had been read. The conclusion had been reached. What remained was procedural.

"All four scenarios have been modeled against the current Confluence acceleration data," he continued. "The VEC's monitoring stations confirm the convergence is tracking the projected timeline. Simultaneous multi-point breach remains estimated at three to five weeks under current conditions."

Three to five weeks. The number had narrowed since Sub-level 7. The Confluence was not waiting for the institution to complete its procedures.

"Partial evacuation," Thorne said. "Transit infrastructure cannot sustain the population displacement without triggering the throughput variance that accelerates breach propagation. The model projects that evacuation of Sectors 7 through 9 alone — the highest-risk residential zones — would reduce conduit network stability below the threshold for controlled protocol execution. The window for BLACKWEIR closes before the evacuation completes."

Harding made a note on his datapad. The legal record. The institutional documentation that someone in this room had stated the evacuation constraint and someone else had recorded it.

"Full rehabilitation," Thorne continued. "Timeline: thirty-six months minimum. The Confluence timeline is three to five weeks. This option is not operative."

"Enhanced containment," Aurielle said.

Thorne looked at her. The managed surface. The assessment that had been running since the gala, since the handkerchief, since the first brick of the first wall.

"Delays the Confluence by eight to fourteen weeks. Does not prevent activation. Doubles the projected casualty figure when activation occurs, because the delayed breach propagates through a more degraded conduit network." He paused — not for effect, for selection. "Enhanced containment is the option that produces the worst outcome on a longer timeline."

"And Scenario C," she said.

"Inoperative since the step-function. The managed attrition timeline assumed gradual escalation. The Confluence is not gradual."

The room held the silence. The hum was there — managed, compensated, the executive floor's version of the sound that was louder in Sub-level 7 and loudest in the Sump where thirteen million people lived above the infrastructure that three generations of Vasquezes had classified as expendable.

"The evacuation question," Aurielle said. "For the record."

Harding's stylus paused over his datapad. The grid management liaison straightened — the posture of a person who understood that *for the record* meant the next exchange would be documented in the LP9 archive as formal institutional deliberation.

"If we evacuate the full Sump population prior to BLACKWEIR execution," Aurielle said. Her voice was level. The Chair's voice — the register that said *status* and *containment* and *the numbers* with the same institutional surface. "What is the projected outcome?"

Thorne did not hesitate. The answer was prepared — had been prepared since before the alternatives analysis, since before the step-function, since the moment someone in Sub-level 7 had modeled the evacuation scenario and found the same result Eduardo's team had found and Eduardo's father's team had found before that.

"Full evacuation requires seventy-two hours minimum for the Sump's residential population — thirteen million, current census. Transit infrastructure operates at maximum throughput during evacuation. Maximum throughput generates conduit variance that accelerates breach propagation by a factor of two point four. Under evacuation conditions, the Confluence activates in approximately thirty-one hours — forty-one hours before evacuation completes. At activation, the absorption gradient propagates upward through the unsevered junction network into the Mid-Levels and the Spires. Grid failure within ninety minutes. Projected casualties: the full population of Neo-Shanghai's vertical architecture."

Forty-seven million. He did not say that number either. The institutional language contained it — *full population of Neo-Shanghai's vertical architecture* — the way the institutional language contained everything, by describing the shape without naming the count.

"The math," Aurielle said. "Is the same."

"The math is the same," Thorne confirmed. "Evacuate and the Confluence reaches the Spires. Don't evacuate and it reaches the Sump. The variable is which population is within the containment perimeter when the protocol executes."

She looked at the table. The alternatives analysis. The authorization form. The blank field where the number would not be written. Five people in a room on the 108th floor of a tower that rose above a city that rose above the Sump that held thirteen million people who could not be evacuated because evacuating them would kill forty-seven million more.

"The analysis is complete," she said. "Thank you."

Harding closed his datapad. The grid management liaison gathered her materials — a single folder, classified, the kind of document that would be filed in the LP9 archive and accessed by no one unless the archive was subpoenaed by an institution with jurisdiction over NitroCore's classification protocols, and no such institution existed because the classification architecture had been designed to ensure that no such institution could exist. The security director stood, nodded — the gesture of a man whose function in this room was complete and whose function in the next room was staging the perimeter enforcement that would seal the Sump when the authorization transmitted.

They filed out. One by one. The door opened to each person's biometrics and closed behind them with the hydraulic whisper of institutional architecture performing its designed function. Harding. The liaison. The security director. Three people who had sat in a room and processed the authorization of thirteen million deaths as a procedural matter and who would return to their offices and continue the work the institution required because the institution required the work to continue and the work was what they did.

The door closed.

---

Two people. The conference room held the silence the way it held everything — absorbed, dampened, the paneling and the carpet and the managed temperature converting sound into the institutional quiet that made the executive floors feel like a different city than the Sump two hundred meters below. The hum was there. Faint. The frequency the building compensated for so thoroughly that the compensation itself became the sound — the silence that was not silence but the grid working at a register the institution had tuned below the threshold of attention.

Thorne remained standing. The same position — to her left, slightly behind, the presenter's posture, the posture of the man who built the corridor and watched the walking. He had not moved during the meeting. He had not adjusted his stance or shifted his weight or displayed any of the small physical negotiations that bodies performed during the institutional rituals of corporate governance. Thorne's body did not negotiate. Thorne's body occupied space the way the building occupied space — structurally, with the certainty of architecture that had been designed for exactly this load.

"The number," Aurielle said.

Her voice was the same. Level. The register that had said *the evacuation question, for the record* and *the analysis is complete* with the same institutional surface. The training was not training anymore. The training was architecture — Eduardo's architecture, Thorne's architecture, the institution's patient decades of producing a person who could sit in this room and say *the number* and mean: tell me how many people I am about to kill.

Thorne looked at her. The managed neutrality held. The aperture behind it — the assessment, the recognition, the patience that had waited for Eduardo and now waited for her — held.

"Thirteen million," he said. "Within the designated containment perimeter. Full residential population of the affected sectors."

The number was in the air. Not on paper. Not in the institutional language of *projected impact* and *designated containment perimeter* and the formatted field on Form CMP-7 that would remain blank because the number was too large for the field and too large for the classification protocol and too large for the institutional architecture that had been designed to contain numbers in the range of eight to fourteen thousand over a thirty-six-month managed attrition timeline. Thirteen million was not a managed attrition number. Thirteen million was the entire Sump. Every sector. Every block. Every junction. Every person who lived in the cheapest housing in the deepest levels of a city that hummed with the sound of the thing beneath them.

She held the number. Not the way she had held Sector 9's forty-seven percent in Sub-level 7 — with the analytical distance of a person processing data. Not the way she had held eight to fourteen thousand — with the institutional framing that made the number feel like a budget line. She held thirteen million the way she held the carbon key. The way she held the pen. The way she held every object the institution had placed in her hands that was heavier than its weight.

The small voice did not speak. The small voice had been running the arithmetic since Sub-level 7 — since the three scenarios, since the Confluence, since the step-function collapsed the timeline and the alternatives analysis confirmed what the arithmetic had already produced. The voice had run every model. Every projection. Every scenario and sub-scenario and second-order cascade that the strategic intelligence Eduardo had installed and Thorne had sharpened could generate. The voice had run them all and arrived at the same conclusion from every direction, and the conclusion was the number Thorne had just said aloud in a room where two people stood and the sound was absorbed by the paneling and the carpet and the institutional architecture that had been designed to absorb exactly this.

The arithmetic was complete.

The voice had nothing left to calculate.

"The document," Thorne said.

He did not gesture toward it. The authorization form sat on the table where she had placed it — Form CMP-7, single page, single signature line, the casualty projection field blank. The field would remain blank. The number existed in this room, in the air between two people, spoken once. The document would record the authorization without recording the cost — the signature without the arithmetic, the decision without the number, the institutional instrument performing its designed function of separating the act from its consequence so that the archive contained the authority and the authority contained nothing that could be read as a count.

Thorne walked to the door. His hand on the biometric reader. He paused — not the way he paused during briefings, for selection, for the placement of the next brick. He paused the way a man pauses at a threshold he has stood at before, with other people, in other decades, when the room behind him held a different person sitting at a table with a document that required a single signature.

"Eduardo signed the Phase Two authorization in this room," he said. "1:47 AM. He asked me to leave first."

She looked at him. The managed surface. The same man. The same suit. The same institutional architecture that had produced Eduardo and produced her and would produce the next one.

"I know," she said.

He left. The door closed behind him. The hydraulic whisper. The institutional quiet.

---

She was alone.

The room was the room — dark wood, recessed lighting, the table with eight chairs and five that had been occupied and three that had not. The holographic display surface was dark. The alternatives analysis sat where she had placed it, edges aligned, eleven pages that documented the exhaustion of every option that was not the option she was about to authorize. The authorization form sat beside it. Form CMP-7. Single page. The signature line at the bottom — a printed line, four inches, with her title beneath it in the institutional typeface: *Chair, NitroCore Corporation.*

The pen was in her jacket. The same pen — NitroCore institutional, weighted, blue ink, the pen that had signed the maintenance deferrals in Eduardo's office while Thorne discussed containment metrics and the sandalwood was thinning. The pen that had been parallel to the outbox tray when she typed the request for alternatives. The pen that had been Eduardo's before it was hers, the way the desk was Eduardo's and the office was Eduardo's and the Chair was Eduardo's and the institution was Eduardo's — inherited, transferred, the same instrument performing the same function in a different hand.

She placed the pen on the table beside the form. The sound was small. The room absorbed it.

Her left wrist was still. It had been still since Sub-level 7 — since Thorne had said *BLACKWEIR* and the small voice had completed the calculation before the word finished and the wrist had rotated once and she had stopped it and it had not rotated since. The body had processed this before the institution required it. The body had arrived at the conclusion the way the arithmetic had arrived at the conclusion — not through decision but through the exhaustion of alternatives, the systematic elimination of every other state until the only remaining state was the state the system had been designed to produce.

She picked up the pen. Uncapped it. The click was louder than it should have been in a room designed to absorb rather than transmit.

The signature line. Four inches of printed rule on institutional paper. The same line Eduardo had signed at 1:47 AM in this room, alone, after asking Thorne to leave. The same line Eduardo's father had signed in the same room — the Phase One authorization, the charges and the flood valves, the infrastructure that had been built to kill and classified as routine maintenance components and maintained by technicians who walked past them every shift without knowing what they were walking past.

Three signatures. Three generations. The same room. The same arithmetic. The same conclusion, reached by three people who had each descended to the floors where the real numbers lived and each returned to the floors where the quarterly projections were excellent and each signed the document that separated the authority from the cost.

She signed.

The pen moved across the line — her name, her title, the date. The sound of ink on paper. Not loud. Not the dramatic gesture the moment should have contained. A signature. The same motion she performed on maintenance deferrals and budget authorizations and the quarterly reports that crossed her desk in the institutional rhythm Eduardo had established and she had inherited. The pen completed the name. She lifted it. Capped it. Set it beside the form.

The sound of the cap was the loudest thing in the room.

She sat for a moment. Her hands flat on the table. The same posture — the stillness that had held in Sub-level 7 and held in every quarterly and held in this room while the alternatives were presented and the evacuation question was asked for the record and the number was spoken aloud and Thorne had walked to the door and paused and told her about Eduardo's 1:47 AM and left.

The small voice was silent. Not suppressed. Not running calculations she could not hear. Silent the way the room was silent — the absence that was not absence but the sound of a system that had completed its function. The arithmetic was finished. The scenarios were exhausted. The alternatives were documented. The number was spoken. The signature was on the line.

There was nothing left to do.

She stood. Placed the pen in her jacket. Collected the alternatives analysis and the signed authorization form — the eleven pages and the single page, the documentation and the instrument, the proof that due diligence had been performed and the act that due diligence had produced. She would file them. The LP9 archive. The classification that Eduardo had built and she had inherited and that would hold these pages beside the quarterly projections and the containment expenditure summaries and the hours that appeared on no schedule.

She walked to the door. The biometric reader registered her palm. The door opened to the corridor — dark wood paneling, recessed lighting, thirty meters to Eduardo's office. The same corridor she had walked after the gala, Thorne's hand on her arm. The same corridor she had walked after Sub-level 7, alone, the numbers settling. The same corridor she had walked after the Confluence briefing, the pen parallel to the outbox tray, the sandalwood gone.

She walked it now. Alone. Her hands at her sides, the documents in her left, the pen in her jacket. Her left wrist was still. The hum was there — managed, compensated, the sound of the grid working at the register the institution had tuned below the threshold of attention. The sound she had found beautiful at nine years old. The sound she could hear through now — through the management, through the compensation, through the sixty-three floors of institutional architecture between this corridor and the Sump where thirteen million people lived above the infrastructure that the document in her hand had authorized to kill them.

The corridor was thirty meters. She walked it at the pace of the position — not fast, not slow, the stride of the Chair performing the function the Chair performed. Walking. From one threshold to the next. From the room where the signature was made to the office where the quarterly projections were excellent. From the act to the next act. From the authorization to the filing to the brief to the next meeting to the next quarterly to the next document that would cross her desk in the institutional rhythm that did not pause for the weight of what the rhythm carried.

She reached Eduardo's office. The door opened. She stepped inside. The room smelled like nothing — the sandalwood gone, the atmospheric scrubbers having completed their work, the dead man's last trace processed into particulate and vented through the return. She sat at the desk. Spanish walnut. The chair received her — the leather conforming to something intermediate, not Eduardo's shape, not quite hers, the material still learning the new occupant the way the institution was still learning her.

She placed the authorization form in the LP9 filing tray. She placed the alternatives analysis beside it. She positioned the water glass on her left. Opened the evening brief on her first datapad.

The brief contained the next day's schedule. Meetings. Reports. The operational cadence of NitroCore's executive function, formatted in the institutional language that made every day look like every other day whether the day followed a quarterly review or a maintenance deferral or the authorization of the deaths of thirteen million people.

The hum was there. She could hear it. Not the way she had heard it at nine years old, when the sound was beautiful and the converter station was an educational exercise and Eduardo's hand was on her shoulder. Not the way she had heard it in Sub-level 7, when the sound was operational and the numbers were the truth. She heard it the way the building heard it — as infrastructure, as the frequency the system generated and the system compensated for and the system continued to generate because the system was designed to continue.

She read from the last page first.
