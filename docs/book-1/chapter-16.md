# Chapter 16
## Private Log

> *"We do not ask you to believe us. We ask you to count. Count the names. Count the dates. Count the readings. Then ask yourself what kind of system requires you to stop counting."*
>
> — *The Kindling Letters*, Vol. III

---

The log was not a log.

It was a file nested inside the maintenance-scheduling partition of his service-issue frame — the partition nobody audited because it contained shift rosters and gasket replacement timelines and the accumulated clerical debris of six years of trunk-line inspections that no supervisor had ever opened and no NitroCore data sweep had ever flagged. Fuxi had created it eleven weeks ago. He had given it a filename that looked like a parts requisition — *REQ-4E-GASKET-BATCH-2175-Q3.dat* — and he updated it at the end of every shift, sitting at station 3 in the monitoring bay while the automated systems uploaded the day's readings and Chen Wei powered down his screens with the same sequence of keystrokes he had used for twenty years.

Eleven weeks. Fifty-three entries. Each one a timestamp, an RCI reading, and a decay profile — the numbers the official logs captured, plus the thing the official logs didn't: the *shape* of each correction. Whether the decline from peak to baseline followed the expected logarithmic curve, the messy asymptotic approach of a physical system settling through friction and redundancy. Or whether it was something else.

Linear. Clean. Deliberate.

He had started the log after the third spike.

The first had been the one eleven weeks and four days ago: monitor 6, peak of 30.8, linear descent, twenty-six seconds. Not the highest reading he'd seen — there had been an earlier spike, months back, the one Chen Wei had dismissed as a fluctuation. But that one had entered the official system, aged through the active queue, and been archived. Gone. This one was different because Fuxi was different — because the earlier spike had lodged in his body like a splinter, and when the second one came he was watching for the shape. The second had come nine days later — monitor 6 again, peak of 31.2, linear descent, twenty-two seconds. Faster. The third had come fourteen days after that, and by then Fuxi had stopped believing that the word *fluctuation* contained enough information to describe what was happening.

The third spike was when he started the file.

He sat now at station 3, the shift's data uploading to the grid management system, and opened the parts-requisition file on his cracked frame while the amber light of the monitoring bay turned his hands the same orange as everything else below the Mid-Levels. The file rendered in the standard service text — monospaced, low-resolution, designed for inventory tracking. Each entry was a row. Each row was a shift.

Fifty-three rows.

Not all of them contained spikes. The trunk-line ran clean for days at a stretch — baseline 26 to 28, the steady resonance of nitro moving through first-generation infrastructure at three times its design specification. Normal. The hum constant. The monitors green. Then a spike would come — not on a schedule, not in a pattern Fuxi could predict, but with a frequency that was increasing. In the first four weeks of the log, he'd recorded three anomalous readings. In the last four weeks, seven. The spikes were getting more frequent, and the peaks were climbing — 30.8, 31.2, 31.5, and last week a reading that had touched 32.1 before the linear correction pulled it back to baseline in nineteen seconds.

The corrections were getting faster too. The first linear descent had taken forty seconds. The most recent had taken nineteen. As if whatever was correcting the reading was getting better at it. As if it was *practicing.*

Fuxi scrolled to the bottom of the file and added today's entry. No spike. Baseline throughout. He typed the date, the shift start and end times, the high and low RCI readings, the ambient temperature in the monitoring bay, and the note he added to every entry regardless of whether anything had happened: *Decay profile: N/A. No anomalous reading this shift.*

Then he opened the second section of the file.

This section had no header. No formatting. Just dates and names — the dates copied from the municipal residential advisory that updated on his frame every seventy-two hours, and the names gathered from the corridor. Not the advisory itself, which never contained names. The advisory said things like: *Residential unit 14-7B: vacancy logged. Residential unit 14-3A: vacancy logged. Residential unit 9-12C: vacancy logged.* Vacancy. As if people had moved. As if they had packed their things and walked to the lift and ascended to the Mid-Levels or descended further into the Deep Sump or done anything other than the thing that the advisory's language was designed to make invisible.

Fuxi did not know what had happened to the people in 14-7B and 14-3A and 9-12C. Nobody did. That was the architecture of a vacancy — it was the absence of information shaped like the absence of a person. One day the corridor lights were on behind a frosted polymer door. The next day they were off. The neighbours said *they moved* or *they went to family* or *I think they transferred,* and the advisory said *vacancy logged,* and the shift continued and the hum continued and the trunk-lines carried their cargo and the monitors stayed green.

But Fuxi had the dates. The advisory updated every seventy-two hours, and the vacancy logs had timestamps, and the timestamps could be matched against the entries in the first section of his file.

He had matched them.

Not tonight. Weeks ago. The night after the fifth spike, sitting on the blue textile in the apartment with Nuwa asleep and the conduit glow pulsing through the frosted polymer window, he had opened both sections of the file side by side on the cracked frame display and looked at the numbers.

The correlation was not clean. It was not a one-to-one alignment — spike here, vacancy there, line drawn between them. The world did not work like that. The world worked in noise and approximation and the accumulated mess of systems interacting with other systems in ways that nobody fully mapped. But the correlation was there. Each anomalous RCI spike on monitor 6 — the linear ones, the ones that self-corrected with a precision that physical systems did not possess — fell within forty-eight to seventy-two hours of a vacancy logged in the residential blocks closest to the Resonance District boundary. Not every vacancy had a spike. But every spike had a vacancy.

Every spike had a vacancy.

He had checked it three times. He had reordered the data by date, by block number, by RCI peak value. He had looked for confounders — throughput changes, scheduled maintenance, demand cycles that could explain both the readings and the timing. He had found none. The throughput logs showed nothing unusual. The maintenance schedules were routine. The demand cycles followed their quarterly pattern, and the spikes did not.

The spikes followed something else.

Fuxi closed the file. The upload chime sounded — the shift's data had been transmitted. The monitoring bay screens showed their post-upload confirmation, amber text on amber background, and Chen Wei was already standing, pulling his jacket from the hook, moving through the end-of-shift sequence with the automatism of a man who had performed it seven thousand times and would perform it seven thousand more.

"Quiet shift," Chen Wei said.

"Quiet shift."

Chen Wei nodded. That was the exchange. The daily confirmation that nothing had happened, that the trunk-lines had held, that the monitors had stayed green, that the system had done what the system was designed to do. Chen Wei left. Fuxi heard his boots on the grating, the sound diminishing down the corridor toward the ascent shaft. Then silence — not true silence, never true silence in the Deep Sump, but the relative silence of a monitoring bay after shift change, when the automated systems ran alone and the hum was the only presence.

Fuxi sat for a moment longer. The hum pressed against his sternum. The orange glow turned his hands to instruments of the infrastructure — orange skin, orange nails, the fine dust that settled in the creases of his knuckles and the lines of his palms and that he carried home every night despite the mat. He thought about the file. About the columns of dates and readings and vacancies that sat inside a gasket requisition on a cracked frame in the maintenance-scheduling partition that nobody audited.

He thought about what the file meant.

Not what it proved — it proved nothing. Correlation was not causation. He had heard this phrase in the quarterly safety briefings that NitroCore mandated and the municipal service attended and nobody believed and everybody signed. *Correlation is not causation.* A statistical relationship between two variables does not establish that one produces the other. The spikes and the vacancies could be coincidental. They could be products of a shared underlying cause — increased throughput generating both RCI fluctuations and the conditions that made people leave. They could be noise. They could be the kind of pattern the human brain was evolved to find in random data — the face in the clouds, the signal in the static, the meaning in the meaningless.

But the decay profiles were linear.

Systems didn't do that. Fuxi had maintained trunk-lines for six years. He had watched thousands of fluctuations spike and settle. He knew what a self-correcting system looked like — the oscillation, the overshoot, the messy asymptotic approach. He knew it in his body the way he knew the feel of a properly seated gasket and the sound of a conduit running at spec and the vibration frequency of a trunk-line carrying R1 nitro at peak throughput. Physical systems decayed through friction. They did not draw straight lines from peak to baseline. They did not arrive at the target in nineteen seconds and stop.

Something was correcting those readings. Not the system's redundancy margins. Not the flow regulators. Something that knew what baseline was and chose to return to it with the efficiency of a hand reaching for a switch.

Fuxi stood. Pulled his jacket from the hook. The reflective strips were peeling worse now — the left shoulder strip had detached entirely, and the zipper still stuck at the midpoint. He had stopped filing for replacements.

---

The apartment smelled like broth.

Nuwa was at the heating element, her back to the door, sleeves pushed to her elbows. The violet staining on her forearms had darkened — five months of chemical solvent exposure accumulating in the skin like a record of shifts worked and safety requisitions unfiled and protective equipment that existed in the plant manager's spreadsheet but not on Nuwa's hands. She was stirring something with the concentration she gave to everything: precise, efficient, present.

"Boots," she said.

He stepped onto the mat. Pulled them off. The routine was the same — the mat, the boots, the cold concrete through his socks, the hum in the walls transmitting the conduit network's pulse into the bones of the building. Home frequency.

"I got it," she said, without turning around.

"Got what."

She turned. Her face was doing the thing it did when she had built a case and was waiting for the right moment to present it — the controlled neutrality that preceded the layered argument, each reason stacked precisely to support the next. But tonight there was something else underneath. Something that looked, from the angle where Fuxi stood in his socks on the cold floor of their apartment in the Sump of Neo-Shanghai, like happiness.

"The P-3."

She held up her left arm. The frame was new. Not refurbished-new — the housing was the same matte grey of a Shenlong factory unit, but the edges were clean, the screen uncracked, and the AR overlay that pulsed once in his peripheral vision when she activated it was sharp and stable and free of the artifacts that had plagued her salvaged unit.

"Stall 14," she said. "He held it. Five months. I think he felt sorry for me. Or he couldn't find another buyer. Either way."

Fuxi looked at the frame on her wrist. Five months of savings. Five months of subsidized rations and double shifts and the math that Nuwa had run forward and backward and sideways until the numbers had agreed to cooperate. Five months of the plan — *their* plan, the architecture of small, accumulated sacrifices that was supposed to open the door to the Mid-Level transfer, the QC positions at Yuen's plant, the employer-sponsored transit permit, the forty meters of altitude that measured the distance between the Sump and something less than the Sump.

"The sensory filter?" he asked.

"Works. I tested it on the walk home. Walked past the junction on level 2 — the one that usually scrambles the overlay. Nothing. Clean signal. Full AR." She turned back to the heating element. "My supervisor is going to look like a complete person for the first time in six months."

He almost smiled. The way he almost smiled — the compression, the containment, the expression that reached the muscles of his face and stopped before it arrived at the surface. Not because the monitoring bay was watching. Because the habit of containment had followed him home and settled into his face the way the orange dust settled into the creases of his hands.

Her shoulders had dropped two centimetres. Her jaw had unclenched. She moved through the apartment without the low-grade calculation that usually governed her steps — the unconscious economizing of a body that had learned to spend nothing it didn't have to. She had the frame. The plan was working. The next step was the transfer application — Nuwa had the paperwork drafted, the QC qualification timeline mapped, the transit permit pathway identified. She had built a structure out of the narrow materials available to a woman in the Sump with violet-stained hands and an uneven haircut and a mind that worked in architectures of opportunity. The structure was sound. The math checked.

He sat at the table. She set a bowl in front of him — broth, reconstituted protein, the green thing that might have been bok choy. Steam rose. He ate.

"The application window opens in three weeks," she said, sitting across from him with her own bowl. "Yuen's plant posts the QC positions first internally. If I apply with the P-3 already integrated — functional frame, sensory filter, full AR qualification — I'm ahead of anyone still running salvaged units. The transfer committee reviews on a thirty-day cycle. That puts the decision six weeks out. If they approve, GridLine processes the employer-sponsored transit permit in another twenty days. Two months. Two months and I'm in the Mid-Levels."

She laid it out the way she always laid things out — the bricks, the structure, the pathway rendered in precise steps with precise timelines and the quiet certainty of someone who had done the math so many times that the numbers had become load-bearing.

Fuxi ate and listened and thought about the file.

About the vacancy logged in block 14, unit 7B, sixteen days ago. He knew the tenant — not well, not as a friend, but as a face in the corridor. A man named Guo who worked the water recycling station on level 1. Guo had a loud laugh and a daughter who played in the corridor and a shift pattern that overlapped with Fuxi's ascent from the Deep Sump, so that they passed each other three times a week at the lift doors, Guo going down and Fuxi going up, and the nod between them was the nod of people who shared an infrastructure — the same corridor, the same hum, the same orange glow through the same frosted polymer windows.

Guo's door had been dark for sixteen days. The advisory had said *vacancy logged.* The daughter was not in the corridor. Nobody had said *shimmer.* Nobody had said anything. The absence had the texture of all the absences in the Sump — a silence that occupied the space where a person had been, bounded by the walls of not-asking and not-saying and the municipal advisory's relentless, functional euphemism.

Sixteen days ago, monitor 6 had spiked to 31.5. Linear descent. Twenty-two seconds.

Fuxi ate his broth.

"You're quiet," Nuwa said.

"Standard."

"Quieter than standard." She studied him across the table. The conduit glow came through the frosted polymer window and mixed with the overhead fixture she'd repaired — the light in the apartment was orange and white, and it turned her face into the study in warmth that he remembered from a hundred shared meals and that he was, right now, in this moment, memorizing. Not because he was afraid. Because the file had taught him to count, and counting had taught him to notice, and noticing had made the ordinary things — Nuwa's face, Nuwa's hands, the bowl of broth, the blue textile on the bed, the hum in the walls — heavier. More present. The way objects gained weight when you understood they could be taken.

"Long shift," he said.

She accepted it. Not because she believed him — Nuwa's acceptance was never credulity. It was a choice. The same choice she had made when she said *normal crowd* and he said *normal crowd* and neither of them named the shimmer near the Hum Market. The architecture of well-intentioned silence. He lived inside it. He maintained it. He maintained it the way he maintained the trunk-lines — not because he believed the system was sound, but because the alternative to maintenance was a question he couldn't answer while his sister needed five more weeks.

No. Two months. Two months for the transfer. Two months of the plan working. Two months of Nuwa rising — forty meters, a different plant, better medical, a frame that worked — and him staying in the Deep Sump where the monitors showed what the monitors showed and the file grew by one entry every shift.

He would not tell her about the file.

This was not the old silence — the mutual avoidance, the shared agreement to keep certain observations inside the body. This was different. This was Fuxi holding information that Nuwa did not have and choosing not to give it to her, because giving it to her would make the plan impossible. You could not save for a frame and plan a transfer and build a future forty meters above the Sump while also knowing that the trunk-lines beneath your feet were spiking in correlation with your neighbours going dark. You could not carry both architectures — the architecture of rising and the architecture of counting. One of them had to be set down, and Fuxi had decided, sitting on the blue textile eleven weeks ago with the file open on his cracked frame, that the one he set down would be the one that was his.

He would carry the file. She would carry the plan. He would count. She would climb.

The division was clean. Precise. Linear.

Like a decay profile that wasn't a decay profile.

---

He found the pamphlet on the ascent shaft floor three days later.

Not *found* — the pamphlets were not hidden. They appeared in the corridors and the lift shafts and the market stalls, folded into single sheets of recycled stock that the municipal service periodically collected and the Kindling periodically replaced. Fuxi had seen hundreds of them. He had stepped over them and around them and sometimes picked them up to clear his path the way he picked up any debris in the infrastructure corridors, because debris near trunk-line access points was a maintenance issue and maintenance issues were his job.

He had never read one.

This one was different only because he was different. The pamphlet was the same — single sheet, recycled stock, printed in the Sump's universal font (the one the corridor signage used, the one every frame defaulted to, the font of infrastructure). The text was Kindling text. He recognized the formatting — the centered header, the short paragraphs, the questions that were not questions but invitations to count.

*How many vacancy advisories has your block received this quarter?*

He knew the answer. Seven.

*How many of those vacancies were transfers? How many had transit permits? How many packed their things and walked to the lift and rode it up?*

He knew the answer to that too.

*The Sump is not losing people to opportunity. The Sump is losing people. The distinction is the system's favorite silence.*

Fuxi stood in the ascent shaft with the pamphlet in his hand and the lift rumbling beneath his boots and the trunk-line hum pressing through the walls and into his sternum. He had read Kindling language before — overheard it in the corridors, seen it in the pamphlets he'd stepped over, registered it as political noise generated by a collective that dealt in grievance and rhetoric and the kind of truth that was true but not useful. He had filed it in the same mental partition where he filed the things that were not actionable: the hum's changing texture, the readings that self-corrected, the questions that formed in the back of his mind like frequencies he couldn't quite resolve.

But the pamphlet's language was not noise tonight. Tonight the language had numbers under it — his numbers, the file's numbers, the fifty-three entries and the vacancy dates and the correlation that was not proof but was not nothing. The Kindling said *the Sump is losing people.* Fuxi's file said *the trunk-lines spike before they go.* The two statements were not the same statement. But they occupied adjacent space. They fit together the way a gasket fits a housing — not by design but by the geometry of the problem, because the problem had a shape and the shape required both pieces.

*Sacrifice district.*

He had heard the phrase. Everyone in the Sump had heard it — Kindling vocabulary, political language, the kind of words that floated through the corridors the way the pamphlets floated through the lift shafts. Words that named what the advisory didn't. Words that replaced *vacancy logged* with something that had edges.

Fuxi had never used the phrase. Had never said it aloud, had never let it settle in his mouth, because Kindling language was political and political was dangerous and dangerous was the opposite of what kept Nuwa fed and housed and saving. He maintained the system. The system maintained his sister. The vocabulary of maintenance was the vocabulary of survival: *conduit inspection, RCI reading, scheduled replacement, self-correction, baseline.*

But *sacrifice district* had a precision to it that the maintenance vocabulary lacked. The maintenance vocabulary described what the system did. *Sacrifice district* described what the system was *for.* And Fuxi, standing in the ascent shaft with fifty-three entries on his cracked frame and a pamphlet in his hand, could feel the second vocabulary pressing against the first — not replacing it, not yet, but occupying the space next to it, the way a new harmonic occupies the space next to a fundamental frequency and changes the texture of the sound without changing the note.

He folded the pamphlet. Put it in his jacket pocket, next to Jun's multitool.

The lift arrived. He stepped in. Pressed the contact for Level 0. Felt the cargo lift begin its slow climb through the shaft, the trunk-lines humming on all sides, the resonance changing as he rose — the Deep Sump's bass drone giving way to the Sump's thinner, quicker composite. His body adjusted. It always adjusted. The headache eased. The copper taste faded. The daily recalibration of a man who lived between frequencies.

The pamphlet was in his pocket. The file was on his frame. The words were in his head — *sacrifice district,* two words that sounded like a technical designation, that had the weight and specificity of infrastructure vocabulary, that fit into the same grammatical slot as *conduit junction* and *trunk-line section* and *monitoring bay.* Two words that described a place. His place.

He did not use them aloud. He did not say them to Nuwa when he walked through the door and stepped onto the mat and sat at the table and ate the food she'd made and listened to the update on the transfer application — three weeks to the posting, six weeks to review, two months to the Mid-Levels — and watched her face in the orange-and-white light and carried, in his pocket and on his frame and in the space behind his sternum where the hum lived, the weight of a correlation that was not proof and a vocabulary that was not his.

Not yet.

But the old vocabulary was losing purchase. *Self-correction* was losing purchase. *Fluctuation* was losing purchase. *Baseline* was a word that meant something different now — not the reading the system returned to, but the reading something *chose* to return to, deliberately, linearly, with the precision of a hand reaching for a switch. And *vacancy logged* — the advisory's phrase, the system's phrase, the phrase that turned a disappearance into a clerical event — was a phrase that Fuxi could no longer read without hearing the Kindling's translation underneath it, the way you can't unhear a harmony once someone has pointed it out.

He sat in the apartment and listened to Nuwa talk about the future — the QC positions, the transit permits, the forty meters — and he held the file and the pamphlet and the dead man's multitool and the silence that was not mutual anymore but *his,* his choice, his architecture, the structure he was building to keep her inside the plan while he counted what the plan couldn't hold.

Outside, the conduit glow pulsed. The hum carried through the walls. The municipal advisory had been updated four times this quarter. The monitors in the Deep Sump showed green. The system was working. The system was self-correcting. The readings were within tolerance.

And in a gasket requisition file on a cracked frame in the maintenance-scheduling partition that nobody audited, fifty-three entries said that the system's corrections were too clean and its tolerances were too precise and its vacancies had timestamps that matched its spikes, and a maintenance man in the Sump of Neo-Shanghai sat with the data in his pocket and the pamphlet next to his father's tool and did not speak.
