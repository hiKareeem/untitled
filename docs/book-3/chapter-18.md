# Chapter 18
## This Way

> *"In triage positioning, the responder's role is not diagnosis. It is placement — locating the patient where intervention remains possible and deterioration is observable. The responder does not treat. The responder ensures that the space between the patient and the threshold is a space someone can cross in time."*
>
> — *Field Logistics: Humanitarian Response in Infrastructure-Compromised Zones*, 2168 ed.

---

She counted before she opened her eyes.

Creds: sixty-four. Room: paid through Thursday — nine per day, thirty-six committed, leaving twenty-eight for food and the adhesive tube on day nine of twelve. Rice: two days in the cabinet. Oil: bought yesterday, full bottle, eleven creds. Greens: none. Fen would bring some tonight or she would buy at the market tomorrow.

The route: confirmed. Three days of walking it. The masonry junction on the east side of the Anchor margin district, where two load-bearing walls met at a corner that pre-dated the breach and pre-dated the conduit infrastructure and pre-dated the system that had routed nitro through the substrate beneath it. The junction's stone was dense. Pre-breach construction — poured aggregate and rebar, the old method, the method that attenuated the signal by twelve to fifteen percent depending on wall thickness. She had measured this with the portable RCI monitor Yara's documentation group had lent her — not the tourist model, the industrial one, the kind the conduit maintenance crews used before the maintenance crews were decommissioned and the monitors became consumer goods and the consumer goods became lanyards around the necks of people paying two hundred creds to stand where eight point two million people had been absorbed.

The junction read 12.8 at baseline. The perimeter fencing was fourteen meters east. The observation platforms were sixty meters north — far enough that the tourist foot traffic would not interfere. The masonry walls at the junction rose four meters and provided a sheltered corridor with sight-lines to the Anchor Zone's outer edge. From the junction, the amber conduit glow was visible through the gaps in the perimeter fencing — the orange light that had been the Sump's permanent sky, leaking into the district that had formed around the boundary of the thing that had consumed her neighborhood.

She had mapped this the way she had mapped the buffer zones for Shuang's family. The way she had mapped the relocation corridors for the families in Sector 12. The topology of safety — which walls carried the signal and which walls blocked it, which paths ran through junction-dense corridors and which paths hugged the masonry, which positions allowed a person to stand at the edge of the Anchor Zone and the standing would not destroy them if they stayed less than forty minutes.

Forty minutes. She had calculated this from the advisory threshold data in the public directory, cross-referenced with Fuxi's conduit density logs for the eastern perimeter, adjusted for the twelve-percent attenuation at the masonry junction. Forty minutes at 12.8 RCI with the attenuation buffer. After forty minutes, cumulative exposure climbed past the threshold where the advisory classification shifted from *elevated* to *intervention recommended* and the shift was not gradual — the shift was a step function, the body's tolerance operating like a load-bearing wall that held and held and held and then didn't.

She opened her eyes. The ceiling. The concrete. The hum through the masonry walls — steady, residential-grade. Nineteen months of this hum.

The frozen notification.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

Beside it, three green notifications. She would read them after the rice.

She stood. Brushed her teeth. Boiled water. Made rice. She ate standing at the sink.

---

The adhesive tube was on the shelf. Day nine of twelve. She unsealed the cap and applied the line along the hairline crack. Thumbnail pressed into the split. Counted to thirty. The chip was in the port — Fuxi's thirteen years, the corroded contacts, the junction maps and conduit densities that had become the foundation of every topology she'd built since the first month.

She pulled on her jacket. The mechanical latch clicked behind her.

She stopped at Fen's door. Knocked once.

"Early today," Fen said through the door.

"Someone is coming."

A pause. Then the door opened. Fen looked at her — the brief assessment, the reading of posture and intent that margin residents performed automatically. "Network someone?"

"The mage. The streamer. Kira Calloway."

Fen's expression did not change. Fen did not follow the mage network or the streaming platforms or the wider architecture of information that existed above and around the margin district's daily operations. Fen followed the things that mattered to the building and the market and the families she helped relocate: which corridors were safe, which walls carried what, when the adhesive was on sale at the Level 2 vendor.

"She's coming to the Anchor margin," Nuwa said. "I'm meeting her at the Level 3 transit junction at fourteen hundred."

"Do you need me?"

"No. I need you to know where I am."

Fen nodded. The exchange was complete. The information was what it was — not a request for help, not an invitation, but the protocol they had established without naming it: tell someone where you are going and when you expect to return. The protocol of women who had learned that the system did not track them and that the not-tracking meant nobody would look if they did not come back.

"I'll be at the junction until fifteen hundred," Nuwa said. "If I'm not back by sixteen hundred, the route is the east masonry corridor from the junction to Level 3 transit. Straight north."

"Sixteen hundred," Fen said. "East masonry to Level 3."

Nuwa turned. Walked.

---

The Level 3 transit junction was loud.

The district had grown since her last walk through — three months ago, the walk with Fen that had shown her the observation platforms and the artifact vendors and the food stalls cooking on ambient RCI. The growth was lateral. The stalls had multiplied. The observation platforms had doubled — six now, extending past the perimeter fencing at intervals along the transit way, each one occupied, each one generating the foot traffic and the commerce and the ambient noise of a district that metabolized proximity into economy.

She stood at the junction's southern edge, where the transit way met the corridor that led to the masonry district on the east side. The corridor was quieter. The tourists stayed on the transit way — the platforms, the stalls, the vendor corridor on Level 2 where the polymer fragments were sold and the shimmer tours departed. The masonry district was residential. The masonry district did not sell anything.

She waited.

The rail arrived at 14:07. She heard it — the transit system's arrival tone, three ascending notes, the sound that meant the city was still moving people through corridors designed for the movement of people. The transit junction's foot traffic shifted as the doors opened and the passengers emerged and the passengers were tourists and residents and workers and one person whose hair caught the mixed light of the corridor — amber from the Anchor Zone bleed, white from the institutional illumination — and became something that did not belong to either system.

Blue on the left. Pink on the right. Neon. Shimmering. The synthetic augmentation that caught light the way a signal caught a receiver — designed for it, calibrated for it, impossible to miss.

She was shorter than Nuwa had expected.

The network had not conveyed height. The testimony exchanges, the topology data, the warning message she had sent after watching the stream where the plan was announced — none of it had included the datum that Kira Calloway was five-foot-four and that the five-foot-four was visible only now, in the transit junction, among the tourists and the vendors and the corridor's four-meter ceiling that made everyone small and Kira smaller.

Nuwa raised a hand. Not a wave — a location marker. The hand said *here*, the way her hands said everything: practically, efficiently, without performance.

Kira saw her. Crossed the junction. The walk was fast — the trained movement of a person whose body had been choreographed since childhood, the posture straight, shoulders open, chin level. The performer's carriage. Kira moved through the transit junction the way the amber light moved through the perimeter fencing — filling the space, claiming it, making the space orient toward her without effort and without choice.

"Nuwa."

The voice was different in person. On the network, Kira's messages had been text — precise, formatted, the filing system's architecture applied to communication. In person, the voice was the stream-voice. Warm. Fast. The energy of someone accustomed to narrating their experience as it happened.

"You're — I've been looking at your topology maps for months and I just realized I never actually pictured — you're real. Obviously. But you're *here*. And I'm here. And we've been exchanging data for —"

"Seven months," Nuwa said.

"Seven months. Right." Kira's eyes moved. Both of them — the natural blue right and the pink ocular implant left, the HUD's faint projection visible on the surface of the left eye as scrolling text, the interface processing something Nuwa couldn't read at this distance. The eyes didn't settle. They scanned — the corridor, the junction, the tourists, Nuwa's face, the masonry wall behind her, the amber light from the south, the observation platforms to the north. The scanning was comprehensive. The scanning was fast.

The scanning was too fast.

Nuwa knew this speed. Not from the network. Not from Kira's stream, which she had watched twice — once before sending the message, once after. She knew this speed from the Sump.

The maintenance crews. Deep-shift workers who serviced the conduit infrastructure in the high-RCI corridors where the throughput ran hot and the shifts ran twelve hours and the body required chemical assistance to sustain the focus and the reaction time and the fine-motor precision that the work demanded. The stacks — stimulant compounds, sourced through the Sump's pharmaceutical economy, dosed by weight and adjusted upward as tolerance built. The crews on stacks moved like this. Eyes jumping between focal points with a precision that biology did not produce. Hands steady but restless — the fingers looking for something to do, the motor cortex running hotter than the task required. The speech faster than the content warranted, the processing speed exceeding the processing need.

The crews on stacks filed the best reports. Made the fewest errors. Worked the longest shifts. They were, by every metric the system measured, improving.

The crews on stacks deteriorated. The metrics didn't measure the deterioration because the deterioration happened in the spaces between the metrics — in the affect, in the sleep, in the incremental flattening of the thing that made a person a person rather than a system that processed inputs and generated outputs. The crews got better. The workers got worse. The distinction was invisible from the inside because the stacks included the instruments of self-assessment and the instruments had been recalibrated by the compound and the recalibrated instruments reported: *functional. No adverse effect noted.*

Kira's eyes moved too fast. Kira's voice was too quick. Kira's posture was too electric for a woman who had traveled on civilian rail for nine hours to stand at the edge of the thing that had consumed eight point two million people.

Nuwa did not say this.

"This way," she said.

---

She walked Kira east through the masonry corridor.

Away from the transit junction. Away from the observation platforms and the tourist traffic and the food stalls and the vendor corridor on Level 2 where the polymer fragments were sold. Into the residential grid where the walls were load-bearing and the stone was dense and the hum dropped from the transit way's elevated baseline to the quieter frequency that the masonry produced — not silence, never silence, but the attenuated version that the body could carry without the itch becoming a burn.

Kira was talking. The stream-voice register — narrating as she walked, processing the environment out loud. "The conduit infrastructure is denser than I expected from the maps. The junction nodes — you can feel them, the gradient shifts every thirty meters or so, the R3 picks up the transition as a thermal change in the lower vertebrae —"

"The masonry blocks the gradient," Nuwa said. "Twelve to fifteen percent attenuation depending on thickness. The junction is the lowest point within sight-line of the perimeter."

"Twelve to fifteen. That's — how did you measure that?"

"RCI monitor. Industrial model. I walked the corridor with it for three days."

Kira looked at her. The eyes — both of them, the scanning paused for a moment, the focus settling on Nuwa's face with the attention of someone who was accustomed to reading audience reaction and was now reading a person who was not an audience.

"You mapped this for me."

"I mapped this for whoever was going to stand at that junction. You're the one who decided it would be you."

They reached the masonry junction. The corner where two load-bearing walls met — the dense aggregate of pre-Breach construction, the stone that had survived the conduit installation and the BLACKWEIR event and the district's conversion from residential to whatever it was now and would survive whatever came next because survival was what load-bearing structures did. The amber light from the Anchor Zone fell across the junction's eastern face. The perimeter fencing was visible — fourteen meters, the institutional barrier with its biometric checkpoints and its warning signage in three languages. Beyond the fencing, the exposed conduit infrastructure. The orange glow. The hum.

"Here," Nuwa said.

She showed Kira the position. Back against the eastern wall. Sight-line to the perimeter through the gap between the masonry and the fencing. The RCI at the junction read 12.8 on the industrial monitor — she checked it, confirmed it, the number sitting in the amber display the way numbers sat in her inventory. Stable.

"Bring water," Nuwa said. "Don't stay longer than forty minutes."

"You sound like my mother."

"I sound like someone who survived the last time someone didn't listen."

Kira's expression shifted. The stream-voice energy dimmed for a half-second — the recognition of something landing, the weight of a sentence that arrived through the body rather than through the processing speed. Then the energy returned. The number, whatever the number was — Nuwa did not know the compound, did not know the dose, knew only the pattern — restoring the speed that had absorbed the weight before the weight could settle.

"Forty minutes," Nuwa said.

She placed the water bottle against the masonry wall beside Kira's position. She checked the dampening field on Kira's chest — the portable unit, military surplus, generating the counter-frequency whine that Nuwa could hear through the cracked housing. Active.

"Keep the dampening on."

"It reduces signal resolution. The stream needs —"

"Keep the dampening on."

Kira looked at her. The eyes moving — the scan, the assessment, the processing. Then a nod. Small. The nod of a person who had spent months receiving topology data from a woman she'd never met and who was now standing in the topology the data described and the data was correct and the woman was correct and the correctness was the thing that the filing system could categorize as *trust*.

"Forty minutes," Kira said.

---

Nuwa positioned herself thirty meters west. Line of sight.

Not because Kira asked. Kira had not asked. Kira had asked for terrain knowledge and positioning data and the corridor-level logistics that the institutional models did not have and the mage network could not provide because the mage network was signal and the signal was not topology. Nuwa had provided what was asked. The thirty meters was what was not asked.

She stood against the masonry wall. The stone was cool through her jacket. The hum was present — the residential frequency augmented by the Anchor Zone's proximity, the cracked housing registering the gradient as the familiar itch behind her left ear. She tilted her head to compensate for the dead zone. The overlay rendered: the frozen notification, grey, permanent. Two green notifications from this morning's network exchange. The time: 14:12.

Kira began the stream.

Nuwa could not see the overlay activation — the software ring in Kira's ocular implant, the HUD indicator that told the audience the performer was live. She was thirty meters away. The cracked implant did not receive at this resolution. But she could see the posture change. The shift she had read about in the network's reports — secondhand, mediated, the description of something she was now watching with the body's attention rather than the text's.

The spine straightened. The shoulders opened. The chin leveled. The five-foot-four frame at the masonry junction became larger — not physically, not measurably, but in the way a person occupied space when the person became the performer and the performer was the thing that four hundred thousand people oriented toward through their overlays from fourteen time zones.

Kira was talking. Nuwa could hear the rhythm — not the words, at thirty meters the stream-voice was ambient, a cadence without content. But the cadence was fast. The associations were pre-assembled. The pauses were structural, not cognitive — the beats that created the shape of revelation for an audience that was listening through screens while Nuwa listened through thirty meters of air and the cracked housing of a civilian implant that received the world through damage.

She counted.

The time: twenty-three minutes remaining. Kira's position: unchanged, back against the eastern wall, sight-line to the perimeter, the amber light across her face. The RCI: she checked the industrial monitor. 13.1. Up from 12.8. The fluctuation was within the advisory range — the Anchor Zone's output varied with the throughput cycles, the conduit infrastructure's load shifting as the system processed whatever the system processed and the processing generated the signal and the signal generated the hum and the hum was in the walls and the walls were the thing between the hum and the body and the body was the thing she was watching.

Kira's body. The posture had not changed — still the performer's spine, still the shoulders open, still the electric presence that commanded the junction's geometry the way it commanded a streaming frame. But Nuwa was watching with the Sump's attention, the attention that did not operate in metrics but in the accumulated pattern of watching people whose work improved as they deteriorated, and the attention saw what the metrics would not have measured.

The right hand. Kira's right hand, at her side, was still. Not resting — still. The fingers not moving. The hand not fidgeting, not doing the thing that hands did when the motor cortex was running at the speed the eyes were running. The stillness of the hand was the datum. The stillness said the compound had taken the thing the hands used to do and the taking was deep enough that the hands did not reach for it even at the Anchor Zone's perimeter where the signal was loud and the proximity was the thing the antenna had come for.

Nuwa did not know what the hands used to do. Nuwa knew that hands should not be that still when the eyes were that fast.

She counted. Seventeen minutes remaining. RCI: 13.4. Rising. Still advisory. The step function was at forty minutes. She had calculated it. She trusted the calculation. She did not trust the person at the junction to stop when the calculation said stop because the person at the junction was running at a speed that did not include stopping.

At fourteen minutes remaining, she began walking toward the junction.

Not quickly. The pace of a woman crossing thirty meters of masonry corridor because the crossing was the next item in the sequence. She carried the water bottle she had packed for herself — the second one, the one she had not given to Kira, the one she carried the way she carried everything, as inventory, as preparation for the thing that might be needed.

She reached the junction at eight minutes remaining.

Kira's right eye was wet. Not crying — the signal. The R3 parsing the populated chord at a range where the masonry attenuation was twelve percent and the remaining eighty-eight percent was 8.2 million absorbed cycling through a twenty-year-old's spine. The left eye scrolled pink text. The HUD processing the audience that was processing the performer that was processing the signal.

"Time," Nuwa said.

Kira did not respond. The stream-voice continued — the cadence, the rhythm, the words that Nuwa could now hear at two meters. "— the density at this range is not what the models predict. The models predict uniform attenuation from the perimeter outward but the signal is *structured*, the chord has directionality here, the R3 is parsing individual threads that at distance blend into the aggregate and at proximity separate into —"

"Time," Nuwa said again.

Kira blinked. The performer left. The spine curved. The shoulders came forward. The five-foot-four frame that had been electric at the junction was a woman in the amber light — the neon hair muted in the conduit glow, the blue and pink warmer than the screens would show, the right eye wet, the left eye still scrolling.

"Did you hear it?" Kira asked.

Nuwa did not have an R3. She had a cracked civilian implant with a data chip lodged in the port and a dead zone in the upper-right quadrant and nineteen months of the hum through masonry walls.

"I hear enough."

She handed Kira the water. Kira took it. Drank. The hands — both of them now, wrapped around the bottle — were shaking. Fine tremor. Not cold. The pharmacological edge meeting the signal edge meeting the body's correct response to standing at the boundary of the thing that contained everyone.

"The data was extraordinary," Kira said. The stream-voice was gone. What remained was quieter. Slower. The sentences unfinished, the energy dropped. "The signal structure at proximity — the chord separates into individual threads at this range. I could almost —"

"We should walk."

They walked. Nuwa led. The masonry corridor, the path she had mapped, the route of least conductivity reversed — west, north, into the residential grid where the hum dropped to baseline and the light was institutional white and the amber was behind them. Kira was quiet. The silence was not the performer's silence — the strategic pause, the beat that created space for the audience. The silence was the silence of a person who had stopped performing and did not know what to do with the quiet.

At the Level 3 transit junction, the tourist noise resumed. The foot traffic. The observation platforms. The food stalls. Kira stopped. She was looking at the observation platforms — the tourists with their RCI monitors, the lanyards, the dampening fields, the guided tours. Looking at them with the right eye that had been wet and was now dry and that was processing the tourism the way Nuwa had processed it three months ago with Fen.

"Three days," Kira said. "I want to come back. Closer."

Nuwa did not say the thing she knew. She had sent the message. The message had been filed. What Nuwa could see — the pattern, the speed, the curve — Kira could not see. The not-seeing was the compound's design.

"If you come back," Nuwa said, "you come to me first."

"I will."

"Bring water."

Kira almost smiled. The almost was the first thing Nuwa had seen that matched the person she had constructed from seven months of text — the tiredness under the speed, the person under the performer, the woman who had built a network of four hundred mages from a streaming chair and who could not hear the one thing the network's filing system did not file.

"Thank you," Kira said. "For the positioning. The maps. The forty minutes. All of it."

"The maps are data," Nuwa said. "The forty minutes is arithmetic. You don't need to thank someone for arithmetic."

Kira looked at her. The eyes — still too fast, still scanning, but for a moment the scanning paused and what was underneath the scanning was young. Twenty years old. The age of a person who had been modified since ten and medicated since sixteen and performing since thirteen and who was now standing in a transit junction in a city she had visited three times and understood from orbit.

"I'll message you before I come back," Kira said.

"Good."

The rail was arriving. The three ascending notes. Kira turned. Walked toward the platform. The neon hair caught the mixed light — the amber and the white producing the color that belonged to neither system. She was small in the transit junction. She was small everywhere except on stream.

Nuwa watched until the rail doors closed.

---

She cooked in the evening. Rice. Cabbage from the Level 3 market — Fen had not brought greens tonight. She ate at the table. The folding surface with the cardboard shim.

The inventory. Creds: fifty-nine. The water bottles: both empty, refill tomorrow. Rice: one day remaining. Adhesive: day nine of twelve. The industrial RCI monitor: returned to Yara's documentation group via the network drop on Level 2.

The route she had mapped: confirmed at operational parameters. The masonry junction's attenuation: verified. The forty-minute window: held. RCI during the stream: 12.8 to 13.4, advisory range, no spikes, step function not reached.

The things she did not put in the inventory: the speed of Kira's eyes. The stillness of her hands. The wet right eye at the forty-minute mark. The fine tremor on the water bottle. The almost-smile that had been the first real thing in thirty-eight minutes of performance.

The stream-voice's speed. The speed that the audience would call growth and that Nuwa called a curve and the curve had a shape she had described in a message and the message had been filed and the filing was the answer and the answer was that Kira was coming back in three days and the three days would require the same route and the same junction and the same forty minutes and the same thirty meters of line-of-sight and the same watching.

The frozen notification.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

The green notifications — five today. Network responses. Topology data requests. And one from Kira, sent forty minutes ago: *The signal structure at proximity is extraordinary. Individual threads separating from the aggregate chord. This changes the model. I'll be back in three days. Thank you.*

Extraordinary. The word the stack produced. The word the audience used. The word the maintenance crews' supervisors had used when the shift reports improved and the improvement was the compound and the compound was the curve and the curve's endpoint was a person who filed extraordinary reports and could not remember filing them.

Nuwa closed the overlay. Washed the bowl. One bowl. One pair of chopsticks. One mug. The count had not changed.

She sat on the mattress. The hum through the masonry walls. Steady. The frequency that meant the system continued.

She would be here in three days. She would map the route again. She would check the RCI readings and confirm the attenuation and calculate whether the forty minutes was still forty minutes or whether the Anchor Zone's output had shifted and the calculation required updating. She would stand at the junction and say *this way* and *bring water* and *forty minutes* and the saying would be the logistics and the logistics would be the care and the care was what she had.

The watching was the work. The work was not done.

The person she was watching was coming back. Faster. Closer. The curve continuing in the direction curves continued when the thing driving the curve was a compound the person could not see because the compound included the seeing. Nuwa could see it. Nuwa had always been able to see it — in the maintenance crews, in the corridors, in the body-knowledge that did not require vocabulary because the pattern was older than the words and the words were never the point. The point was the watching. The point was the thirty meters. The point was being close enough to cross the distance when the step function arrived and the forty minutes ended and the person at the junction needed someone who had mapped the route out.

The inventory was open. She was not done.
