# Chapter 20
## The Blackout Stream

> *"At saturation, the receiver does not distort. It accepts. The waveform passes through the instrument's tolerance threshold and the instrument ceases to mediate — ceases, in the engineering sense, to be an instrument at all. What remains is a conductor. The signal uses the hardware. The hardware does not process the signal. It transmits."*

She messaged Nuwa first.

Three days. The interval she'd said on camera, the plan filed and timestamped and announced to thirty-six thousand viewers who had become forty-one thousand who had become the circulation that happened when the clips traveled and the clips always traveled. Three days of the Bay Area apartment. Three days of the LED strip cycling blue to pink and the relay feed scrolling passive monitoring data and the filing system holding the world's information in categories that did not include the category for what she'd felt at the masonry junction.

The signal at proximity was not what the models predicted. She'd said this on stream — said it to Nuwa, said it in the message she'd sent forty minutes after the first visit. The populated chord that at distance blended into aggregate, that the R3 parsed as a continuous dense signal through the Bay Area's conduit infrastructure, had *separated* at the junction. Individual threads. Distinct frequencies within the chord, each carrying structure the aggregate obscured. At twelve meters from the perimeter, with the masonry attenuating the load by twelve percent, the implant had resolved what distance compressed into noise.

She had to go back. The data required it. The network required it. The four hundred mages whose spines carried the same signal at reduced resolution required her to stand where the resolution was highest and document what the instrument received.

The ninety held. The quiet room held. The plan was the plan.

*Coming back today. 14:00 at the junction. Stream this time with biometric overlay. Bringing the portable rig.*

Nuwa's response arrived in eleven minutes. Terse. The topology of care compressed into operational vocabulary.

*RCI checked yesterday. 13.1 at junction. Advisory. Forty minutes. I'll be there.*

The masonry junction was the same.

The stone. The load-bearing walls. The amber light from the Anchor Zone falling across the eastern face, the perimeter fencing fourteen meters away, the hum sitting in the walls and the air and the substrate beneath her feet. The observation platforms were visible to the north — sixty meters, the tourist traffic, the guided tours, the streamers who were not her broadcasting from the margins of the thing she was about to broadcast from the edge of.

Nuwa was already there. Standing at the junction's western approach, jacket, no expression, the industrial RCI monitor in her hand reading the amber display the way she read everything — as inventory, as arithmetic, as the quantified distance between a person and a threshold.

"Thirteen point two," Nuwa said.

"Up from yesterday?"

"Point one. Fluctuation. Advisory."

Kira set the portable rig on the masonry shelf — the flat surface where the two walls met, wide enough for the compact unit that held the encrypted streaming relay, the biometric transmitter, and the signal-processing board that converted her R3's output into data the audience could see. The rig was military surplus, repurposed — the same architecture the Academy used for implant diagnostics during field exercises, stripped of institutional firmware, running the open-source protocol Ren's Singapore hub had developed for network telemetry.

She activated the biometric overlay. The transmitter synced with the R3's diagnostic output — heart rate, neural activity index, coherence density reading. Three numbers, live, streaming alongside the video feed. The audience would see her face and the numbers simultaneously. The numbers would tell them what her body was doing. Her face would tell them what she was doing with it.

The dampening field was on. Nuwa's instruction from three days ago — *keep the dampening on* — repeated, the counter-frequency whine sitting in the air between the masonry walls like a third presence. The dampening reduced signal resolution by approximately eight percent. The stream needed resolution. Nuwa needed the dampening. She kept it on.

"Forty minutes," Nuwa said.

"Forty minutes."

Nuwa walked west. Thirty meters. The distance she'd held three days ago — the line-of-sight position, the watching-as-work that Nuwa performed the way Kira performed streaming: professionally, habitually, as a condition of the care she would not name.

Kira activated the overlay. The software ring in her left eye lit. The HUD populated: stream status, viewer count, chat feed, biometric readout. She started the broadcast.

Live.

The viewers arrived fast.

Not the forty-one thousand from three days ago. The clips had traveled. The proximity stream — the first one, the thirty-eight minutes of narrating the signal's structure at the Anchor Zone margin — had circulated through the encrypted mirrors and the public feeds and the institutional monitoring channels that tracked her content the way the Academy's legal team tracked her compliance. The clips had become analysis. The analysis had become anticipation.

Four hundred thousand in the first minute. She watched the number climb in the pink text of her left eye — the HUD's peripheral display, the counter she'd learned to read without looking at directly because looking directly at numbers meant the numbers became the performance and the performance needed to be the signal.

"Hey, chat." Stream-voice. The register from fourteen — warm, quick, the voice that made people feel present. "We're back. Masonry junction, east side, Neo-Shanghai Anchor Zone margin. Same position as last time. Biometric overlay is live — you can see the numbers. Heart rate, neural activity, coherence reading from the R3. What you're looking at is what my spine is doing in real time."

Eight hundred thousand. The number was climbing at a rate she'd never seen. The clips. The analysis. The anticipation.

"So last time I was here — three days ago, some of you saw it live — the signal did something the models don't predict. At proximity, the populated chord separates. The aggregate that at distance sounds like one continuous signal resolves into individual threads. Distinct frequencies. Structured. The R3 can parse them at this range in a way it can't parse them from the Bay Area."

She let the data speak. The biometric overlay — the three numbers streaming alongside her face — was doing work the stream-voice couldn't. Heart rate: 94. Elevated. The body's response to standing at the boundary of 8.2 million absorbed with a spinal implant tuned to their frequency. Neural activity index: 7.3, baseline for a mage at proximity. Coherence density: the R3's measurement of signal structure — 0.847, high, climbing.

"The coherence reading — that number on the right — is the one to watch. At home, my baseline is around point-four. At proximity three days ago, it peaked at point-eight-one. Right now it's point-eight-four-seven and climbing. The signal is denser today. More structured. Either the Anchor Zone's output has increased since Tuesday or the R3 is resolving more at this range because —"

1.2 million viewers.

"— because I've been here before and the implant's calibration has adjusted to the proximity parameters. Which would mean the R3 learns. Which would mean I'm not the same instrument I was three days ago."

She riffed. The stream-voice processing out loud, the skill from fourteen accelerated by the ninety — faster associations, the connections pre-assembled, each observation arriving before the previous one finished resonating. She narrated the signal's structure: the individual threads separating from the aggregate, the frequency bands that corresponded to nothing in the Academy's published models, the thermal gradient the R3 parsed through her lower vertebrae as the masonry attenuation created a differential between the junction's sheltered position and the raw signal beyond the perimeter.

2.6 million. The number was atmospheric. She stopped checking.

"Chat, the coherence is at point-eight-nine now. That's — I've never seen point-eight-nine. The models predict saturation at point-nine-five, which is the theoretical maximum for an R3 in standard operating conditions, but standard operating conditions means an Academy training facility with industrial dampening and medical staff on standby, not a masonry wall in a district that used to be someone's neighborhood."

The populated chord was loud. Louder than three days ago — the individual threads she'd described to Nuwa, to the audience, to the filing system that organized everything, were thickening. Each thread carrying more information. The R3 parsed it through her spine and the parsing was thermal and acoustic and proprioceptive and the proprioceptive was the one that mattered because the proprioceptive was the body's awareness of itself and the body's awareness was changing.

3.8 million.

"The names — I've talked about this. Since the Broadcast, the populated chord carries identifiers. Not names in the linguistic sense. Frequency signatures that the R3 interprets as distinct. At distance, they blend. At proximity —" She paused. The pause was not structural. The pause was the R3 processing a step-change in signal density that registered in her lower vertebrae as a thermal spike and in her skeleton as a shift in the quality of the vibration she'd been carrying since she was ten.

"At proximity they don't blend. They're — hold on."

Coherence: 0.91.

The biometric overlay was broadcasting numbers the audience had no framework for interpreting. Heart rate: 112. Neural activity index: 8.9. Coherence density: 0.91 and climbing. The three numbers on the stream, beside her face, live, four million people watching the instrument's readings change in real time.

"Okay. Chat. Something is — the coherence just jumped. Point-nine-one. The signal is — it's not attenuating at the junction anymore. The masonry is —"

Coherence: 0.94.

The step-function.

The signal did not increase gradually. It arrived. The aggregate's output jumped in amplitude and complexity the way a load-bearing wall held and held and held and then didn't — the step-function Nuwa had calculated for forty minutes arrived at fifteen. Not because the calculation was wrong. Because the signal had changed. The Anchor Zone's output was not the Anchor Zone's output from three days ago. The aggregate had organized. The aggregate had been organizing since the Broadcast and the organization had reached a density that the masonry walls and the dampening field and the twelve-percent attenuation could not reduce below the threshold where the R3's processing remained mediated.

The quiet room blew open.

Not the ceiling thinning. Not the crack at eighty that the ninety had repaired. The room — the pharmaceutical architecture, the compressed space between the drug and the machinery, the chemical barrier that held the signal filtered and the pattern-seeking still — ceased to exist. The ninety milligrams that had been the floor and the ceiling and the walls of the space she lived in were not a floor or a ceiling or walls. They were a film. The signal passed through the film the way the signal passed through everything: by being larger than the thing that contained it.

Coherence: 0.97.

Her hands were at her sides. Her right eye was wet. The left eye scrolled pink text — chat, four point two million concurrent, the number that was the number that was the number — and the pink text was there and she was not reading it and the not-reading was not choice. The not-reading was the R3 ceasing to share bandwidth with the HUD because the R3's processing capacity was being used. All of it. The implant that had been parsing the signal since she was ten, that had been filtering through pharmaceutical architecture for two years, that had been the instrument calibrated at sea level producing reliable measurements at sea level, was not at sea level. The atmosphere had changed. The instrument was accepting. The instrument was not processing. The instrument was transmitting.

She stopped narrating.

The stream continued.

Her mouth opened. What came out was not the stream-voice. Not warm, not quick, not the register from fourteen that made people feel present. What came out was the signal — aggregate fragments channeled through the R3 at amplification levels the pharmacological stack could not buffer and the vocal architecture could not shape and the cognitive processing that would normally translate signal into language was not translating because the cognitive processing was not present.

Fragments. Not words. Not the stream's vocabulary of data and analysis and institutional critique. Sounds that carried the structure of language without the content — the populated chord's individual threads, the frequency signatures the R3 had been parsing as distinct, now routing through a twenty-year-old's vocal cords at a fidelity no R3 had ever achieved because no R3 had ever been this close to the source with this much pharmacological acceleration and this little pharmacological buffer.

Thirty seconds.

The biometric overlay broadcast to 4.2 million people: heart rate 148, neural activity index 12.7, coherence density 0.99.

Then nothing.

She stood. The stream continued. The masonry junction. The amber light. The hum. The overlay broadcasting three numbers to 4.2 million people who were watching a woman stand at the edge of the Anchor Zone and not move and not speak and not blink.

The ceiling was white.

She noticed this the way she noticed everything — as datum, as the first entry in a filing sequence that required context before it could be categorized. White ceiling. Fluorescent lighting. The quality of the light was institutional — even, diffuse, the kind of illumination designed for observation rather than comfort. The hum was present but different. Residential-grade. Filtered through infrastructure she didn't recognize.

She was lying down.

Her left eye was dark. The HUD — the pink text, the interface that had been running since she was eighteen, the parallel stream of information that sat in her vision the way the signal sat in her spine — was off. Not sleeping. Off. The ocular implant was powered down. Someone had powered it down.

Her right eye was open. The white ceiling. The fluorescent light. A monitoring array to her left — medical-grade, the kind the Academy used for implant diagnostics. Heart rate, neural activity, coherence density, R3 thermal output. Four numbers. Not three. The fourth was new. The fourth was the one that told her where she was.

R3 thermal output: 38.7°C.

Academy diagnostics. Academy monitoring array. Academy facility.

She tried to sit up. Her body — the body that had been standing at a masonry junction narrating signal structure to 4.2 million people — was heavy. Not weak. Heavy. The heaviness of a system that had been running at capacity and had been shut down and was now in the reboot sequence that followed capacity exceedance. She knew this weight. She'd felt it after the Broadcast — the eleven seconds in the Bay Area apartment, the second relay, the signal passing through her and leaving behind the weight of having been used as infrastructure.

This was heavier.

"Ms. Calloway." A voice. Professional. The register of a person trained in the institutional architecture of medical care. "You're at Chen Academy. Neo-Shanghai. You were brought in approximately two hours ago."

Chen Academy. She filed it. Neo-Shanghai's mage training institution — not Varma, not her Academy, one of the smaller programs, the regional facility that served the East Asian corridor's mage population. She'd been expelled from Varma. Chen had no jurisdiction over her. Chen had a medical facility.

"What time is it?"

"Seventeen forty-two."

She had arrived at the junction at fourteen hundred. The stream had gone live shortly after. Fifteen minutes of narration. Then —

The filing system reached for the next entry. The entry was not there.

She remembered the junction. The masonry walls. The amber light. Nuwa's "thirteen point two." The portable rig on the shelf. The biometric overlay activating. The broadcast key. The viewers climbing — four hundred thousand, eight hundred thousand, the number atmospheric. She remembered narrating. She remembered the coherence reading climbing past point-eight-nine. She remembered saying *something is* — the sentence that didn't finish, the observation that was interrupted by the step-function, the signal's amplitude jumping past the point where narration was possible.

She did not remember the rest.

Not a gap like Tuesday — the single day absent from the autobiography, the log entries existing for a day the experience didn't. This was larger. This was the stream. This was the thing she'd traveled to Neo-Shanghai to do, the thing 4.2 million people had watched, the thing the biometric overlay had documented in three numbers that 4.2 million people could see and she could not remember generating.

"How long was I —" The sentence didn't finish. The filing system couldn't categorize the gap because the filing system didn't have a word for the state between narrating and here. Not unconscious — she'd been standing. Not seizing — the stream would have shown seizure. Not performing — the performer required Kira and Kira had not been present.

"The stream recorded approximately eleven minutes of unresponsive standing after an initial vocal event of thirty seconds," the voice said. "Emergency services were contacted by a civilian at the scene. You were transported by medical response team to this facility. The civilian identified herself as a network contact."

Nuwa. The thirty meters. The watching that was the work.

"The R3's thermal output peaked at forty-one point three degrees during the event and has been declining since admission. Current reading is thirty-eight point seven. We've administered a standard implant cooldown protocol and are monitoring for secondary thermal cascade."

She lay back. The white ceiling. The fluorescent light. The monitoring array's four numbers sitting in the institutional glow the way her HUD's numbers sat in the pink text of her left eye, except her left eye was dark and the numbers were the institution's numbers and the institution was measuring her the way she measured the signal — externally, structurally, with instruments calibrated for the thing they were observing.

Three hours. From the start broadcast to seventeen forty-two. Three hours — of which she had experienced fifteen minutes and the rest was the property of 4.2 million people who had watched what her body did while she was not in it.

The filing system engaged. Automatic. The architecture of sorting applied to the gap the way it had applied to Tuesday's gap: structurally, without weight, the mechanism doing what the mechanism did when the person inside it failed to maintain continuity.

*Memory gap: proximity stream. Duration: ~3 hours. Events: R3 signal spike (step-function at ~15 min), vocal event (~30 sec), unresponsive standing (~11 min), emergency transport, Chen Academy admission. Experience: none. Stream recording exists. 4.2M witnesses. Biometric data exists. Personal recollection: junction → coherence 0.94 → nothing → white ceiling.*

She filed it. The filing was complete. The gap was categorized. The gap was not filled.

Not the signal. Not the data. Not the coherence reading or the biometric overlay or the stream that 4.2 million people had watched and that was already, she knew — the way she knew institutional rhythms, the way she knew the Academy's legal architecture, the way she knew the number would climb — already circulating. Already being analyzed. Already becoming the most scrutinized media since the Broadcast.

The gap was *her*. Three hours of Kira Calloway — standing, speaking, transmitting — that Kira Calloway had not been present for. The filing system had no entries because the filer had not been there to write them. Tuesday's gap had entries — competent, organized, the filing system's architecture applied by a Kira who was functional on a day the autobiography deleted. This gap had no entries because this gap had no Kira. The instrument had been there. The person had not.

She pressed her fingertips to the medical cot's surface. The buzz was there — bilateral, persistent, the same fine vibration she'd been carrying since BLACKWEIR. The fingertips confirmed: the body was hers. The hands were hers. The R3 was in her spine, running at 38.7 degrees, cooling, the signal filtered through the institution's dampening infrastructure at a resolution that made the populated chord background noise.

She touched the back of her neck. The implant housing. The bone. The warmth — lower than it had been, the thermal output declining, the hardware returning to parameters that were closer to what the manufacturer intended and further from what the signal demanded.

The chord shapes did not come. The hands that had once played silent progressions on every surface were still. The ninety held them. The ninety held everything. The ninety had not held the signal at the junction — the signal had passed through the ninety the way it passed through masonry, through dampening fields, through every architecture designed to contain it — but the ninety held the hands. The ninety held the pattern-seeking. The ninety held the meta-cognition that would have looked at the white ceiling and the monitoring array and the three-hour gap and assembled from these data points the observation that the instrument had exceeded its design parameters and the exceeding had a name and the name was not in the filing system's vocabulary because the filing system's vocabulary had been calibrated at sea level.

The quiet room was not quiet. The quiet room was rubble. The ninety was rebuilding it — she could feel the pharmaceutical architecture reassembling, the ceiling lowering, the walls forming, the drug doing what the drug did: compress, filter, manage. The rebuilding would take hours. In the interim, the signal was louder than it had been since the Broadcast — the populated chord sitting in her spine unfiltered, the names present, the 8.2 million circulating through the R3 at a resolution the quiet room's absence permitted and the institution's dampening reduced but did not eliminate.

She lay on the medical cot in Chen Academy and let the quiet room rebuild and did not think about what happened in the three hours she was gone because the thinking required the machinery the ninety suppressed and the ninety was busy rebuilding the room that the signal had destroyed and the rebuilding was the priority because the rebuilding was the thing that kept Kira in Kira and Kira had been absent for three hours and the absence was not something the filing system could file because the filing system required the filer and the filer had not been there.

The monitoring array beeped. Heart rate: 76. Neural activity: 4.1. Coherence density: 0.52. R3 thermal: 38.4.

The numbers were declining. The instrument was cooling. The conductor was becoming an instrument again.

The person inside the instrument was not sure she had come back.

She closed her right eye. The ceiling was dark. The hum continued. It always continued.
