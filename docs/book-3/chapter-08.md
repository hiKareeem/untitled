# Chapter 8
## Both Systems

> *"The intertidal zone develops structural complexity precisely because nothing in it is permanent. Organisms that colonize the boundary between stable states do not resemble the organisms of either state. They are their own system — adapted to destruction, dependent on the cycle that destroys them."*
>
> — *Littoral Ecology and Substrate Colonization*, pre-Breach marine curriculum (deaccessioned)

---

She counted before she opened her eyes.

Creds: eighty-seven. Room: paid through the week — nine per day, sixty-three committed, leaving twenty-four for food and the adhesive tube on day six of twelve. Rice: three days in the cabinet. Oil: a quarter bottle. Greens: Fen had left a bundle of morning glory outside her door last night, wrapped in the damp cloth, the cloth she used for greens and nothing else.

The frozen notification. Still there. Still the first thing the overlay rendered, before the transit map, before the Feed, before the permanent grey of the dead zone in the upper-right quadrant.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

Beside it, four green notifications. Network responses. She did not read them yet — she would read them the way she read everything, after the inventory, after the rice, after the body confirmed what the counting had mapped. The green notifications had become part of the morning display the way the frozen credential notice had become part of the morning display. One was the system's last word on her institutional existence. The others were the system she was building beside it.

She opened her eyes. The ceiling. The concrete. The hum through the walls — the Mid-Level frequency, steady, residential-grade. Eighteen months of this hum. Not the E-flat. The E-flat was gone the way Block 14 was gone — not destroyed, not silent, but absorbed into a composite that the document's vocabulary called a *conscious collective field* and that she called the sound of everyone she had known continuing inside the infrastructure that had consumed them.

She stood. Brushed her teeth. Boiled water. Made rice.

She ate standing at the sink.

---

The adhesive tube was on the shelf beside the mattress. Day six of twelve. She unsealed the cap and applied a thin line along the hairline crack in the implant housing behind her left ear — the split from BLACKWEIR, eighteen months old, the fracture that let in moisture and micro-fluctuations and the signal's edge frequencies that the sealed housing would have filtered. The adhesive was warm from the shelf's proximity to the water heater pipe. She pressed it into the crack with her thumbnail. Held it. Counted to thirty.

The chip was in the port. Fuxi's thirteen years. She had not removed it since the first month — the corroded contact pins, the risk of widening the crack, the body's accommodation of the data as a permanent extension of the hardware. The chip lived in her. Junction coordinates and conduit densities and the handwriting in the metadata field and the topology of a district that existed now only as data behind her left ear and as a field inside the infrastructure the data described.

She pulled on her jacket. The mechanical latch clicked behind her.

---

The corridor was dim. Second-floor lighting only — the building's standard, the code-minimum. She touched the masonry wall as she passed. The stone was cool. Dense. Eighteen months and the hand still confirmed what the body already knew.

She stopped at Fen's door. Knocked once.

"Market day," Fen said through the door. "I know. Give me two minutes."

Nuwa waited. The corridor smelled of someone's breakfast — garlic, fermented bean paste, the cooking from the floor above — two families who had moved closer after Shuang's topology sessions outgrew her room in Unit 2. The building had reorganized itself around the work. The families who had come for the maps had stayed for the proximity to someone who knew which walls mattered, and the staying had produced a density that was not institutional and not random but something in between — a settlement pattern organized around survival knowledge the way the Sump's settlement pattern had been organized around conduit infrastructure.

Fen emerged. Jacket on. The sorting hands already moving — adjusting a sleeve, pocketing keys, the efficient choreography of a woman whose room was organized for the speed of leaving.

"The observation platforms are up," Fen said. "Three of them. Since last week."

Nuwa had heard. The network had mentioned it — not the testimony network, the other one, the informal channel where margin residents exchanged information about the district's daily changes the way Sump residents had exchanged information about which corridors the shimmers favored.

"Let's go see," she said.

---

The Anchor margin was four blocks south.

She had walked this route for a year. The masonry corridors of Levels 4 and 5, the concrete stairwells, the transit junction at Level 3 where the public display still scrolled its headlines and where she still stood at the edge of the projection range and tilted her head to compensate for the dead zone. The route had not changed. The destination had.

Six months ago, the Anchor margin had been a boundary — the line where the Mid-Levels' residential corridors ended and the Anchor Zone's administrative perimeter began. Municipal fencing. Biometric checkpoints that scanned credentials Nuwa did not carry. Warning signage in three languages: *Anchor Zone — Restricted Access. Elevated RCI. No Unauthorized Entry.* The boundary had been institutional. Clean. The system's architecture applied to the space between the living city and the place the city had consumed.

The boundary was still there. The fencing. The checkpoints. The signage. But the boundary had acquired a margin, and the margin had acquired a population, and the population had acquired an economy, and the economy had built a district that did not exist on any municipal map and that operated on the logic of every district that had ever formed at the edge of something the system could not fully contain.

She and Fen descended to Level 3 and turned south and the district opened in front of them.

The first thing was the light. Not the Mid-Levels' managed white — the institutional illumination that the residential corridors maintained at specification. This was mixed. Amber conduit glow bled upward from the Anchor Zone's exposed infrastructure, the orange light that had been the Sump's permanent sky now leaking through the perimeter fencing and the gaps in the municipal barrier and the cracks where the boundary's architecture met the Mid-Levels' older construction. The amber mixed with the white. The result was a color that belonged to neither system — a warm, uneven wash that turned the corridor walls copper and cast double shadows from every vertical surface.

The second thing was the sound. The hum was louder here. Not Sump-loud — not the pressure through floors and furniture and the bones of her hands. But louder than the Mid-Levels' residential baseline, the frequency climbing as the conduit infrastructure thickened toward the perimeter. She could feel it in the implant — the cracked housing registering the gradient as a rising warmth behind her ear, the itch intensifying as the RCI climbed from residential-grade toward the advisory threshold that the municipal signage warned about and that the district's population had learned to live inside.

The observation platforms were new.

Three of them, cantilevered from the corridor's structural beams at the point where the Level 3 transit way met the perimeter fencing. Improvised construction — reclaimed infrastructure materials, the grey composite panels that NitroCore's decommissioning crews stripped from condemned conduit housings and that appeared on the black market three days later as building material. The platforms jutted over the fencing, extending two meters past the municipal boundary into the airspace above the Anchor Zone's outer perimeter. From the platforms, you could see down.

Nuwa did not go to the platforms. She stood at the corridor's edge and watched.

The platforms were occupied. Fifteen, twenty people — standing at the railings, holding portable RCI monitors the size of a hand, pointing them at the Anchor Zone the way tourists once pointed cameras. The monitors were new too. Six months ago they had been industrial instruments — conduit maintenance equipment, calibrated for the technicians who serviced the Mid-Level grid. Now they were consumer items. Vendors sold them at the corridor's entrance — fifty creds for the basic model, which displayed a single RCI reading in amber numerals; a hundred and twenty for the enhanced model, which rendered a false-color gradient map on a personal overlay and pulsed when the reading crossed the advisory threshold. The enhanced model came in three colors. The vendor had a display rack.

The tourists wore the monitors on lanyards around their necks. Some held them up, screens facing outward, recording the readings the way people recorded views. The RCI at the platform's edge read 14.7. She saw the number on three screens simultaneously — amber numerals, the font that NitroCore's institutional architecture applied to everything, glowing against the mixed light.

14.7 was not dangerous. 14.7 was the upper range of Mid-Level normal — elevated, flagged, within the parameters that the system classified as *advisory*. In the Sump, 14.7 had been a quiet day. In the Sump, the baseline had been 15 to 25, and the spikes had been higher, and the spikes had meant something the monitoring protocols had no classification for, and the monitoring protocols had classified them anyway, and the classifications had been wrong, and the wrongness had been administrative, and the administration had continued.

14.7. The tourists recorded it. The tourists did not know what 14.7 meant.

Nuwa knew. 14.7 meant the hum was in the walls. 14.7 meant the implant itched. 14.7 meant the infrastructure beneath the platform was carrying a signal density high enough to warm the conduit housings and make the air taste faintly of ozone and produce the shimmers that appeared near junction nodes when the throughput spiked — the shimmers that the Sump's residents had learned not to name and that the tourists were paying to see.

"Shimmer tours," Fen said. She was reading a vendor's display — a flat-panel advertisement mounted on the corridor wall, cycling through promotional text in three languages. *Experience the Edge. Guided proximity excursions with certified RCI monitoring. Dampening field included. 90 minutes. 200 creds.*

Two hundred creds. The system's price for leaving the containment buffer zone — the relocation stipend from the notice that had been posted on their doors — was the same as the price of a guided tour to stand where the buffer zone met the thing the buffer zone was buffering.

"Dampening field included," Nuwa said.

The dampening fields were the third new thing. Portable units — chest-mounted, the size of a canteen, generating a localized counter-frequency that reduced the hum's amplitude within a one-meter radius. Military surplus, originally. Stalker equipment, designed for soldiers who operated at breach perimeters where the RCI exceeded the threshold the human nervous system could sustain without performance degradation. The surplus had reached the market through the same channels that moved everything from institutional inventory to civilian use: decommissioning, reclassification, a vendor with a supply chain and a corridor with foot traffic.

The tourists wore them. Chest-mounted, lanyarded, the dampening field's counter-frequency producing a faint electronic whine that Nuwa could hear beneath the hum — a thin, insistent note that said *I am not from here and the place I am standing is not designed for me and the technology I am wearing is the distance between my body and the frequency this body was not built to carry.*

She did not wear one. The cracked implant, the eighteen months, the body's accommodation of the hum through masonry-wall routing and the instinctive path selection that the document had quantified as correlation 0.89 — these were her dampening field. The body's knowledge. The routing that did not explain itself.

---

The food stalls ran along the corridor's east wall.

Six of them. More than last month — she had counted four on her last walk through, and the four had been more than the two from the month before that. The stalls occupied the structural alcoves that the corridor's original construction had designed as emergency equipment storage — fire suppression, atmospheric filters, the infrastructure that building codes required and that the building's conversion to commercial space had rendered administrative rather than functional. The alcoves were the right size. A vendor, a cooking surface, a counter. The stalls faced the observation platforms. The foot traffic flowed between them — tourists moving from platform to stall to vendor to platform, the circulation pattern of a district organizing itself around the movement of money through proximity.

The stalls sold food that referenced the Anchor Zone. Not explicitly — not *Sump cuisine*, not marketed as the food of the district the system had consumed. The reference was atmospheric. *Thermal gradient rice bowls* — cooked on surfaces heated by the ambient RCI, the conduit infrastructure beneath the corridor floor providing supplemental warmth that the vendors had learned to channel through metal grating into their cooking surfaces. The rice had a faint amber tint from the nitro-adjacent heating — not a flavor, not a contamination, but a visual marker that said *this was cooked at the edge* and that the tourists photographed before they ate.

She watched a woman hold a bowl up to the amber light bleeding through the perimeter fencing. The amber tint of the rice matched the amber of the conduit glow. The woman's overlay — visible as a faint projection on the surface of her left eye, the high-resolution rendering that Mid-Level implants produced — was recording. The woman was composing an image. The image was the bowl and the light and the proximity and the RCI monitor's amber numerals in the background and the composition said *I was here* and the *here* was the place where eight point two million people had been absorbed into the infrastructure and the bowl was cooked on the heat their absorption had generated and the woman did not know this because the woman did not count.

Nuwa counted.

Forty-seven. The names she had recognized during the Broadcast. Mrs. Huang. Yuto. Lin Wei. Her mother's neighbor. Forty-three others — from Block 14, from the eastern blocks, from the corridors she had walked and the market stalls she had visited and the textile reclamation plant where the chemical staining had entered her fingertips and not left. Forty-seven names out of 8.2 million absorbed. The number had not changed. The number would not change. The Broadcast had been a census and the census was complete and the forty-seven were the ones she had recognized and the 8.2 million were the ones she had not and the ratio between them was the measure of her life's radius — how far she had walked, how many faces she had filed, how much of the Sump she had carried in the inventory she maintained without numbers and without purpose.

The tourists counted nothing.

They counted creds. They counted the RCI readings on their monitors. They counted the minutes of their dampening field rental. They counted the steps to the observation platform and the seconds of the shimmer that appeared near the perimeter fencing and the followers who would see the images they composed. They counted everything that could be counted in the vocabulary of transaction and nothing that could be counted in the vocabulary of loss.

She stood in the corridor between the food stalls and the observation platforms and the amber light from the Anchor Zone fell across her face and the itch behind her ear was the itch of proximity to the infrastructure that had consumed everyone she had known and the infrastructure was still there and the consumed were still there and the tourists were recording the light their presence generated.

Fen touched her arm.

"We should get the greens before the stall crowd hits the market."

Nuwa nodded. Turned from the platforms. Walked.

---

The artifact vendors were on Level 2.

She had not intended to pass them. The route to the market from the Anchor margin ran through Level 3's transit corridor — east, away from the perimeter, into the residential grid where the hum dropped to baseline and the light was institutional white and the district's economy thinned to nothing. But the stairwell between 3 and 2 was blocked — construction, a new platform being installed, the district expanding downward the way the city had always expanded, filling available space with the infrastructure of whatever the space was being used for.

She rerouted. Down to Level 2. Through the maintenance corridor that ran beneath the transit way. And the vendors were there — arranged along the corridor wall in the same alcove-stall configuration as the food vendors above, selling objects that were not food and that cost more than food and that the tourists carried in bags the way the tourists carried everything, as evidence of proximity.

Polymer fragments. She recognized them immediately — the transparent panels that the Sump's residential units had used as windows, the frosted polymer that Nuwa and Fuxi's apartment had faced and that had looked at nothing and that had, during BLACKWEIR, become the surface through which she had watched a hand enter and not return. The fragments were cut to palm-size. Smoothed at the edges. Mounted on small stands — display stands, the kind that held decorative objects on shelves in apartments where shelves held decorative objects rather than adhesive tubes and rice bags and the functional minimum of a life organized for the speed of leaving.

Some of the fragments had marks. Discoloration in the polymer — handprints, pressure distortions, the residue of contact between human skin and a surface that had been, during the eleven seconds, a boundary between the living and the absorbed. The fragments with marks cost more. The vendor's display listed prices: unmarked, forty creds; marked, one-twenty; *verified marked* — authenticated by an Anchor Zone salvage crew as containing absorption-event residue — three hundred.

Three hundred creds for a piece of the window a hand had gone through.

She did not stop. She did not look at the marks. She knew what the marks were. She had seen the hand — the fingers spreading into the polymer the way fingers spread into water, except water releases and the polymer did not release. The hand was in the infrastructure. The hand was in the conscious collective field. The hand was a resolvable pattern in the aggregate signal, a name she might have recognized if the hand's owner had spoken during the Broadcast, a data point in someone else's inventory.

The hand was now a product.

She walked through the vendor corridor without stopping and Fen walked beside her and neither of them spoke and the not-speaking was the shared language of two women who had survived something that was being sold in a corridor they had not chosen to walk through.

---

The market on Level 3 was unchanged.

This was the thing she returned to — the market's ordinary commerce, the vegetable stalls and the dry goods vendors and the woman at the far end who accepted unregistered cred and who did not ask where Nuwa was from because the woman had been accepting unregistered cred from people whose *where* had become a *when* for longer than Nuwa had been in the margins. The market was the district's anchor — the economy that preceded the tourism and that would continue after the tourism because the market sold what people needed and what people needed did not require an observation platform or a dampening field or a polymer fragment on a display stand.

She bought greens. Rice. A tube of adhesive — eleven creds, the same price as six months ago, the one commodity in the margin whose price had not inflated with the district's growth. She paid in unregistered cred. The woman counted the payment. The counting was transactional, efficient, the arithmetic of a vendor whose inventory was produce and whose margins were the margins between wholesale and retail and nothing else.

They were walking back through the Level 3 transit corridor when it happened.

"Nuwa Lin?"

She stopped. The voice was behind her — a woman's voice, accented, the inflection rising on *Lin* the way a question rises when the questioner is not certain of the answer. Nuwa turned.

A woman. Mid-twenties. Clean clothing — Mid-Level standard, not margin wear. Her overlay was visible as a faint projection on the surface of her left eye, the high-resolution rendering that indicated a current-generation implant with full credential access. She was holding a portable display — smaller than the overlay projector Nuwa had rigged from a discarded terminal, sleeker, the consumer model that cost more than Nuwa's rent for a year.

"You're Nuwa Lin," the woman said. The question had become a statement. "From the testimony network. You sent the topology data — the junction maps. The forty-seven names."

Nuwa's hands were at her sides. The market bag in her left. The greens, the rice, the adhesive. Her fingers tightened on the handles. The reflex — the Sump reflex, the body's response to being identified, to being located in someone else's inventory, to the conversion of *invisible* to *known*.

"I'm with the documentation group," the woman said. "The one that contacted you — the absorption pattern database. We've been using your corridor-level data for six months. Your maps are in the Lagos integration now. I recognized your handle on the public channel and I —" She stopped. Read something in Nuwa's posture — the shoulders forward, the chin down, the compressed stance of a woman who had spent her life occupying as little space as possible. "I'm sorry. I should have messaged first."

"Yes," Nuwa said.

"I just — I wanted to say thank you. In person. Your data changed what we're able to map. The junction-level resolution, the path-poor classifications — nobody else has that. The institutional models are grid-level. Yours is corridor-level. Building-level. The difference is —"

"I know what the difference is."

The woman stopped. Fen was beside Nuwa, reading the exchange the way margin residents read everything — the brief assessment, the measurement of who this person was and what this person wanted and whether what this person wanted was something that could be given without cost.

"My name is Yara," the woman said. "I should have introduced myself first. I'm sorry."

She was sorry. Nuwa could see the sorry in the way the woman held her portable display — angled away now, the screen dimmed, the instinct of someone who had realized that the person she was thanking had not consented to being found. The sorry was genuine. The sorry did not change the fact that a stranger had said her name in a transit corridor and the name had traveled through the air the way the hum traveled through the walls — a signal, propagating, locating her in a space she had navigated for eighteen months on the assumption that the navigation was invisible.

"The forty-seven names," Nuwa said. "You have them?"

"In the database. Cross-referenced with the junction data you provided. Each name paired with its infrastructure coordinates. It's the most complete ground-truth record from the Neo-Shanghai zone."

Ground truth. The phrase the network used for data that came from the surface rather than from instruments. Data that a person had carried in their body before they carried it in a file. Data that smelled like garlic greens and sounded like a child's voice saying *I touched the light* and looked like chemical staining on fingertips that the solvents had marked and the marking had never left.

"Good," Nuwa said.

She did not say more. The word was sufficient. The word contained what she could carry into the transit corridor without the carrying becoming something other than the walk home — *good*, meaning the names were in a system that would hold them, meaning the junction data was being used, meaning the forty-seven were not only in her inventory but in an architecture that might outlast her adhesive budget and her unregistered cred and the cracked implant behind her ear that received the world through damage.

Yara nodded. Took a step back. The assessment complete — the reading of Nuwa's posture that told her the exchange was over, that the woman she had come to thank was a woman whose gratitude arrived as a single word and whose departure was already in the angle of her shoulders.

"If you need anything," Yara said. "The documentation group. We're on the network."

"I know."

She turned. Walked. Fen walked beside her. The transit corridor carried them north, away from the Anchor margin, away from the observation platforms and the food stalls and the artifact vendors and the woman who had said her name in a public space and the name had been heard by the corridor and the corridor had not changed and the name was in the air and the air was the air.

---

She cooked in the evening. Rice. The morning glory Fen had left — sliced thin, fried in the last of the oil until the edges darkened. The smell filled the room. Green. Sharp. The smell of a choice made in a kitchen four meters by three, in a building without credential verification, in a district that was forming around the boundary of the thing that had consumed the place where she had learned to cook.

She ate at the table. The folding surface with the cardboard shim. She used the table now — not every night, but most nights. The table had stopped being the surface she kept for company and become the surface she kept for herself. The change had been gradual. She did not know when it had happened. She did not know if it was a decision or a habit. The distinction had never been clear. The distinction did not need to be clear.

The overlay pulsed. She opened it. Tilted her head to compensate for the dead zone.

The four green notifications from this morning. Three network responses — routine, the daily exchange of data points and junction coordinates and the steady accumulation of information that the testimony network processed and filed and forwarded to the Lagos integration and the documentation group and the informal architecture of people in fourteen cities who counted what the system did not count. The fourth was from Yara — a message sent after their corridor meeting, two lines: *Thank you for speaking with me. The data matters.*

She did not reply. She would reply tomorrow. Or the day after. The reply would be data — junction coordinates, conduit density readings, the topology of the sector's latest infrastructure changes that she tracked through the public directory and through the body's knowledge of which walls carried what. The reply would not be personal. The reply would be the work.

The frozen notification sat at the edge of her vision. Grey. Permanent.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

The green notifications sat beside it. Accumulating.

She was in both systems now. The one that had archived her and the one that was asking for what she knew. The first had made her invisible — deleted her residential record, decommissioned her credential server, reclassified her district, and called the reclassification *remediation*. The second was making her visible — her name on a public network, her data in a database, her junction maps in an integration that spanned fourteen cities, her handle recognized by a stranger in a transit corridor who had said *Nuwa Lin* the way the system said *Lin, Nuwa* — the same name, different architecture.

The first system had taught her that invisibility was safety. Be small enough and the system doesn't notice you. She had carried that since she was eleven — since her father, since the first time she understood that being too small to count was the only architecture available. Be careful. Be small. Route along the masonry walls. Do not give the system a reason to look.

The second system was teaching her that visibility was the work. The topology data required a source. The forty-seven names required a witness. The corridor-level knowledge that the institutional models did not have required a person who had walked the corridors and survived the walking and carried the walking in the body's memory of which walls mattered. The work required her name. The work required her face in a transit corridor where a stranger could say *Nuwa Lin* and the saying was not a credential check and not a biometric scan and not the system's inventory of expendable bodies but a recognition — the specific recognition of a person who had data the architecture needed.

She had not decided if this was better. Visibility in the margins was not the same as visibility in the system. The system's visibility was inventory — tracked, classified, processed. The network's visibility was testimony — named, located, heard. The difference was the difference between being counted by the system that consumed you and being counted by the people who survived the consumption.

The difference was the difference between the frozen notification and the green ones.

She closed the overlay. Washed the bowl. One bowl. One pair of chopsticks. One mug. The count had not changed. Everything she owned could still be packed in four minutes.

The inventory was larger now. Creds and food and adhesive and rent — and junction coordinates and conduit densities and forty-seven names and the topology of a district that was forming at the boundary between the system that had consumed her neighborhood and the system she was building from the data the consumption had produced. The inventory could not be packed. The inventory was in the network and in the database and in a stranger's portable display and in the cracked overlay behind her left ear and in the body's knowledge of which walls carried death and which walls carried nothing.

She sat on the mattress. The hum through the masonry walls was faint. Steady. The frequency that meant the system continued.

She was inside it. In both of them. The one that did not see her and the one that had learned her name.

The inventory was open. She was not done.
