# Chapter 51
## Eighty Milligrams

> *"I asked my partner what I did on Wednesday. She told me. It sounded like something I would do. I cannot verify this from the inside."*
>
> — Anonymous, Mage Network Encrypted Forum, 2176

---

The number was atmospheric.

2.1 billion. She'd seen it on the feed three days after the event — the aggregate view count, authenticated by seven independent forensic labs, the eleven seconds of the Broadcast relayed through a human nervous system verified as unaltered, unmanipulated, the signal captured at R3 fidelity through a streaming rig in a Bay Area apartment by a woman who was conscious and narrating and bleeding from her right ear. She'd seen the number and filed it the way she filed everything: category, magnitude, timestamp, relevance. The filing system processed it. The filing system did not hold it. 2.1 billion was not a number the filing system had a container for.

She turned the HUD on for the first time in days. The pink text scrolled in her left eye. Messages. Hundreds of messages. The relay channel, the network feed, the encrypted mirrors she'd set up after the first broadcast went viral fourteen months ago. She scrolled. The messages were not from her network.

Government requests for "cooperative testimony." Three of them — PCC, Euro-Asian Energy Bloc, Pan-Asian Cooperation Council. The language was institutional, the vocabulary designed to sound like invitation and read like compulsion. *Your unique experiential data would be invaluable to the ongoing assessment of the event's implications for public safety and infrastructure management.* She'd read institutional language since she was fourteen. She knew what it sounded like when an institution wanted to study you and called it cooperation.

Academy legal correspondence. Two communications — the first a "formal advisory" regarding "unauthorized broadcast of proprietary R3 telemetry data," the second a cease-and-desist for "continued operation of an unregistered mage network utilizing Academy-classified implant protocols." She'd been expelled seven months ago. The Academy had revoked her credentials, her access, her institutional identity. They had not revoked the implant. They couldn't. It was in her spine. And now the implant had broadcast the most-viewed media event in human history and the Academy wanted the data back the way institutions always wanted things back — through legal architecture, through the vocabulary of ownership applied to a signal that had moved through a woman's skeleton and out through a streaming rig into the world.

Corporate recruitment. Four offers. NitroCore's media division, two independent analytics firms, a defense contractor she'd never heard of. The offers were generous. The language was different — warmer, more personal, the institutional compulsion replaced by market vocabulary. *Your platform reach and unique signal processing capabilities represent an extraordinary opportunity.* They wanted the antenna. They wanted to hire the antenna and point it at whatever they needed the antenna pointed at.

Three military subpoenas. Separate jurisdictions. She read the first one — *pursuant to emergency authorization under the Global Security Coordination Framework* — and stopped reading.

She closed the HUD.

The ring light was off. The LED strip cycled blue to pink along the wall behind the desk. The apartment was the apartment — the same desk, the same chair, the same streaming rig she'd used for the second broadcast to however many people had watched and were still watching and would keep watching because the eleven seconds existed now in the world's architecture the way the absorbed existed in the void's architecture: permanently, replicating, beyond retrieval.

She ignored all of them.

Not with responses. Not with legal counsel. Not with the careful institutional navigation the Academy had trained her for before the Academy had expelled her. She ignored them the way she'd ignored the first clip's viral wave fourteen months ago — by not engaging with the architecture of institutional attention, by understanding that the attention was not about her but about the signal and the signal was not hers to give or withhold or negotiate with. The attention was atmospheric. The requests were weather. She sat in the weather and did not respond to it the way you did not respond to rain.

The signal continued.

---

Tuesday was gone.

She discovered it on Wednesday — or what her HUD said was Wednesday, the timestamp in the corner of her left eye that she'd learned to check because the filing system needed dates and dates needed days and the days had been reliable until they weren't. Wednesday morning. She was in the chair. The LED strip was cycling. The Lumina from last night was holding, the quiet room assembled, the ceiling compressed, the eighty doing its work. She checked the log.

Monday's entries were there. Filed, timestamped, formatted in the system she'd built fourteen months ago. Network status. Signal monitoring. Three messages from Ren about the passive monitoring protocol. A note about the viewer count — 1.8 billion at Monday's timestamp, still climbing. A reminder to contact Paz.

Wednesday's entries started at 06:14. She was writing them now.

Between Monday and Wednesday there was nothing.

Not a gap in the entries — a gap in the days. She opened the log's metadata. Tuesday existed. Tuesday had entries. Twelve of them. She opened the first:

*Network status: stable. 398 nodes active. Passive monitoring. Signal density: post-event baseline, holding. Right ear: intermittent tinnitus, manageable. Lumina: 80mg, 22:30.*

Her filing format. Her timestamps. Her abbreviations. She'd been there on Tuesday. She'd sat in this chair and opened this log and typed these words and filed them in the system and the system had them and she did not.

She read all twelve entries. They were competent. Organized. The filing system's architecture applied with the precision she'd maintained since the network passed fifty members. Status reports, signal monitoring, a relay channel update she'd apparently sent to Ren about adjusting the passive monitoring thresholds. A note about the military subpoenas — *three jurisdictions, not responding, filed under institutional pressure / ignore.* Tuesday's Kira had been functional. Tuesday's Kira had done the work. Wednesday's Kira had no access to Tuesday's Kira's experience of doing it.

The day was gone. Not the events — the events were in the log, timestamped, formatted, recoverable. The *day.* The lived experience of waking and sitting in the chair and checking the feed and filing the data and taking the Lumina and going to sleep. Whether the ring light had been on. Whether she'd eaten. The experiential texture of being alive on a Tuesday — erased, the neural record absent, the autobiography missing a page that the filing system had dutifully preserved because the filing system was not her memory. The filing system was an architecture she'd built to hold what her mind processed. Her mind had not processed Tuesday. Her mind had processed Tuesday and then deleted it.

She sat with it.

The pattern-seeking machinery — suppressed at eighty, the quiet room holding, the ceiling low — registered the gap as data. Filed it. *Memory gap: Tuesday. Duration: full day. Entries exist. Experience does not. Lumina-related? Fatigue? Event proximity?* The filing of the unfiled experience of a day she couldn't remember was the system doing what the system did when the person inside it failed to maintain continuity.

She opened the network's encrypted forum. The channel she didn't post in — the one the long-term users maintained, the mages who'd been on Lumina longer than her, the ones who'd been managing the signal with chemical architecture since before the network existed. She'd read it before. Casually. The way you read accounts of a road you hadn't driven yet.

She searched: *memory.*

The posts loaded. Dozens of them. Scattered across months of forum activity, threaded into conversations about dosage and duration and the pharmacology of a compound that had no official documentation because the compound was illicit and the people using it were managing a condition that the institutions that gave them the implants refused to acknowledge as medical.

*"Lost a Thursday. Not what happened — the day. My logs have entries. I wrote them. I don't remember writing them."*

*"At 70mg for six months. The gaps started at month four. Not every week. Maybe twice a month. A day just isn't there. My partner tells me what I did. I believe her. I don't remember."*

*"The gaps aren't blackouts. Blackouts are holes in a wall. This is the wall being a different length. You don't notice the missing section until you measure."*

*"80mg. Nine months. The gaps are weekly now. I've started logging everything. The logs are the memory. I am the logs."*

She closed the forum. Opened her own log. Checked the past two weeks. Monday through Monday — seven days, every entry hers, every experience accessible. Then the gap. Tuesday.

One day.

The posts said it started with one. The posts said it got worse. The posts said eighty was where it started happening regularly — not because eighty was pharmacologically special but because eighty pushed the suppression deep enough that the brain couldn't write the record anymore. The R3 had been in her spine since she was ten. The brain had woven the implant's output into its baseline architecture — the signal was part of how she encoded experience the way vision and hearing were part of how she encoded experience. Suppress the signal deep enough, the encoding lost a channel. Lose a channel long enough, lose the day.

The filing system had the entries. The filing system was the prosthetic memory she'd built without knowing she was building a prosthetic.

The filing system was Tuesday. She was not.

---

Paz had said *floors don't move.* Paz had said *ceilings do.* Paz had said *when the ceiling comes down again you're going to be at eighty with nowhere to go.*

The ceiling had not come down. The ceiling was not the problem. The floor was the problem — the eighty that was no longer a dose she took but a baseline she maintained, the chemical architecture that held the quiet room in place and the quiet room was where she lived now and living in the quiet room meant the machinery that encoded experience was intermittently insufficient and the insufficiency was going to get worse.

She could reduce the dose. The option was available the way all her options were available — structurally present, logically accessible, operationally meaningless. Reduce the dose and the quiet room opens and the ceiling rises and the signal comes back at full resolution — every Anchor Zone, every name, every addition to the populated chord that the R3 parsed through her spine without asking permission. Reduce the dose and become the relay again. Become the hub. Become the instrument that processed four hundred mages' data and lost fourteen of them to burnout and relayed the eleven seconds to 2.1 billion people and bled from her right ear and narrated the absorbed identifying themselves because the narration was what the antenna did when the antenna was pointed at the signal and the signal was pointed at everything.

She did not reduce the dose.

The kit was behind the mirror. Matte black case. Scale. Vial. Straw. Eighty milligrams. The same as yesterday — or the day before, or Tuesday, the Tuesday she didn't have, the Tuesday where the log showed she'd taken eighty at 22:30 and the log was the only proof and the proof was not experience.

The cold burn. The subtraction. The quiet room assembling, the ceiling lowering, the machinery decelerating into the compressed space where she could sit and the sitting was function and the function was the trade.

She pressed her fingertips to the desk. The buzz was there — bilateral, persistent, the signal routing through her extremities. She pressed harder. The vibration was finer than before the Broadcast. More organized. The populated chord carried its post-event density through the Bay's conduit infrastructure — fuller, the names still in it, the absorbed who had spoken still present in the frequency. The chord had not gotten quieter after the Broadcast. The chord had gotten more specific. As if the act of the absorbed identifying themselves had sharpened their presence — not louder but more *articulated,* each signature carrying the phrasing it had spoken, each name etched into the hum.

She could hear it at eighty. That was the thing Paz's curve didn't account for, the thing the forum posts didn't describe because the forum posts were written by people managing suppression and Kira was managing reception. The eighty suppressed the pattern-seeking. The eighty built the quiet room. The eighty compressed the ceiling and stilled the chord shapes and held the machinery in place. But the eighty had also become a medium — the Broadcast had used the suppression as a conductor, had passed through the chemical barrier the way light passed through glass, and the passing had changed the glass. The Lumina at eighty no longer blocked the signal. It *filtered* it. The noise was gone — the pattern-seeking, the filing compulsion, the racing machinery that made every frequency a problem to be solved. What remained was the signal itself, unprocessed, uninterpreted, arriving through the R3 at a resolution the unsuppressed implant had never achieved because the unsuppressed implant came with Kira attached and Kira's machinery added noise.

The drug removed Kira. What was left heard clearly.

She sat in the chair. The quiet room held. The LED strip cycled. The ring light was off — it had been off since the Broadcast, the toggle untouched, the streaming rig dark. She had not gone live since the eleven seconds. The audience — the 2.1 billion, the institutional requests, the subpoenas, the recruitment offers, the entire atmospheric weight of being the most visible human on earth — existed on the other side of the ring light's switch. She had not flipped the switch. She did not know when she would flip the switch. All of it on the other side of a ring light she'd been using since she was thirteen.

The signal was on this side.

The names were in the chord. She could hear them — not as the catastrophic density of the eleven seconds, not as the relay's four-hundred-node cacophony, but as the post-event baseline, the new normal, the populated hum carrying its enriched information through her spine and through the R3 that had been in her since she was ten and would be in her until it wasn't. The names were quieter now. Individual. The signal's phrasing had shifted after the Broadcast — the emphasis different, the cadence slower, as if the absorbed had said what they needed to say at volume and were now continuing at a register that didn't require the entire planet's infrastructure as amplifier. They were still talking. Still identifying. Still circulating through the void's architecture and broadcasting their presence through every nitro-connected system on earth.

She was still listening.

The ringing in her right ear was constant now — not the signal, not the chord, something mechanical. The R3's upper terminus, where the implant met the base of her skull, was warm. Not hot. Warm. The residual heat of a system that had conducted more than its design specifications accounted for and was now running at a baseline that exceeded the manufacturer's parameters for sustained operation. The manufacturer was the Academy. The Academy wanted its data back. The data was in her spine.

Her hands were still on the desk. No chord shapes — the eighty held the impulse suppressed, the muscle memory locked behind the chemical barrier. The fingers that had played intervals she didn't recognize during the Broadcast were motionless. The signal had given them back during the event and taken them away after. The taking was the eighty's work and the eighty was the floor and the floor was where she lived.

She touched the back of her neck. The implant housing. The bone. The warmth.

Tuesday was gone and the signal continued through everything — through conduits and infrastructure and walls and floors and the skeleton of a woman who was twenty years old and had been carrying the frequency since ten and was now the most famous person on earth and the most hunted and the most listened-to and the least available, sitting at eighty milligrams with a day missing from her life and a ringing in her right ear and a log full of entries she didn't remember writing and a forum full of people describing what came next and a network of four hundred mages who needed the hub and the hub was in the chair and the chair was in the quiet room and the quiet room was the cost.

She did not stop listening.
