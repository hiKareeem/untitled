# Chapter 8
## Somewhere That Isn't

> *"Residential records for Sump-tier districts within the designated Anchor Zone have been archived per Municipal Code 7.14.2(b). Affected residents who completed biometric registration at a relief processing station may request credential transfer to an active residential district. Requests must be submitted in person at a Municipal Services office with valid temporary identification. Processing time: 6–8 weeks."*
>
> — Neo-Shanghai Municipal Services, Anchor Zone Administrative Notice

---

She counted before she opened her eyes.

Creds: four hundred and twelve. Room: paid through the end of the month — nine creds per day, the rate the building charged for units without credential verification. Food: six days of dry staples in the cabinet above the sink, plus whatever the market on Level 3 would sell for unregistered cred. Implant: functional. Cracked housing, degraded overlay, the frozen notification still there.

*Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Evacuation priority: None assigned.*

Six months. The notification had been there for six months. She couldn't dismiss it — dismissal required a system update, and the update required a credential handshake she could no longer perform. The Sump residential server that had authenticated her implant had been decommissioned when the district was reclassified. The credential it had issued was dead. The notification it had generated was permanent.

She had stopped seeing it the way she had stopped seeing the chemical staining on her fingertips — a feature of the hardware, part of the display, no longer information.

She opened her eyes.

The ceiling was low. Not Sump-low — not the compressed headroom of Block 14's residential stacks, designed for density rather than comfort. But low enough to notice. This building was a converted warehouse on Mid-Level 4 — repurposed decades ago, the ceilings retained from its industrial life, the credential-inspection protocols never properly installed because the building was never properly residential. The landlord collected rent in unregistered cred. The system's oversight was her shelter.

The room was small. Four meters by three. A sink in one corner. A cooking surface beside it. A window that looked at the ventilation shaft between this building and the next. The window let in light — not sky-light, not the direct sun that the Spires hoarded and the observation decks charged a day's wages to stand under. Reflected light, bounced down from the upper Mid-Levels. Grey. Sufficient.

She stood. The floor was concrete, unsealed. Cold through her feet.

The hum was here.

Quieter than the Sump. The Mid-Levels' conduit infrastructure ran at lower density — fewer trunk-lines, more distribution branches, the grid thinning as it climbed toward the Spires' clean delivery points. In the Sump, the hum had been pressure through floors, through furniture, through the bones of her hands when she pressed them against the sorting table at the textile plant. Here it was a suggestion. A low vibration in the walls that registered through the soles of her feet when she stood still.

Not the E-flat. Not the specific harmonic of Block 14's decommissioned junction, the note she'd fallen asleep to every night for twenty-four years. That note was gone — or buried so deep beneath the new frequencies that it was the same as gone. The hum had changed after BLACKWEIR. Everyone's had. But hers had changed in a specific way: the note that had meant *home* was now part of a composite that meant something she did not have language for.

She did not think about this. She brushed her teeth. She boiled water. She made rice.

---

The implant itched.

Behind her left ear, the salvage-grade housing pressed against skin that had been irritated since the day she'd bought the unit at sixteen — three months of creds, the cheapest external-mount on the Hum Market, an older model with visible seams and a Frame overlay that rendered at a resolution Mid-Level residents would call unusable. The itch had been worse in the Sump, where the ambient RCI made the housing burn on bad days. Here, in the lower Mid-Levels, the RCI was lower. The itch should have been less.

It wasn't. The hairline crack from BLACKWEIR — from the eleven-second pulse that had passed through masonry and concrete and skull and split the seam she'd repaired twice with salvaged adhesive — let in moisture. Micro-fluctuations the sealed housing would have filtered registered as heat behind her ear, persistent, flaring when she was tired.

She could not repair it. Registered tech. Credentials. She could not replace it. A salvage-grade unit cost more than she'd saved in six months.

The overlay still worked. Barely. Frame rendering at minimum resolution — transit routes dim and partial, Feed headlines scrolling with character artifacts, cred balance readable if she tilted her head to compensate for the dead zone in the upper-right quadrant. She had learned to read around the damage. The way she'd learned to read around everything — the missing credential, the deleted residential record, the system's architectural assumption that she did not exist.

She ate standing at the sink. Rice, plain. Not because there was no table — there was a table, folding, propped against the wall. She did not use it. Standing was faster, and faster meant she was out of the room before the walls started to feel like the annex.

She did not think about the annex. She washed the bowl.

One bowl. One pair of chopsticks. One mug. One change of clothes. One thin mattress. One temporary ID in her pocket — *Lin, Nuwa. Credential tier: Basic. Re-housing eligibility: Confirmed* — that she had carried for six months and presented to no one. Everything she owned could be packed in four minutes. She had timed this. The timing was not paranoia. It was the arithmetic of a woman who had learned that the system could reclassify you out of existence, and that when it did, what you could carry was what you kept.

---

The building had fourteen units. She did not know all the tenants.

She knew the ones who used the shared water point on the third floor: the woman in Unit 6 who worked nights somewhere she didn't discuss, the old man in Unit 11 who played radio through a speaker with blown-out bass, the couple in Unit 2 who argued in a language she couldn't place and then cooked something that smelled like garlic and fermented bean paste and made the whole corridor feel like someone's home. She knew them the way she'd known her neighbors in the Sump — by schedule, by habit, by the sounds they made through walls. Not by name. Names required exchange, and exchange required the kind of mutual disclosure that credentials made easy and their absence made careful.

She passed Unit 9 on her way to the stairwell. The door was open. A woman sat on the floor inside, sorting vegetables from a plastic bag. Mid-thirties. Dark hair pulled back. Hands quick and efficient — the sorting speed of someone who'd worked produce stalls or kitchens. She'd moved in three weeks ago. Nuwa had registered her presence the way she registered everything: noted, filed, not acted on.

The woman looked up.

"Morning."

Nuwa nodded. Kept walking. The stairwell was at the end of the corridor — concrete, unlit above the second floor, the kind of infrastructure that existed because building codes had required an emergency exit and no one had updated the requirement to include functional lighting.

"Hey."

She stopped. Not because the voice demanded it. Because the tone was casual — not suspicious, not official, not the weighted register of someone about to ask for credentials. Just a person in a hallway.

"You're on five, right? I'm Fen."

"Nuwa."

"Where are you from?"

The question. She had been asked it four times since moving in. The old man in Unit 11. A woman at the market on Level 3. A relief volunteer at a distribution point she'd visited once and not returned to. And now Fen, on the floor of Unit 9, surrounded by bok choy and spring onions, asking the question the way people in the margins asked each other — not because the answer mattered but because the asking was a door, and the door was how people in buildings like this found out whether you were someone they could share a water point with.

She had language for the geography. The Sump. Block 14. Residential stack six. She could say the words — the district designations, the sector numbers, the infrastructure terminology that mapped her childhood onto the city's administrative grid. The words were precise. They located a point on the vertical axis of Neo-Shanghai as exactly as a transit coordinate.

They did not locate what happened there.

"Somewhere that isn't there anymore," she said.

Fen looked at her. The sorting hands paused. Then Fen nodded — the nod of someone who recognized the shape of the answer without needing its content. The margins were full of people whose *where* had become a *when*. The Sump wasn't the only district the system had consumed. It was the largest. But smaller reclassifications, relocations, infrastructure rezoning — the city ate neighborhoods the way it ate everything, administratively, one form at a time.

"I've got extra spring onions," Fen said. "If you want some."

"How much?"

"I said extra. Not for sale."

Nuwa's hands tightened at her sides. The reflex — the Sump reflex, the arithmetic that converted every offer into a transaction because transactions had terms and terms could be calculated and calculation was the only safe way to receive anything. She could feel the refusal forming: *no thank you* or *I'll pay you back* or *what do you need* — the phrases that converted kindness into economy, that kept the ledger balanced, that prevented the one thing she could not afford, which was debt.

"Thanks," she said.

She took the spring onions. Three stalks, rubber-banded. She carried them up the stairwell to her room and put them on the counter beside the rice cooker and looked at them for longer than the act of putting vegetables on a counter warranted.

Fen had not asked for anything. Had not implied a future claim. Had not registered the exchange on any ledger Nuwa could identify.

The spring onions sat on the counter. Green against grey concrete. The brightest thing in the room.

---

The Feed was on the public display at the Level 3 transit junction.

Nuwa passed it every day. Not because she needed the transit junction — she walked everywhere, routing along the quieter corridors, the ones with fewer biometric checkpoints and lower pedestrian density. But the public display was the only Feed access that didn't require a credential handshake, and the Feed was the only source of information about the place she'd come from.

She stood at the edge of the display's projection range. Not directly in front — directly in front was for people who belonged to the city's daily rhythm of consumption and response, who had credentials, who were legible to the system that curated the content. She stood to the side, where the projection dimmed and the text became harder to read, and she tilted her head the way she tilted it to compensate for the dead zone in her overlay.

The headlines scrolled.

*NitroCore Q3 Earnings Exceed Projections*

*Church of the Blessed Nitro Announces Cascade Remembrance Schedule*

*Anchor Zone Stabilization Update: Phase 2 Remediation On Schedule*

She stopped on the third one.

The text expanded into a summary. Municipal language. The vocabulary of institutional progress applied to the place where everyone she'd known had been absorbed into the conduit infrastructure and the building walls and the walkway gratings between residential blocks.

*Anchor Zone atmospheric monitoring confirms progressive stabilization of resonance parameters. The designated zone, formerly the Neo-Shanghai Sump residential and industrial district, continues to exhibit elevated RCI readings consistent with mass absorption events, but trend analysis indicates a 3.2% month-over-month reduction in ambient coherence volatility. Municipal Services Director Zhao confirmed that the remediation timeline remains on track: "The stabilization process is proceeding as projected. We expect the zone to reach monitoring-only status within eighteen months."*

Nuwa read it twice.

*Stabilization.* The word they chose. As if the Sump were a chemical reaction that had been brought to equilibrium. As if the absorption of a district's entire population into its own infrastructure were a volatility parameter trending toward acceptable limits. As if "monitoring-only status" were a destination rather than the system's way of saying *we will stop looking at what we did in eighteen months.*

*Formerly the Neo-Shanghai Sump residential and industrial district.* Formerly. The word that converted a place into a tense. The Sump was not destroyed — not missing, not mourned. It was *former.* Reclassified from present to past by a single adverb in a municipal update that scrolled between quarterly earnings and a liturgical calendar.

She had not been in the Sump for six months. She had not been anywhere for six months.

The system had deleted her residential record when the district was reclassified. The credential server that authenticated her implant had been decommissioned. The transit permits, the biometric checkpoints, the shift-tracking protocols that had monitored her movements for eight years at the textile reclamation plant — all of it, the entire administrative apparatus that had tracked and classified and inventoried her from birth — was archived per Municipal Code 7.14.2(b). Not because the system had decided to release her. Because the system had reclassified the district that contained her, and when the district was archived, the records went with it.

She was not free. She was *unprocessed.*

The temporary ID in her pocket was the only document that connected her to the system's inventory. A laminated card issued at a relief station by a civil servant who had never been below the transit shaft. She could take it to a Municipal Services office. She could request credential transfer. She could enter the system again — different database, same architecture. New district, new residential record, new shift tracking, new transit permits, new biometric checkpoints. The system that had inventoried her for disposal was offering to inventory her again.

Six to eight weeks processing time.

She had not gone to the Municipal Services office. She did not know if this was a decision or a habit. In the Sump, the distinction between choosing and enduring had been so thin that most days she couldn't locate it. She had not chosen to survive BLACKWEIR — she had walked to the room with masonry walls because masonry walls didn't make her implant itch. She had not chosen to leave the relief shelter — she had not gotten in line because the line was for people who believed the system's next offer would be different from its last one. She had not chosen this room — she had walked until she found a building that didn't ask for what she couldn't produce.

The choices and the habits looked the same from the outside. From the inside, too.

The Feed scrolled on. The Anchor Zone update was replaced by a transit advisory, then a weather projection, then an advertisement for Oroteden's latest P-Frame line — a woman in a Spire apartment wearing an frame so thin it looked like clothing, the tagline: *Move without compromise.* The advertisement occupied the same display where the stabilization update had been. The same projection surface. The Sump's administrative eulogy and a luxury frame separated by three seconds of scroll time.

She turned from the display.

The transit junction moved around her. Mid-Level residents in clean clothing, walking with the unhurried pace of people whose transit was unrationed. A maintenance crew in marked coveralls, heading downward toward the conduit access points. A child holding a woman's hand, looking up at the vaulted ceiling of the junction as if the height were a novelty.

Nuwa walked. She routed along the masonry wall of the junction's east corridor — old construction, load-bearing, the kind of wall that didn't carry conduit paths. The routing was automatic. She had been doing it her whole life. In the Sump, it had been a habit born of the implant itch — metal-framed corridors made the housing burn, masonry corridors didn't, and the body learned the difference before the mind catalogued it. Here, where the itch came from the crack rather than the ambience, the routing continued. Her body remembered what her mind had not yet named: that walls mattered. That the composition of the thing between you and the infrastructure was the difference between being absorbed and being still here.

She walked home.

---

The spring onions were on the counter. Green against grey. Fen's gift, unreciprocated, unledgered.

She cooked. Rice again, but this time with the spring onions sliced thin, fried in a drop of oil until the edges crisped. The smell filled the small room — sharp, green, alive. The smell of a choice. She had cooked in the Sump because cooking was the one domain where she controlled what entered her body. She cooked here for the same reason. The reason had not changed. The kitchen had.

She ate at the table. She did not know why she chose the table tonight. The folding surface was unsteady on the concrete floor, one leg shorter than the others, requiring a piece of cardboard wedged beneath it. She had propped the table against the wall six months ago and not used it because the table was for company. For Fuxi. For the version of domestic life that included another person at the other edge of a shared surface, eating something she had made.

She sat alone at the table and ate rice with spring onions and the room did not feel like the annex.

Afterward, she sat on the mattress. The overlay flickered in her peripheral vision — the frozen notification, the degraded Feed, the dim transit map of a city she navigated without the system's guidance because the system's guidance required a credential she no longer carried. She did not turn it off. Turning it off required reaching behind her ear and holding the housing's power contact for three seconds, and the housing was hot, and touching it sent a spike of itch through her jaw.

She counted.

Not creds. Not food. Not the days until rent was due.

The buildings. Block by block. The ones she remembered. Block 7, where the produce market had run on rest days and the woman with the fruit cart sang folk songs her grandmother had taught her. Block 9, where the apartments had emptied two months before BLACKWEIR and the Feed had said "relocated" and no one had believed it and no one had said so. Block 11, connected to Block 14 by the walkway where the grating had gone violet and the laundry was still hanging. Block 14. Sixth floor. The apartment where her father had come home smelling of coolant and her mother had coughed through the last year and Nuwa had stood at the kitchen counter and cut spring onions with a utility blade and pretended the arithmetic would eventually reach a number that meant something other than *stay.*

She counted without numbers. Without arithmetic. Counting the way a hand counts beads — not for the total but for the texture, each one held and released and held again.

The Feed said *stabilization.*

The system said *formerly.*

Nuwa sat in a room that cost nine creds a day, in a building that didn't verify credentials, in a city that had consumed its lowest district and called the consumption *remediation* — and she held what the Feed's words were not, which was the sound of the hum changing pitch the night everyone died, and the hand against the window that sank into the polymer, and the wet cloth on her face, and the eleven seconds, and the quiet after.

She did not have a word for what the Sump was now.

She knew the Feed's words were wrong.

That was the first thing she had known in six months that felt like ground beneath her feet.
