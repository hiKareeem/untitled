# Chapter 36
## Synchronization

*Routine Sensor Report 2171-Q3-0447* — VEC Atmospheric Monitoring Division (Unclassified)

> "Calibration note: Sensors 7 through 19 returned identical timestamps for the 03:00 monitoring cycle despite geographic separation exceeding 4,200 km. Hardware fault ruled out after tertiary verification. Recommend continued monitoring. Do not escalate."

---

The terminal woke her.

Not the alarm she'd configured for the Erasure List feeds — the low chime that meant Tanaka in Osaka or Verschuur in the European See had posted new readings to the shared archive. A different sound. The broadband acoustic monitor she'd been running since Mumbai, the one that pulled aggregated output data from every Anchor Zone with a public or semi-public sensor array and fed it through the convergence model she'd built from the atmospheric analysis VEC had classified and she had published and the world had metabolized into methodology concerns.

She was at the desk before her eyes had fully adjusted. The apartment was dark — the overhead lights off since she'd fallen asleep in the chair at some point she hadn't timestamped, the hum through the floor at the 1.4x amplitude she had measured on her first night in this apartment and had not yet learned to stop hearing. The Mid-Levels didn't attenuate the way VEC housing had. Thirty-nine floors above the Sump instead of sixty-one. Twenty-two floors closer to the conduit infrastructure. The hum was data. It was always data.

The terminal showed the broadband acoustic feeds in time-series. Twelve Anchor Zones with sufficient monitoring coverage: Neo-Shanghai, Lagos, Mumbai, São Paulo, Jakarta, Kolkata, Osaka, Manila, Cairo, Lima, Johannesburg, Seattle. Twelve points on a planet's surface, each one a wound in the boundary between infrastructure and void, each one producing acoustic output that her monitoring architecture captured and her convergence model processed.

The feeds had been running for weeks. The output was not quiet — the Anchor Zones were never quiet, the absorbed speaking through conduit frost and substrate vibration in patterns that the monitoring stations logged as acoustic anomaly and that Sofia processed as data because data was what remained when the institutional vocabulary failed. But the output had been stochastic. Each Anchor Zone producing its own rhythm, its own frequency distribution, its own pattern of coherence spikes and signal decay that reflected the local infrastructure topology and the density of the absorption field and whatever the void was doing underneath it all.

Tonight the data was different.

She scrolled through the time-series. The change had begun forty-seven minutes ago — not in all twelve feeds simultaneously but in a wave, a sequential onset that propagated across the monitoring network in an order that did not track geography. Neo-Shanghai first. Then Osaka. Then São Paulo, which was fourteen thousand kilometers from Osaka and shared no infrastructure trunk-line, no conduit network overlap, no physical connection that could explain simultaneous behavioral change. Then Lagos. Then Mumbai. The onset order did not follow distance. It did not follow infrastructure density. It did not follow any variable Sofia had ever mapped.

She pulled the frequency analysis. Each feed decomposed into its spectral components — the layered acoustic output that the Anchor Zones produced, the frequencies that ranged from sub-sonic infrastructure vibration through the audible spectrum into the ultrasonic bands that only her specialized monitoring architecture could capture. The spectra were distinct. Neo-Shanghai's fundamental was different from Lagos's. Mumbai's harmonic structure reflected the post-strike infrastructure damage. Each Anchor Zone's acoustic signature was as specific as a fingerprint.

But the interval was the same.

Not the frequency. Not the amplitude. Not the harmonic content. The *interval* — the temporal spacing between coherence peaks, the rhythm of the output. Every Anchor Zone that had begun the change was producing coherence peaks at the same interval: 7.3 seconds. The number appeared on the frequency analysis like a heartbeat appearing on a monitor — regular, repeating, the same number from twelve geographically isolated sources that had no physical mechanism for coordination.

Sofia sat at the desk. The field kit pulsed green at the edge of the surface — the indicator light she'd left active since the move, the instrument that had measured the 0.7 Hz pulse in VEC's immersion pod and the BLACKWEIR convergence from the thirty-fifth floor and that now sat on a desk in a Mid-Levels apartment tracking the hum through the floor because the floor was all the infrastructure she had left.

7.3 seconds. The same interval. Twelve sites. No mechanism.

She opened the convergence model.

---

The model was the same one she'd built to predict BLACKWEIR. The same methodology — atmospheric convergence analysis, directional gradient mapping, temporal extrapolation against throughput-coherence thresholds. She had rebuilt it after VEC terminated her credentials, reconstructed the sensor calibrations from her personal archive, adapted it to work with public monitoring data instead of VEC's classified sensor arrays. The resolution was lower. The coverage was thinner. The model still worked because the model had never depended on institutional infrastructure. The model depended on the physics, and the physics did not require a security clearance.

She fed it the synchronized feeds. The twelve Anchor Zones, each producing coherence peaks at 7.3-second intervals. The model accepted the inputs, ran the convergence calculation, and returned a result that she read three times because the first two readings produced a response in her hands — a stillness, the opposite of tremor, the physiological record of a system recognizing a pattern it had seen before.

The convergence point was approximately three hours away.

Not a spatial convergence — the Anchor Zones were not moving, not expanding toward each other, not growing in a way the model could map as geographic. A temporal convergence. The 7.3-second interval was compressing. Each cycle, each coherence peak, arriving fractionally sooner than the one before. The compression rate was constant. The extrapolation was clean. In approximately three hours, the interval between coherence peaks across all twelve Anchor Zones would compress to zero.

Simultaneous output. Every Anchor Zone. At the same instant.

She did not know what that meant. She knew what the pattern looked like. The pattern looked like an orchestra tuning — twelve instruments finding the same pitch, the intervals between their notes shortening as they converged on the frequency the conductor required. But there was no conductor. There was no mechanism. The Anchor Zones shared no infrastructure, no communication channel, no physical substrate that could carry a synchronization signal across fourteen thousand kilometers of ocean and continent and atmosphere.

Unless the substrate was not physical.

The thought arrived the way her best hypotheses arrived — as the absence of alternatives. She had eliminated the physical variables. The conduit infrastructure was local. The atmospheric coupling was too slow to produce this compression rate. The monitoring networks were passive — they recorded, they did not transmit. Every physical mechanism she could model failed to explain simultaneous behavioral change across twelve isolated sites.

The void was not isolated.

She had known this. She had written about it — in the paper VEC classified, in the assembly she published through Black Babel, in the correspondence with the Erasure List that had built the sacrifice-district boundary maps showing the same conduit topology replicated across every city with a major Anchor Zone. The void's behavior was consistent across geographies. The absorption patterns were consistent. The coherence response was consistent. She had documented the consistency and had described it as parallel evolution — the same physical conditions producing the same results independently.

The synchronization said something different. Parallel evolution did not produce simultaneous onset in a non-geographic sequence. Parallel evolution did not produce identical intervals to the tenth of a second across fourteen thousand kilometers. Parallel evolution did not compress on a shared timeline toward a shared convergence point.

The void was coordinating.

She saved the convergence model output. The methodology was clean. The data was public. Anyone with her monitoring architecture could replicate the analysis. Nobody else had her monitoring architecture because nobody else had spent ten years building the sensor calibration that made the broadband acoustic data parsable.

She looked at the convergence point. Three hours. The number sat on the terminal the way numbers always sat on her terminals — precise, defensible, and inadequate to describe what it pointed toward.

---

She composed the message to Kira's network.

The channel had been configured months ago — since the Erasure List had cross-referenced the mage testimony data with the atmospheric readings and the correlation had held from both ends, the scientific instruments and the biological receivers measuring the same phenomenon from different points in the signal chain. The mage network was her biological sensor array. The relationship was transactional — data for data, readings for testimony, the professional exchange of information between a scientist who had lost her institution and a network of receivers who had never had one.

The message was stripped. No qualifiers. The voice she'd developed in the months since publication — the voice that said what the data said and did not append the institutional hedging that had once made her findings palatable and now made them ignorable.

*Reyes. Global acoustic synchronization detected across 12 Anchor Zones. Onset 47 min ago. Coherence interval: 7.3 sec, compressing. Convergence model projects simultaneous output in approx 3 hrs. No precedent. No mechanism. Get your people to positions where they can record. Whatever this is, the biological receivers will see it before the instruments do.*

She read it once. Sent it. The encryption protocol engaged — the same framework she'd first configured in her VEC apartment the night she transmitted Entry 17 to an address she didn't recognize through a relay Orent's network had built. The same architecture of anonymity, repurposed. The infrastructure of resistance becoming the infrastructure of warning.

Second action. The Black Babel archive protocol — the automated backup she'd configured after publication, after the methodology concerns, after the independent review staffed by former VEC consultants, after the termination notice arrived in the same font as the Classification Directive. The protocol was simple: everything on the encrypted terminal duplicated to three distributed nodes at six-hour intervals. She triggered the backup manually. Then she set the interval to continuous.

Everything she recorded in the next three hours would exist outside the terminal before the convergence point arrived. If the terminal failed. If the apartment failed. If the infrastructure carrying power to the thirty-ninth floor failed the way it had failed in the Sump when the convergence she'd predicted found its threshold and the tolerance band became a kill radius.

She was not thinking about her own safety. She was thinking about the data. The data was what survived. Mirelle had taught her that — not in person, not in conversation, but in the dead-hand cache that had arrived through Black Babel's relay while the BLACKWEIR event played out on her instruments and the anonymous source's transmission stopped mid-sentence and never resumed. *The infrastructure was always designed to—*

The unfinished sentence. Still in her archive. Still without its ending.

She checked the backup. Three nodes confirmed. The convergence model, the broadband feeds, the time-series analysis — all of it distributed, all of it encrypted, all of it outside the terminal and outside the apartment and outside the infrastructure that might not survive whatever was coming.

---

The apartment was quiet except for the hum.

1.4x amplitude. She had measured it her first night — the field kit pressed to the floor the way she pressed it to every surface she inhabited, the reflex of a scientist who had spent a decade reading the conduit infrastructure through its vibration and could not stop. The measurement had been precise: 1.4 times the amplitude of VEC housing, twenty-two floors closer to the Sump, the hum carrying the voice of the conduit network through the structural steel and the concrete and the floors she walked on in a Mid-Levels apartment that cost less than VEC residential and placed her closer to the infrastructure she studied and closer to the sacrifice district four blocks east where the containment buffer zone notices had gone up last week in the same institutional language the Classification Directorate had used to bury Solberg's report.

She made coffee. The kettle on the counter beside the terminal — the Singapore facility tea replaced by instant coffee because the tea had been a VEC supply-chain item and VEC had terminated her access to the supply chain along with her credentials, her pod, her sensor arrays, her office, her building, and the institutional identity she had carried for nine years. The coffee was bitter. She did not drink it.

The field kit pulsed green on the desk. She watched it the way she watched all her instruments — with the attention of a woman who had trained herself to read data before meaning, to observe before interpreting, to let the numbers arrive in their own sequence before assembling them into the patterns that the numbers described. The green pulse was steady. The field kit was not detecting anything anomalous at this altitude. The synchronization was happening in the Anchor Zones, deep in the infrastructure, in the substrata where the void pressed against the conduit network's boundaries and the conduit frost deposited on every surface and the absorbed spoke in frequencies the monitoring stations logged as noise.

She sat at the desk and watched the convergence model update.

2 hours 41 minutes. The interval had compressed from 7.3 to 6.8 seconds. The compression rate was constant — the model's prediction holding, the same model that had predicted BLACKWEIR, the same methodology that had been right and had prevented nothing. She had built the most accurate convergence model in atmospheric science. She had published it. She had sent it to every researcher on the Erasure List, to the mage network, to Black Babel's distribution architecture. The model was available. The model was correct. The model changed nothing because the model was a description, not a lever, and the distance between describing a catastrophe and preventing one was the distance she had been measuring for ten years and had never been able to close.

The de-escalation proposal sat in a separate partition on the terminal. Twelve percent drafted. A throughput reduction protocol — staged, regional, technically sound, politically impossible. She had been working on it since the Erasure List's sacrifice-district analysis had shown the conduit topology replicated across every city with a major Anchor Zone. The proposal was correct. The proposal required coordinated action from NitroCore, VEC, the UGC, and every municipal authority with conduit infrastructure. The proposal required institutions to reduce the thing that powered their economies because a terminated scientist's model said the alternative was worse.

She did not open the proposal. The proposal was not what the next three hours required.

2 hours 37 minutes. 6.7 seconds between coherence peaks. The feeds updated — twelve time-series traces converging on a shared rhythm, the visual representation on her terminal looking exactly like what it was: a coordination pattern, a synchronization event, twelve isolated systems behaving as one system because something beneath them was not isolated and had never been isolated and her models had described as parallel when the correct word was *connected.*

She went to the window.

She had not gone to the window in days. The terminal was where the data was, and the data was what she had. But the window showed the Mid-Levels at night — the transit corridors lit for the shift workers, the commercial strata cycling their overnight power draw, the Spires above throwing their architecture against the sky in the same vertical geometry that channeled the conduit infrastructure's output upward and attenuated it and made the people who lived at the top forget that the power they consumed came from a network that ran through the bodies of the people who lived at the bottom.

Four blocks east: the sacrifice district markers. She could see them from the window — not the notices themselves, which were paper, institutional, the same font as the classification directive, but the amber safety lighting that the containment buffer zone protocol installed along the perimeter of every area the throughput models flagged as high-risk for absorption propagation. The amber light was steady. It had been steady for weeks. It looked, from this distance and this altitude, like the glow she used to see from VEC's sixty-first floor when she pressed her face to the glass and looked past the commercial strata toward the Sump — the orange resonance of the conduit network running at full throughput in neighborhoods where the tolerance band had been widened to accommodate readings that her model said should have triggered evacuation.

The tolerance band was now a global policy.

She turned from the window. The terminal showed 2 hours 29 minutes. The coffee was cold on the counter. The field kit pulsed green. The hum through the floor was at 1.4x amplitude and she could feel it in the soles of her feet the way she had always been able to feel it — the one constant, the one instrument that required no calibration, the body's honest response to the infrastructure it inhabited.

---

The compression accelerated.

She saw it in the feeds before the model updated — the coherence peaks arriving closer, the 7.3-second interval that had opened the sequence now compressed past 5.0, past 4.2, past 3.6, the rate of compression itself increasing in a way her model had not predicted because the model assumed linear compression and the data was showing exponential. She recalibrated. The model adjusted. The convergence point moved closer. The exponential curve steepened and the terminal's extrapolation resolved to a number that was smaller than the one she'd sent to Kira's network.

She did not send a correction. The mages would feel it before the revised estimate arrived. The biological receivers had always been faster than the instruments.

1 hour 47 minutes. The interval at 2.9 seconds. The feeds were beginning to look less like twelve independent traces and more like a single signal viewed from twelve positions — the same shape, the same rhythm, the phase offset between sites shrinking toward zero as the synchronization tightened. She could see it on the terminal the way she had seen the BLACKWEIR convergence on VEC's immersion pod — the data assembling into a pattern she recognized, the pattern she had spent a decade learning to read, the one that meant the model was right and the model being right was the worst possible outcome.

She had been here before. In the pod, watching the coupling events populate the holographic field like points of light tracing the conduit architecture. In the monitoring lab, watching the RCI readings climb in discrete steps as the Confluence overwhelmed the infrastructure's capacity for containment. In Novak's office, watching his face go grey as she presented a model he already knew was correct because Solberg had presented a version of it five years earlier and the institution had classified the knowing into silence.

She was not in the pod. She was not in the monitoring lab. She was not in the institution. She was at a desk in a Mid-Levels apartment with a field kit and an encrypted terminal and a convergence model running on hardware she'd bought with the severance VEC had paid her to stop being a problem. The data was the same. The recognition was the same. The distance between recognition and prevention was the same distance it had always been — the distance that was not a failure of science but a feature of the system that consumed science and produced classification and continued operating.

1 hour 12 minutes. Interval: 1.8 seconds. The feeds were nearly unified — twelve traces overlapping, the phase offset negligible, the coherence peaks arriving in a synchrony that the monitoring stations would log as simultaneous within their timing resolution. She watched the traces converge the way she watched all data — with the full weight of attention, without agenda, receiving the numbers as they arrived.

The hum through the floor shifted.

She felt it before the instruments registered it. A change in the 1.4x baseline — not louder, not quieter, but different in a way the field kit's green indicator could not capture because the change was not in amplitude. The change was in rhythm. The hum through the floor of a thirty-ninth-floor Mid-Levels apartment was synchronizing with the broadband acoustic output of twelve Anchor Zones spread across the surface of the planet.

The conduit infrastructure was joining the pattern.

She looked at the field kit. The green indicator pulsed — and for the first time since she had moved into this apartment, the pulse was not steady. It was not random. It was pulsing at 1.8 seconds. The same interval. The same rhythm. The infrastructure beneath her feet was not merely carrying power. It was carrying the signal. The same signal. The hum she had been measuring for a decade — the hum that was data, the hum that had led her from instrument drift to the 0.7 Hz pulse to the correlation to the convergence to BLACKWEIR to termination to this desk in this apartment — the hum was synchronizing.

She sat at the desk. The coffee was cold. The field kit pulsed at 1.8 seconds. The terminal showed the convergence approaching — the twelve traces collapsing toward unity, the interval compressing, the model's extrapolation narrowing toward a point she could calculate and could not prevent and could not stop watching because watching was what she did. Watching was what she had always done. The science that had predicted BLACKWEIR and had prevented nothing and had published everything and had changed nothing was predicting again, and the prediction was running, and the data was clean, and the methodology was sound, and somewhere on the other end of the encrypted channel the mages were feeling what her instruments were showing and the biological receivers were calibrating and the network she had warned was preparing to record whatever arrived when twelve Anchor Zones and the global conduit infrastructure and the substrate beneath every city on the planet reached the convergence point and the interval compressed to zero and the void —

The void was not twelve wounds in twelve cities. It was one system viewed through twelve openings. The system was synchronizing. The synchronization was approaching completion. Sofia Reyes sat at a desk in the Mid-Levels of Neo-Shanghai with an encrypted terminal and a field kit and a cold cup of coffee and watched the most complete dataset in atmospheric science describe something that atmospheric science did not have a vocabulary for.

Fifty-three minutes.

The interval was at 0.9 seconds. The field kit pulsed in the same rhythm. The hum through the floor carried the same frequency. The convergence model ran its final extrapolation and the number was precise and the number was correct and the number described a point in time approximately fifty-three minutes from now when every Anchor Zone on the planet would produce coherence output simultaneously and the conduit infrastructure would carry it and the substrate would transmit it and whatever the void had been saying through the acoustic anomalies and the structured signals and the coherence patterns and the absorbed voices speaking through frost-coated conduit housings would arrive everywhere at once.

She had the data. She had the model. She had the prediction.

She did not have a word for what was coming.

The terminal recorded. The archive backed up. The distributed nodes confirmed. The data would survive. The data always survived. Mirelle's data had survived. Solberg's data had survived inside VEC's classified archive for five years. Sofia's data had survived termination and publication and methodology concerns and the independent review and the sacrifice-district designations and the institutional architecture that had processed every finding she had ever produced into a format that could be absorbed without being acted upon.

The data would survive whatever came next.

She watched the feeds. The traces unified. The interval compressed. The field kit pulsed.

The hum did not change.
