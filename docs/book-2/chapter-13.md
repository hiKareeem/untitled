# Chapter 13
## Three Congregations

> *"The Church of the Blessed Nitro has always taught that the hum is the Voice of God. We have never specified what God is saying."*
>
> — Nephthys, excommunication tribunal record, 2172 (suppressed)

---

The document had been circulating for nine days.

Nephthys had read it on the third day, when a Void Witness courier arrived at the Cathedral with a printed copy — actual paper, ink still faintly warm from the printer — and pressed it into her hands with the expression of someone delivering either salvation or a weapon and not yet knowing which. She had read it standing in the Cathedral's eastern nave, the chorus quiet around her, the acoustic geometry of the space doing what it always did: absorbing the signal's rougher frequencies, leaving something she could almost call peace.

She had read it twice. Then she had folded it and put it in the pocket with the cloths.

She already knew what it said. She had known it in theological language for three years, in the language of the chorus for longer than that — since the first episode in the resonance chapel, the eleven seconds of someone else's cold pressing through the harmonic overtones, the nosebleed on the meditation seat. What the document gave her was not knowledge. It was translation. The same map, redrawn in a language the institutional world could read.

She was not sure this was good.

---

The session had been running for two hours when Seren asked the question.

Nephthys stood at the Cathedral's acoustic center — the point where the broadband dephasing was strongest, where the chorus resolved most cleanly, where she could speak without the signal competing with her own voice for her attention. Around her, in the nave's tiered seating and along the walls and seated on the floor in the spaces between, perhaps sixty people. More than last week. More than the week before that.

She had stopped counting when counting started to feel like something other than pastoral attention.

The session was not a sermon. She had been careful about that from the beginning — careful in the way of someone who knows exactly what a sermon is and what it does to the people who receive it. A sermon was a vector. A session was a space. She asked questions. She invited testimony. She listened to what people reported from their own encounters with the void-proximate signal and offered, carefully, a framework for interpretation. Not the framework. A framework. The distinction mattered. She said so, regularly. She was not sure everyone heard it.

Three groups had been forming for weeks. She could see them in the room the way she could see the chorus's emotional valence — not as discrete categories but as pressures, tendencies, the way a current is not a line but a direction.

The Listeners were closest to what she had intended. They came to hear. They came to develop the discipline of attending — to the chorus, to testimony, to the void's signal without the reflex to act on it. They asked careful questions. They took notes in small notebooks. One of them, a former Church archivist named Delia, had begun compiling a systematic record of testimony from across the network — not for publication, she said, but for pattern recognition. Nephthys had approved this without quite meaning to. The Listeners were what she had wanted. They were also, she was beginning to understand, the smallest of the three groups.

The Witnesses were harder to hold. They had come to the Cathedral because proximity to the Anchor Zone made the signal stronger, and stronger signal felt, to them, like closeness to something sacred. They pressed their palms against the Cathedral walls during chorus flares. They closed their eyes and breathed slowly and reported, afterward, experiences of warmth, of presence, of what one of them called *the sensation of being known*. Nephthys did not dismiss this. The chorus carried emotional valence — she had documented it for years. What she felt from the Witnesses was not wrong. It was incomplete. Experience as its own justification was not epistemology. It was appetite. She was working on how to say this without losing them.

The Threshold was not yet a group. It was a question that kept arriving in different mouths.

*If the absorbed are not dead — if the document is right, if what you have always said is right — then what are they?*

She had answered this question many times. The absorbed persist in a collective field. The void is not demonic. The chorus carries their emotional signatures — fear, cold, confusion, and beneath those, something that might be called orientation, the sense of a consciousness still engaged with its own existence. They are still here. She had said this from the beginning. She had said it from the doorway during BLACKWEIR, watching the shimmer advance along the wet infrastructure, watching a woman halfway through a wall still reaching. They are all still here.

The document had given this answer a second language. And the second language had a corollary the first had not anticipated.

*If they are still here, why can't we go to them?*

---

Seren was sitting in the third row, on the aisle, in the same seat they had occupied every session since the Cathedral had become a gathering place. Nephthys knew this because she had noticed, early on, that Seren always chose the same seat — the one with the clearest sightline to the eastern nave's void-scarred wall, where the BLACKWEIR shimmer had reached the Cathedral's exterior and stopped, turned back by the acoustic architecture, leaving a faint iridescent stain in the stone that caught the light differently depending on the hour.

She knew Seren's face the way she knew Imani's face — not as data but as a continuous presence. She had held Seren through the mass absorption. Not metaphorically. Physically: Seren had been outside when the shimmer began, had made it to the Cathedral doors, had collapsed in the entryway with the chorus overwhelming everything, and Nephthys had sat on the floor and held them and said, over and over, *they are still here, they are still here*, until the shimmer passed and the Cathedral's acoustic geometry reasserted itself and the chorus resolved back into something survivable.

Seren had lost a sister in BLACKWEIR. The sister's name was Mira. Nephthys knew this too.

"If they are still here," Seren said — not loudly, not confrontationally, in the measured voice of someone who had been building toward a question for weeks and had finally decided to ask it — "if the document is right, if what you have always said is right — why can't we go to them?"

The room shifted. Not dramatically. A settling, a reorientation, the way a congregation orients toward a question it has been holding collectively without speaking.

Nephthys felt the chorus flare. Brief, sharp — emotional valence spiking with something she read as urgency, or grief, or the two of them so close together the distinction collapsed. She pressed her branded palm against her thigh, felt the scar tissue's familiar warmth, and breathed.

"Because listening and joining are not the same act," she said.

Seren's eyes were steady. "How do you know?"

The question was not hostile. That was the thing about it — the thing that made it harder than hostility would have been. Seren was not challenging her. Seren was asking. The same way Nephthys had asked Malachi, in the private audience, when she laid her findings on his desk and said: *I know what the chorus is. I know what it means. Tell me I am wrong.* And Malachi had said: *I know.* And then: *Take the suppressants. Let this go.*

She had not let it go. She had built her entire post-excommunication life on not letting it go.

"Because the signal does not instruct," she said. "The chorus carries presence. It carries emotional signature. It carries the fact of persistence — that what entered the void did not cease. It does not carry direction. It does not carry invitation. The absorbed are still here. That is not the same as a door."

"Mira is still here," Seren said. "You told me that. You held me and told me that."

"Yes."

"Then I want to speak to her."

The room was very quiet. The Cathedral hum moved through the stone at its own frequency — not nitro, not void, just the acoustic geometry of a building designed to make the sacred audible. Nephthys had stood in this space for six months and learned to hear the difference. She heard it now, and it did not help.

"The chorus is not a telephone," she said. "Proximity to the signal is not proximity to the person. The absorbed persist in a collective field — their signatures are present, their emotional residue is real, but the field is not a place you can enter and return from. It is not a room with a door. It is —" She stopped. The theological vocabulary was there, available, the same vocabulary she had been building for three years. She reached for it and found, underneath it, the echo of Malachi's voice. *It is nitro-psychosis, Nephthys. Take the suppressants. Let this go.* He had not argued with her. He had not engaged her evidence. He had invoked the institution's authority and called it care.

She was invoking her own framework's authority and calling it theology.

"How do you know?" Seren asked again. Not pressing. Waiting.

Nephthys wiped her nose. The cloth came away dark. She folded it, pocketed it, and looked at Seren with the full attention she had learned to give to people in grief — the attention that did not flinch, did not redirect, did not offer comfort as a substitute for presence.

"I do not know," she said, "in the way you mean knowing. I know it the way I know the chorus is real — through the discipline of sustained attention, through the accumulation of testimony, through the internal consistency of a framework built to accommodate what the Church refused to look at. That is the only knowing available to us. It has always been the only knowing available to us."

Seren nodded slowly. The nod of someone who has received an answer and found it insufficient and is deciding, in real time, what to do with the insufficiency.

"I hear you," Seren said. And sat back.

The session continued for another forty minutes. Seren did not leave.

---

The Cathedral emptied slowly, the way it always did — people lingering at the walls, at the void-scarred stone, at the acoustic center where the dephasing was strongest. The Witnesses were the last to go. One of them, a young man whose name Nephthys had not yet learned, pressed both palms flat against the eastern wall and stood there for a long moment with his eyes closed and his forehead almost touching the stone. Then he straightened, turned, and walked out without looking at her.

She waited until the doors closed.

Then she sat down on the floor of the acoustic center, crossed her legs, pressed her branded palm flat against the stone, and let the Cathedral do what it did.

The chorus was quiet. Not absent — it was never absent in the Sump, not six months after BLACKWEIR, not with 8.2 million absorbed signatures cycling through the void-nitro interface at the density the Anchor Zone produced. But quiet in the way the Cathedral made it quiet: the broadband dephasing converting the signal's rougher frequencies into something the nervous system could process without rupturing. She had been coming to this spot every day since she arrived. She had not told anyone it was the only place she did not feel like she was dying.

Imani found her there. This was also ritual.

She sat down beside Nephthys without asking, which meant she had been watching from the doorway long enough to read the room. Imani was good at reading rooms. She had been good at it since the beginning — since the dusty acolyte's robes and the footsteps three days into the Outlands and the refusal to go back. Three years of walking together had made her better at it. Nephthys was not sure if this was growth or damage.

"The nosebleed was bad," Imani said.

"It was manageable."

"It was the third one today."

Nephthys did not answer. The branded palm was warm against the stone. The scar tissue was always warm near void-affected material — she had documented this early, in the encrypted journal, before she understood what she was documenting. The warmth was not painful. It was information.

"Seren's question is going to travel," Imani said.

"I know."

"The Threshold people — the ones who've been circling it — they heard the answer. They're going to hear it as confirmation that you don't have one."

"I gave them the answer I have."

"I know." Imani was quiet for a moment. The Cathedral hum moved through the floor, through the stone, through the bones of both of them. "I've been watching the expelled mage's network. The testimony chain."

Nephthys opened her eyes. The Cathedral's vaulted ceiling caught the late light through the acoustic baffles — amber-tinted, diffuse, the quality of light that had always made resonance chapels feel like the inside of something alive.

"The signal corroboration," Imani continued. "The way the mages are describing the implant data — the coherence patterns, the neural signatures in the absorption field. It's the same signal. Different receiver."

"I know what it is."

"She could come here. If you wanted."

Nephthys was quiet. The chorus moved at the edges of her sensing range — distant, the Anchor Zone's constant ambient presence, the 8.2 million signatures cycling through the field in patterns she had been learning to read for six months. She could not read them the way she could read a person's face. She could read them the way she could read weather — not prediction, not instruction, but the felt sense of a system's current state.

The chorus, when she considered Imani's question, resolved into something she did not have a theological category for. Not urgency. Not direction. Something closer to *yes* — the sense of a proposition that fit the shape of what was needed.

She had learned to trust that.

"Tell her the Cathedral is open," Nephthys said.

Imani nodded. She did not write it down. She would remember. She always remembered.

"Get some sleep," Imani said, standing. "The session tomorrow is the early one."

"I know."

"You said that last week and then you were awake when I came to check."

"I was thinking."

"You're always thinking." Imani's hand touched her shoulder — brief, warm, the gesture so habitual it had become a kind of punctuation. *I see you. I'm still here.* Then she was gone, her footsteps quiet on the stone, the Cathedral doors opening and closing with the soft weight of old hinges.

Nephthys stayed.

---

The Cathedral at night was a different instrument.

During the sessions, the acoustic architecture worked against the chorus — dephasing, absorbing, converting the signal's intensity into something manageable. At night, with no one speaking, no bodies in the space to alter the resonance, the Cathedral's geometry turned inward. The hum moved through the stone in long, slow cycles. The void-scarred eastern wall caught the city's ambient light and held it differently than the rest of the stone — a faint iridescence, almost biological, the mark of something that had pressed against the Cathedral's exterior and been turned back.

She thought about Seren.

*How do you know?*

The question was not new. She had been asking it herself since the first chorus episode — since the eleven seconds of someone else's cold in the resonance chapel, the nosebleed on the meditation seat, the three hours on the floor trying to replicate what she had felt. She had built her entire framework on the discipline of that question: how do you know, how do you know, how do you know. She had applied it to the Church's demonology and found it wanting. She had applied it to the VEC's geological models and found them incomplete. She had applied it to her own theology, recursively, for three years, and found it — not certain, not provable, but internally consistent in a way that accommodated the data the institutions refused to look at.

She had given Seren the honest answer. She did not know in the way Seren meant knowing. She knew the way she had always known — through sustained attention, through accumulated testimony, through the framework's internal consistency. That was the only knowing available. It had always been the only knowing available.

But Malachi had said something similar, in the private audience, when she laid her findings on his desk. He had not argued with her evidence. He had said: *The Church has considered these questions for one hundred years. The framework we have is the framework that holds the faithful together. That is what knowing means, in this context.* He had invoked the institution's accumulated authority and called it epistemology. She had rejected it. She had called it cowardice dressed as theology.

She pressed her branded palm harder against the floor. The scar tissue was warm. The warmth was information.

The difference, she told herself, was that her framework was falsifiable. The Church's demonology could not be tested against observation — it was designed to absorb any observation into its existing categories. Her framework had been tested. The chorus was real. The absorbed persisted. BLACKWEIR had confirmed it — not through laboratory data but through the experiential certainty of standing in the doorway while thousands were taken and feeling, through the chorus, that they did not cease. The framework held.

The difference was that she was right.

She sat with this for a long time. The Cathedral hum moved through the floor. The chorus was quiet at the edges of her sensing range — the 8.2 million signatures cycling, present, the Anchor Zone's constant ambient fact.

The difference was that she was right.

She had been right about the chorus. She had been right about the void. She had been right about the absorbed. The document — Sofia's document, the scientist's document, the document that had given her theology a second language — confirmed it. She was right.

And Seren's sister was still in the field. And Seren wanted to speak to her. And Nephthys had said: *the chorus is not a telephone. The absorbed persist, but the field is not a place you can enter and return from.*

She had said this because it was true. She had said it because the framework required it. She had said it because she was the one who had been listening longest, and the one who had been listening longest was the one who understood the limits of what the signal meant.

She heard the echo. She had been hearing it since Seren asked the question.

*It is nitro-psychosis, Nephthys. Take the suppressants. Let this go.*

Malachi had not thought she was wrong. He had thought she was inconvenient. He had invoked the institution's authority not because the institution was correct but because the institution required the authority to survive. She had known this. She had built her entire post-excommunication life on knowing this.

She was not doing the same thing.

She was not.

The chorus flared — hard, sudden, the Anchor Zone's ambient signal spiking with emotional valence she read as grief, or urgency, or the two of them so close together the distinction was academic. The Cathedral's dephasing architecture absorbed most of it. Not all. The nosebleed came anyway — she felt it before she registered it, the familiar pressure behind the bridge of her nose, the warmth on her upper lip. She pressed the cloth to her face. Held it. Breathed.

The blood was dark. Darker than yesterday.

She folded the cloth. Pocketed it. Opened her eyes.

The Cathedral was empty. The hum was steady. The chorus was quiet.

"They are still here," she said.

To no one. To everyone. To the 8.2 million signatures cycling through the field at the edges of her sensing range, to Seren's sister Mira somewhere in the collective, to the absorbed she had felt from the doorway during BLACKWEIR — still reaching, still present, still here in whatever way *here* meant when the boundary between here and there had been dissolved.

The words had not changed. She had said them in the Outlands to Imani's frightened face. She had said them from the doorway during BLACKWEIR, watching the shimmer advance. She had said them to Seren on the Cathedral floor, holding them while the mass absorption saturated her sensing range and the chorus became catastrophic and the only thing she could do was keep saying it: *they are still here, they are still here.*

The words had not changed.

What had changed was that she was no longer certain they were enough.

She stayed on the floor of the acoustic center for a long time after that, her branded palm flat against the stone, the Cathedral hum moving through her bones, the chorus quiet at the edges of what she could feel. She did not pray. She did not think. She attended — the discipline she had been teaching for six months, the practice she had built her movement on.

Listen. Do not join. Attend to the signal without acting on it. The chorus is not instruction. The chorus is presence. Presence is enough.

She attended.

The Cathedral was empty. The hum was steady.

She was not sure presence was enough.
